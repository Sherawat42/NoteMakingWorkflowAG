22.1 INTRODUCTION

Note: In this unit, a number of theorems are stated and proved. The proofs may
be skipped altogether, provided the essential underlying ideas become clear
from the statements of the theorems. One should go through the proofs only for
getting clarifications about the underlying essential ideas.

Modern study of economic theories entails study of behaviour of economic
agent/s who tend to optimise her desired outcome at the end of process. One of
the fundamental principles of mathematical economics, is that, economic
agent/s try to maximize an objective function subject to resource constraints.
This principle is adopted to explain economic behaviour at one instance of time
as well as the evolution of economic behaviour through time. Optimisation
from an economist’s sake can be broadly classified into static optimisation and
dynamic optimisation. In contrast to static optimisation, in dynamic
optimisation, the variables of model are time variant or in other words they
change with respect to time. Each of the various Dynamic optimisation
techniques consists of set of tools which can be useful, apart from in
Economics, in operations research, engineering and other applied field;
moreover, these tools have applications in different subfields including
economic growth macroeconomics, microeconomics, finance, and dynamic
games. Main methods for solving Dynamic optimisation problems can depend
on (i) Pontryagin’s maximum principle by Lev. S.Pontryagin and (ii) Dynamic
programming principle by Richard Bellman. Pontryagin’s method is based on
ideas from classical calculus of variations whereas Bellman’s principle is based
on recursive method which is akin to the concept of sub-game perfect
equilibrium in context of dynamic game theory. Dynamic programming is not
to be misconstrued as synonym of dynamic optimisation rather it is one of the
methods to solve optimal control problems.

Dynamic optimisation problem can be divided into two major categories:
discreate and continuous. This distinction is based upon the nature of objective
function whether that is continuous or piecewise continuous. Moreover, the
objective function can be deterministic or stochastic in nature. The study of
stochastic dynamic optimisation is beyond the scope of the syllabus. We shall
first consider the unconstrained optimisation and then delve into constrained
optimisation. But even before that, we need to have clarification between some
closely-connected pairs of concepts.

22.1.1 Some Conceptual Clarifications

The two pairs of concepts discussed are (i) static optimization vs. dynamic

22.1.1.1 Static Optimization vs. Dynamic Optimization

We briefly illustrate the essential difference in the subject-matter of the current
block, viz. dynamic optimization, vis-a-vis the subject-matter of the previous two
blocks, which we retrospectively call static optimization. For this purpose, we
consider a very simplified case of profit maximization. Consider the case of a
producer of, say fruit-icecream using three fruits, viz. mango, grapes and banana.

Let

CP (fruit)= (CP (mango), CP (grape), CP (banana)), the cost-price-vector per unit
of the fruits,

Q (fruit) = (Q(mango), Q(grape), Q(banana)), the quantity vector used per unit
of icecream,

SP(Icecream) = sales-price per unit of icecream

Total-Production (Icecream) = the total production of fruit-icecream
Thus,

Total-revenue = Total-Production (Icecream) < SP(Icecream).

Total-cost = Total-Production (Icecream) < {CP (fruit). Q(fruit)"}, where Q’ is
the transpose of matrix Q, and ‘.’ denotes product of two matrices.

Total-Profit = Total-revenue — Total-cost, is the objective function, the value of
which is sought to be optimized, subject to CP(x), Q(x) etc.

In the above, the variables CP(x), Q(x), SP(Icecream), Total-Production
(Icecream) are treated as independent variables.

However, on deeper analysis, we find that the prices of fruits depend on the
season, i. e. the time period of the year. For example, mango, in India, is the
cheapest (and, of course, of the best quality) during peak period, viz. from May
to August. And its price varies over the year according to the distance in time
from the peak period. Similarly, grape, in India, has the peak period from January
to May. Thus, CP(x) is not independent variable, but dependent on time t.

Hence, regarding price, instead of CP(x), its appropriate modelling is by CP (x,
Q). Similarly, the quantities, of various fruits used in one unit of icecream,
depends on time, e. g., during February-April, more of grape, and less of mango
may be used because of respective availability and quality during the period.
Hence, instead of Q(x), the appropriate modelling is by Q (x, 0).

Also, regarding sales, the units of icecream sold depends on the time of the year,
e. g. during the summer period of April to July, sales may be at the peak, while
during December-February, the sales may be the least. Thus, Total-Revenue, and
the objective function Total-profit also are functions of time.

Thus, in such problems, time is an important independent variable, and in many
of such cases, the only independent variable. Other variables, like CP(x), Q(x),

22.3. THE CALCULUS OF VARIATIONS

The origin of calculus of variations can be traced back to John Bernoulli who in
year 1696 posed a problem called brachistochrone problem: (If a small object
moves under the influence of gravity which path between two fixed points
enables it to make the trip in the shortest time). The problem was solved by him
and independently by his brother James in 1697. Other eminent mathematician
who contributed to this branch of calculus include Euler, Lagrange, Legendre,
Jacobi, Hamilton, Weierstrass, Hilbert.

Next, we discuss fundamental result, of Calculus of Variations, viz, Euler-
Lagrange Equation, also known as Euler-Lagrange Condition, or even E-L
condition, EL equation etc.)
