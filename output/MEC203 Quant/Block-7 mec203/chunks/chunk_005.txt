23. 1.1.1 Optimisation Problem

Here, we start our summarization of the topic by recalling that optimization
problem has three components: an objective function, decision variables, and
constraints, where

The objective function, often denoted as f or z, reflects a single quantity to be
either maximized or minimized.

The decision variables ((also called choice variables, often denoted as the
vector X) are independent variables, and reflect aspects of the problem that we
(or the decision maker) have control over.

Constraints represent any kind of limitation/bounds on the values that the
decision variables must satisfy.

The involved concepts are illustrated through the following two examples.

Example 1: A business firm may seek to maximize profit P. In the simplest
case, profit is the difference between total revenue R and total cost C.,
respectively depending on market demand for the firm’s product and production
capacity for output, say Q. Thus, both R and C are functions of Q, and hence so
is the profit P.

The optimization problem then is that of choosing the level of Q that
maximizes P

It may be noted that the goal is to find the value of Q, for which P is maximized.
The goal is not to find the maximum value of the choice/independent variable Q.

off area? For this problem,

The objective is to maximize the rectangular area enclosed by the fence.

generally related by first-order (not involving second derivatives) differential
equations or difference equations. As explained above, such variables, called
state variables, evolve over time in a way that depends on the values they have at
any given instant and on the externally imposed values of input variables. Output

variables’ values depend on the values of the state variables.
