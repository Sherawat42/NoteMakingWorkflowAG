28.5 TYPES OF SAMPLING

We proceed by partitioning sampling techniques into two groups:

Non-probability (non-random) sampling

Probability (random) sampling.

A.

Non-probability sampling is used in the absence of a sampling frame and
are characterized by the following properties:

1) Some population units have no chance (zero probability) of being
selected.

2) Units which can be selected have an unknown (non-zero) probability of

selection.

3) Sampling errors cannot be quantified.

There are three types of non-probability sampling:

i)

Convenience Sampling — samples are selected from the members of the
population which happen to be in the vicinity. Of course, such an approach is
unlikely to yield a representative sample. For example, media is approaching
people in a market for some debated political opinion. In such sampling, there
is no sampling frame and sample is collected as per the convenience. People
who accept the offer and give some opinion cannot be generalized because
people who did not accept the offer of providing their opinion are completely
ignored.

ii) Judgement Sampling - uses expert opinion to judge which individuals to

choose. An example would be a research firm selecting people to participate
in a focus group by telephoning people and identifying who matches the
target profile through a series of questions developed by an expert. Similarly
in a popular TV show people from different expertise are called to discuss
their opinion.

iii) Quota Sampling - attempts to obtain a representative sample by specifying

quota controls on certain specified characteristics, such as age, gender, social
class and any other variables relevant to the investigation being undertaken.
The (approximate) distribution of such characteristics in the population is
required in order to replicate it in the sample. Such sampling is useful in the
absence of a sampling frame. Since non-probability sampling technique does
not require a sampling frame, it does not allow us to measure the sampling

error.

All three techniques above (convenience, judgement and quota) have some
appeal. However, in all cases we have no real guarantee that we have achieved an
adequately representative sample (we might do by chance, of course, but this
would be highly unlikely). Since we do not know the probability that an
individual will be selected for the survey, the basic rules of inferential statistics
which we will later learn to use (like confidence intervals and hypothesis testing)
are not applicable here. Specifically, standard errors (a key ingredient in
inferential procedures) are not estimatable. However, in the absence of a
sampling frame, we will have to resort to non-probability sampling methods.

B. Probability sampling means that every population unit has a known (not
necessarily equal), non-zero probability of being selected in the sample. In all
cases, selection is performed through some form of randomization. For
example, a pseudo-random number generator can be used (like Tippet’s
random numbers tables or by throwing of a dice, draw of a lottery, etc.) to
generate a sequence of ‘random' numbers. Suppose we want to select r
candidates out of n, we assign the numbers | to n, one number to each
candidate and write this numbers (1 to n) on n slips which are made as
homogeneous as possible, in shape, size, etc. These slips are then put in a bag
and thoroughly shuffled and the r slips are drawn one by one. The r
candidates corresponding to the number on the slips drawn will constitute the
random sample.

Relative to non-probability methods, probability sampling survey can be
expensive and time-consuming, and requires a sampling frame. However, we
can aim to minimize both the (random) sampling error and the systematic
sampling bias. Since the probability of selection is known, standard errors can
be computed which allows confidence intervals to be determined and
hypothesis tests to be performed.

Note: Tippet’s random number tables consist of 10400 four-digit numbers,
giving in all 10400 x 4, i.e., 41600 digits, taken from the British census reports.
These tables have proved to be fairly random in character. Any page of the table
is selected at random and the number in any row or column or diagonal selected
at random may be taken to constitute the sample.

Following are the different types of probability sampling:

i) Simple Random Sampling (SRS) -It is a special case where each population
unit has a known, equal and non-zero probability of selection. Of the various
probability samples, it is simple and it produces unbiased estimates, although
more accurate methods exist (i.e., those with smaller standard errors).

ii) Systematic Random Sampling - Systematic sampling is a statistical method
involving the selection of elements from an ordered sampling frame. The
most common form of systematic sampling is an equi-probability method.
The sampling starts by selecting an element from the list at random and then

population size. Systematic sampling is to be applied only if the given
population is logically homogeneous, because systematic sample units are
uniformly distributed over the population. The researcher must ensure that the
chosen sampling interval does not have a pattern. Any pattern would threaten
randomness.

iii) Stratified Random Sampling- This sampling technique achieves a higher

level of accuracy (smaller standard errors) by exploiting natural groupings
within the population. Here the entire heterogeneous population is divided
into a number of homogeneous groups, usually termed as ‘strata’, which
differ from one another but each of these groups is homogeneous within
itself. Then units are sampled in random from each of these strata, the sample
size in each stratum varies according to the relative importance of the stratum
in the population. A simple random sample is taken from each stratum, thus
ensuring a representative overall sample since a broad cross-section of the
population will have to be selected. Hence great care needs to be taken when
choosing the appropriate stratification factors, which should be relevant to the
purpose of the sample survey such as age or gender. The sample, which is the
aggregate of the sampled units of each of the stratum, is termed as stratified
sample and the technique of drawing this sample is known as stratified
sampling. Such a sampling is by far the best and can safely be considered as
representative of the population from which it has been drawn.

iv) Cluster Sampling — This sampling is used to reduce costs (time and money).

Here the population is divided into clusters, ideally such that each cluster is
as variable as the overall population (i.e., heterogeneity within clusters and
homogeneity between clusters). Next, some of the clusters are selected by
SRS. This is where some savings are expected, since typically the clusters
may be constructed on a geographical basis allowing interviewers to restrict
themselves to visiting households in certain areas rather than having to travel
long distances to meet the respondents required by a simple random sample
who potentially cover a large area.

A one-stage cluster sample would mean that every unit in the chosen clusters
is surveyed. It may be that the cluster sizes are large meaning that it is not
feasible to survey every unit within a cluster. A two-stage cluster sample
involves taking a simple random sample from the clusters selected by SRS
during the first stage. When a subsample is taken from a selected cluster, this
is known as a multistage design.

Multistage Sampling- It refers to the case when sample selection occurs at
two or more successive stages. Multistage sampling is frequently used in
large surveys. During the first stage, large compound units are sampled
(primary units). During the second stage, smaller units (secondary units) are

28.6 PARAMETER AND STATISTIC

In order to avoid verbal confusion with the statistical constants of the population,
viz., mean (1), variance (c”), etc., which are usually referred to as ‘parameters’,
statistical measures computed from the sample observations alone, e.g., mean
(x), variance ( s’), etc., have been termed by Professor R. A. Fischer as
‘statistic’.

As discussed in the introduction, in practice, parameter values are not known and
the estimates based on the sample values are generally used. Thus, statistic,
which may be regarded as an estimate of parameter, obtained from the sample, is
a function of the sample values only. It may be pointed out that a statistic, as it is
based on sample values and as there are multiple choices of the samples that can
be drawn from a population, varies from sample to sample. These differences in
the values of a ‘statistic’ are called ‘sampling fluctuations’. The determination of
the characterization of variation (in the values of the statistic obtained from
different samples of a same population) that may be attributed to chance or
fluctuations of sampling is regarded as one of the fundamental problems of the
sampling theory.

respectively while the sample mean and variance will be denoted by x and s
