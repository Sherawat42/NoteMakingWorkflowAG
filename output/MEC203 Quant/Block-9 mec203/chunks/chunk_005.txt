30.1 INTRODUCTION

The objective of sampling is to study the features of the population based on
sample observations. A carefully selected sample is expected to reveal these
features, and hence we shall infer about the population from a statistical analysis
of the sample. This process is known as ‘statistical inference’.

There are two types of problems. First, we may have no information at all about
some characteristics of the population, especially the values of the parameters
involved in the distribution, and it is required to obtain estimates of these
parameters. This is the problem of ‘estimation’. Secondly, some information or
hypothetical values of the parameters may be available, and it is required to test
how far the hypothesis is tenable in the light of the information provided by the
sample. This is the problem of ‘hypothesis testing’ or ‘test of significance’.

This unit will elaborate on estimation. In order to delve deep into the topic,
properties of good estimators are introduced initially which are unbiasedness,
consistency and efficiency. After describing these properties, the famous Cramer
Rao Inequality is introduced along with the asymptotic properties of the
estimators. The last sub-section of the unit deals with the methods of estimation

used for ascertaining the ‘best guess’ of true values of the estimators.

30.2 THEORY OF ESTIMATION

Suppose, we have a random sample x1, x2, ... ,Xn on a variable x, whose
distribution in the population involves an unknown parameter 0. It is required to
find an estimate of 8 based on sample values. The estimation is done in two
different ways: (i) Point estimation, and (ii) Interval estimation.

In ‘point estimation’, the estimated value is given by a single numerical value,
which is a function of sample observations (i.e., statistic). This function is called
an ’estimator’. Since the point estimate is just a numerical figure, the value of it
is less likely to coincide with the true value of the population parameter. Some
degree of uncertainty is bound to be there due to sampling error. To overcome it,
some intervals are constructed and it is claimed that the true value of the
population parameter will lie within this interval at a confidence level of say 95%
or 99%.

In ‘interval estimation’, an interval within which the true value of a parameter is
expected to lie is computed by using two quantities based on sample values. This
is known as ‘confidence interval’, and the quantities, which are used to specify
the interval, are known as ‘confidence limits’. Since our basic objective is to
estimate the parameter associated with the sample conservations, so before going
into further details, let us discuss about the notion of parameter space.
