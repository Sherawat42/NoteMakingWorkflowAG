15.1  INTRODUCTION 
In Unit 14 we discussed the use of qualitative variables (dummy variables) as 
explanatory variables in a regression model. Such qualitative variables can 
assume binary values of 0 and 1. A question may be coming to your mind: Can 
we use a dummy variable as explained variable (or, dependent variable) in a 
regression model? The answer is: Yes.  
In this Unit we will look at regression models where the dependent variable, Y, is 
a qualitative variable. Such models are called ‘limited dependent variable 
models’, or dummy dependent variable models. The term ‘limited dependent 
variable’ was first used by James Tobin in 1958 to denote regression models 
where the dependent variable is constrained to be non-negative. As you know, 
nominal variables can assume more than two categories. If the dummy dependent 
variable has exactly two categories, it is called a binary (or dichotomous) 
variable. Otherwise, it is called polychotomous variable (containing more than 
two categories). The present Unit is introductory in nature, and we will focus on 
binary cases where a dummy variable can take only two values, 0 and 1. 
If the dependent variable of the model is a dummy variable, the usual OLS 
method will not be applicable. Instead, the method of maximum likelihood 
estimation is used. This is because when the dependent variable is a dummy 
variable, the objective is to find out the maximum probability ( hence the name 
maximum likelihood) of occurrence of the given set of data. 
Binary choice models are commonly used for micro analysis in social sciences 
and medical research. There are three types of ‘limited dependent variable 
models’. These are: (i) Linear Probability Models (LPM), (ii) Logit Models, and 
(iii) Probit Models. The last two models have quite similar probability density 
functions, similar estimation procedures, and they are often used interchangeably. 
15.2 LINEAR PROBABILITY MODEL 
A linear probability model (LPM) is a regression model where the dependent 
variable (regressand) is a binary (or, dummy) variable, and the independent 
variables are either dummy or continuous.

Bernoulli distribution. As a result, it is not normally distributed. 
Recall from Unit 4 that the OLS method does not require the error term ( 𝑢௜) to be
