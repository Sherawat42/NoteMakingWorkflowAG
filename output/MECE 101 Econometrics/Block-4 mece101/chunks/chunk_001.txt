14.1 INTRODUCTION 
In regression models considered so far, we assumed that there is no change in 
parameters within a sample. There could be situations, however, where there is a 
change (or shift) in a parameter within the sample. For example, assume that 
industrial output of India depends upon inputs such as capital and labour apart 
from technology and policy environment. After the economic liberalization in 
1991, there is a major shift in the economic environment in India - there is an 
improvement in the level of technology and the level of competition. In short, we 
can say that there is a structural change in the Indian economy after economic 
liberalization. In such circumstances we cannot assume that the parameters

Let us consider another scenario. We assumed that the explanatory variables are

such as gender as explanatory variable in a regression model? Yes, we can. A 
qualitative variable can be represented by a dummy variable , which assumes 
numerical values as we will see later in this Unit.  
The dummy variables are also known as binary variables , indicator variables , 
categorical variables , and dichotomous variables . In this unit we consider the 
role of qualitative explanatory variables in regression analysis. We include 
dummy variables in regression models and show that it is a flexible tool for 
handling many interesting problems. 
14.2 CHOW TEST FOR STRUCTURAL STABILITY 
We begin with the Chow test for structural stability of a regression model. A 
structural change (also called structural break) occurs if the parameters 
underlying a relationship differ from one subset of the data to another. There may 
be several relevant subsets of data with the possibility of several structural breaks 
in a sample. For the moment we consider two subsets of 1n  and 2n  observations 
so that sample size is 1 2n n n  . Suppose, for example, we wish to investigate 
whether savings rate differs between rich households and poor households. 
Suppose we have 1n  poor households and 2n  rich households. An option before 
us is to estimate separate regression equations for the poor and the rich 
households. By comparing the estimated coefficients of both the regression 
models we can find out whether there is difference in savings rate of poor and 
rich households. Alternatively, we can estimate the regression model for the poor 
households and predict the savings rate for the rich households (or, vice versa). It 
is however not clear which choice should be made, and the two procedures might 
well yield different answers! Chow test gives concrete idea on the structural 
stability of a model.

14.3 THE NATURE OF DUMMY VRIABLES 
In regression analysis the dependent variable is frequently influenced not only by 
variables that can be readily quantified on some well-defined scale (e.g., income, 
output, prices, costs, weights, etc.), but also by other variables that are essentially 
qualitative in nature (e.g., marital status, gender, religion, caste, etc.). For 
example, holding all other factors constant, IIT graduates are found to earn more 
than their counterparts from the regional engineering colleges in India. Similarly, 
studies in the US have reported that female college teachers earn less than their 
male counterparts. Whatever may be the reason for this disparity, qualitative 
variables like gender, institution of education, etc. do influence the dependent 
variable and should be included among the independent variables. 
Since qualitative variables usually indicate the presence or absence of some 
attribute or quality, such as rural or urban, male or female, married or unmarried, 
etc. We can quantify such attributes by constructing artificial variables that take 
value 1 or 0, 1 indicating the presence (or possession) of a particular attribute and 
0 the absence of it and vice versa. For example, 1 may indicate that the person is 
a male and 0 may indicate that the person is female; or 1 may indicate that a 
person is educated and 0 that he/she is not educated and so on. Such variables 
which assume values 0 and 1 are called Dummy Variables. 
Like the quantitative variables, the dummy variables can be used in regression 
analysis very easily. There can be two cases: (i) a regression model may contain 
only dummy explanatory variables. This type of regression models is called 
analysis of variance (ANOVA) models. (ii) a regression model may contain both 
quantitative variables and dummy variables. This type of regression models is 
called the analysis of variance (ANOVA) models.

perfect multicollinearity, it is not possible to estimate the various

There are, however, several ways of resolving this problem but the simplest 
one is by using dummy variables as we had done in model (14.5). We 
should use only one dummy variable if there are two categories of a 
qualitative variable.  
Rule of Thumb: If a qualitative variable has ‘m’ categories, introduce ‘(m – 
1)’ dummy variables. 
Thus, if a qualitative variable has 4 characteristics, we introduce only 3 
dummy variables. If this rule is not followed, we will fall into what is 
known as the dummy variable trap , i.e., a situation of perfect 
multicollinearity.
