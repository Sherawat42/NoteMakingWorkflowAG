# UNIT 1: The Classical Approach

## 1. Core Theme

The Classical Approach explains the economy as a **self-adjusting system**. Markets clear automatically through **flexible wages, prices, and interest rates**. As a result, the economy normally operates at **full employment**.

## 2. Why Macroeconomics Matters

Macroeconomics studies economy-wide aggregates like output, employment, prices, inflation, and interest rates. It is necessary because what is true for one individual or firm may not be true for the whole economy.

**Example**: One firm can hire workers without changing wages. But if all firms demand more labor at once, wages rise.

## 3. Schools of Thought

### Classical School
- Believes in flexible wages and prices
- Believes markets clear automatically
- Emphasizes supply-side determination of output
- Favors minimal government intervention

### Keynesian School
- Emerged after the Great Depression
- Argues wages and prices are sticky
- Says output depends on aggregate demand in the short run
- Supports active fiscal and monetary policy

## 4. Basic Features of Classical Theory

- Full employment is the normal state of the economy
- Wages and prices are perfectly flexible
- Competition is assumed in all markets
- Saving and investment are equated by the interest rate
- Money affects only nominal variables, not real variables
- Government intervention is unnecessary in normal conditions

## 5. Determination of Output and Employment

### Main Logic
- Output depends on the production function: **Y = f(N, K̄)**
- In the short run, capital is fixed, so labor determines output changes
- Firms hire labor until **real wage = marginal product of labor**
- Labor demand slopes downward; labor supply slopes upward
- Their intersection gives equilibrium employment and output

## ✅ Important Result

In the classical model, **output is determined by supply-side factors**, not by aggregate demand.

## 6. Quantity Theory of Money

### Fisher Equation

**MV = PY**

Where:
- M = money supply
- V = velocity of circulation
- P = price level
- Y = real output

### Implication
If **V** and **Y** are constant, then **P is directly proportional to M**.

## ✅ Important Result

An increase in money supply increases the **price level**, but does **not** change real output or employment.

## 7. Say's Law of Market

**Supply creates its own demand.**

### Logic
- Production generates income
- Income is either consumed or saved
- Savings become investment through the interest rate mechanism
- Therefore, general overproduction is impossible

## 8. Classical Dichotomy

The economy is divided into:

### Real Sector
- Output
- Employment
- Real wage
- Real interest rate

### Monetary Sector
- Price level
- Nominal wage
- Nominal values

These two sectors are treated as independent.

## 9. Neutrality of Money

Money is called **neutral** because changes in money supply affect only nominal variables, not real variables.

### Example
If money supply doubles:
- Prices double
- Nominal wages double
- Real wage stays unchanged
- Output and employment remain unchanged

## 10. Long Run vs Short Run

- **Long run**: prices and wages flexible, so classical model works well
- **Short run**: prices and wages may be sticky, which opens the door to Keynesian analysis

## ⚠️ Exam Tip

If asked to criticize classical theory, write:
- unrealistic assumption of full flexibility
- inability to explain mass unemployment during the Great Depression
- excessive faith in automatic adjustment

# UNIT 2: The Keynesian Model

## 1. Core Theme

The Keynesian model explains how an economy can settle below full employment because of **deficient aggregate demand**.

## 2. Main Assumptions

- Short-run framework
- Wages and prices are sticky
- Economy may operate below full employment
- Firms respond to demand conditions
- Output is demand-determined

## ✅ Very Important

The central Keynesian claim is:

> **Output and employment depend on effective demand in the short run.**

## 3. Components of Aggregate Demand

In a closed economy:

**AD = C + I + G**

Where:
- C = consumption
- I = investment
- G = government expenditure

In an open economy:

**AD = C + I + G + NX**

## 4. Consumption Function

### Basic Form

**C = a + bY**

Where:
- a = autonomous consumption
- b = MPC (marginal propensity to consume)
- 0 < b < 1

### Important Ideas
- Consumption rises with income
- But it rises by less than income
- Some part of extra income is saved

### Related Concepts
- **MPC = ΔC / ΔY**
- **APC = C / Y**
- APC falls as income rises
- MPC remains constant in the simple linear model

## 5. Investment Function

### Main Idea
Investment means spending on **real capital goods**, not financial assets.

### Forms
- Autonomous investment: **I = Ī**
- Extended form: **I = c̄ + dY − ei**

### Important Result
Investment is **inversely related to interest rate**.
Higher interest rate -> lower present value of returns -> less investment.

## 6. Equilibrium Output

### Two-Sector Model

**Y = C + I**

Substituting consumption function:

**Y = a + bY + Ī**

So equilibrium output is:

**Yₑ = (a + Ī) / (1 − b)**

## 7. Multiplier

### Formula

**k = 1 / (1 − b)**

### Meaning
A rise in autonomous expenditure causes a multiplied rise in income.

### Logic
One person's spending becomes another person's income, creating repeated rounds of spending.

## ✅ Very Important

Higher MPC -> larger multiplier

## 8. Government Multipliers

### Government Expenditure Multiplier

**ΔY / ΔG = 1 / (1 − b)**

### Tax Multiplier

**ΔY / ΔT = −b / (1 − b)**

### Balanced Budget Multiplier

**= 1**

## ✅ Important Conclusion

Government spending multiplier is larger in absolute value than the tax multiplier.

## 9. Saving Function and Alternative Equilibrium

### Saving Function

**S = −a + (1 − b)Y**

Since:
- **MPS = 1 − MPC**
- **MPC + MPS = 1**

Equilibrium can also be written as:

**S = I**

In a three-sector economy:

**S + T = I + G**

## 10. Paradox of Thrift

### Main Idea
When everyone tries to save more, aggregate demand falls, income falls, and total saving may not rise.

## ✅ Exam Line

Paradox of thrift is a **short-run Keynesian result**. Individually saving is rational, but collectively it may reduce national income.

## 11. Liquidity Preference

People hold money for three motives:
- transactions motive
- precautionary motive
- speculative motive

### Money Demand Function

**L = kY − hi**

Money demand:
- rises with income
- falls with interest rate

## 12. Liquidity Trap

At very low interest rates:
- people prefer holding cash
- speculative demand becomes infinitely elastic
- monetary policy becomes ineffective

## ✅ Very Important

In a liquidity trap:
- **monetary policy fails**
- **fiscal policy remains effective**

## 13. Role of Government in Keynesian Theory

Government intervention is necessary because:
- markets do not always self-correct quickly
- low demand causes unemployment
- fiscal expansion raises aggregate demand directly

# UNIT 3: The Neoclassical Synthesis (IS-LM)

## 1. Core Theme

The IS-LM model combines:
- Keynesian goods market equilibrium
- Keynesian money market equilibrium

It gives simultaneous equilibrium in output and interest rate.

## 2. IS Curve

### Meaning
The IS curve shows all combinations of **income (Y)** and **interest rate (i)** where the **goods market is in equilibrium**.

### Why Downward Sloping?
Lower interest rate -> higher investment -> higher demand -> higher equilibrium income

### Main Shifts
- ↑G -> IS shifts right
- ↑autonomous consumption -> IS shifts right
- ↑autonomous investment -> IS shifts right
- ↑taxes -> IS shifts left

## 3. LM Curve

### Meaning
The LM curve shows all combinations of **income (Y)** and **interest rate (i)** where the **money market is in equilibrium**.

### Why Upward Sloping?
Higher income -> more money demand -> interest rate must rise if money supply is fixed

### Main Shifts
- ↑money supply -> LM shifts right
- ↓money supply -> LM shifts left

## 4. IS-LM Equilibrium

The intersection of IS and LM gives:
- equilibrium output
- equilibrium interest rate

## ✅ Very Important

### Policy Effects in IS-LM
- Fiscal expansion: IS shifts right -> Y rises, i rises
- Monetary expansion: LM shifts right -> Y rises, i falls

## 5. AD Curve from IS-LM

The AD curve is derived by varying the price level and tracing the resulting IS-LM equilibrium.

### Keynes Effect
Lower price level -> real money balances rise -> LM shifts right -> interest falls -> investment rises -> output rises

Therefore, the AD curve slopes downward.

## 6. AS Curve Interpretations

### Classical AS
- Vertical at full employment output

### Keynesian AS
- Horizontal in deep recession / excess capacity case

### Intermediate / Short-run AS
- Upward sloping

## ⚠️ Exam Tip

If asked to compare Classical, Keynesian, and Neoclassical Synthesis, write:
- Classical: full flexibility, full employment, money neutrality
- Keynesian: sticky prices, demand-determined output
- IS-LM synthesis: combines real and monetary sectors in short-run equilibrium analysis

# UNIT 4: Open Economy Macroeconomics I

## 1. Open Economy Income Identity

**Y = C + I + G + NX**

Where:
- NX = net exports = X − M

## 2. GDP, GNP, Current Account

- **GDP**: output produced within domestic borders
- **GNP**: GDP + net factor income from abroad
- **Current Account** includes trade balance, services, income, and transfers

## 3. Twin Deficits Identity

**(S − I) + (T − G) = CA**

## ✅ Very Important

A current account deficit reflects either:
- low private saving relative to investment
- government budget deficit
- or both

## 4. Balance of Payments (BOP)

### Main Components
- Current Account
- Capital Account / Financial flows
- Official reserve transactions

### Identity
BOP as an accounting statement always balances when reserve transactions are included.

## 5. Exchange Rates

### Nominal Exchange Rate
Domestic currency price of foreign currency

### Real Exchange Rate

**r = (e × p_f) / p_d**

Where:
- e = nominal exchange rate
- p_f = foreign price level
- p_d = domestic price level

### Interpretation
Real depreciation -> exports become relatively cheaper -> net exports improve

## 6. Exchange Rate Regimes

### Flexible Exchange Rate
Exchange rate determined by market forces

### Fixed Exchange Rate
Central bank/government maintains a chosen parity

## ✅ Important Distinction

- **Depreciation / appreciation**: market-driven changes under flexible rate
- **Devaluation / revaluation**: official changes under fixed rate

# UNIT 5: Open Economy Macroeconomics II (Mundell-Fleming)

## 1. Core Theme

This unit extends IS-LM to an open economy with capital flows and exchange rates.

## 2. Open Economy IS Curve

In open economy:
- foreign income affects exports
- real exchange rate affects net exports
- therefore external sector affects equilibrium output

## 3. Perfect Capital Mobility

With perfect capital mobility:
- domestic and foreign assets are close substitutes
- domestic interest rate tends to equal world interest rate

## 4. Policy Effectiveness Under Flexible Exchange Rates

### Fiscal Policy
Generally ineffective under perfect capital mobility.

### Why?
- Fiscal expansion shifts IS right
- interest rate rises
- capital inflow occurs
- domestic currency appreciates
- net exports fall
- IS shifts back

### Monetary Policy
Very effective.

### Why?
- Monetary expansion shifts LM right
- interest rate falls
- capital outflow occurs
- currency depreciates
- net exports rise
- IS shifts right further

## ✅ Very Important Result

Under **flexible exchange rates**:
- **Fiscal policy is weak / ineffective**
- **Monetary policy is strong / effective**

## 5. Policy Effectiveness Under Fixed Exchange Rates

### Fiscal Policy
Very effective.

### Why?
- Fiscal expansion raises interest rate
- capital inflow occurs
- central bank intervenes to maintain parity
- reserves rise
- money supply expands
- LM shifts right

### Monetary Policy
Ineffective.

### Why?
- Monetary expansion lowers interest rate
- capital outflow occurs
- central bank sells reserves to defend exchange rate
- money supply contracts back
- LM returns to original position

## ✅ Very Important Result

Under **fixed exchange rates**:
- **Fiscal policy is effective**
- **Monetary policy is ineffective**

## 6. Policy Trilemma / Impossible Trinity

A country cannot simultaneously achieve all three:
- fixed exchange rate
- perfect capital mobility
- independent monetary policy

It can choose only **two out of three**.

## ✅ Best Exam Conclusion

- Fixed exchange rate + capital mobility -> no monetary independence
- Flexible exchange rate + capital mobility -> monetary independence possible
- Fixed exchange rate + monetary independence -> requires capital controls

# High-Value Comparison Tables

## Classical vs Keynesian

| Feature | Classical | Keynesian |
|---|---|---|
| Prices and wages | Flexible | Sticky |
| Output determined by | Supply | Demand |
| Employment | Full employment normal | Unemployment possible |
| Government role | Minimal | Active |
| Money | Neutral | Can affect output indirectly in short run |
| Saving-investment adjustment | Interest rate | Income adjustment |

## Flexible vs Fixed Exchange Rate (Perfect Capital Mobility)

| Policy | Flexible Exchange Rate | Fixed Exchange Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Ineffective | Effective |
| Monetary policy | Effective | Ineffective |
| Exchange rate | Adjusts freely | Defended by central bank |
| Money supply | Exogenous / policy-controlled | Endogenous under intervention |
