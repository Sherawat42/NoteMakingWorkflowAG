## 1.1 Introduction & Schools of Thought

### Why Macroeconomics?
Macroeconomics analyses economy-level aggregates. What holds for individual units may **not** hold for the economy (**fallacy of composition**). Example: a single firm can hire workers without affecting wages, but if all firms increase demand simultaneously, wages rise.

### Two Major Schools

| Feature | Classical | Keynesian |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| **Era** | 18th-19th century | Post-1936 (Great Depression) |
| **Prices/Wages** | Fully flexible | Sticky (rigid) |
| **Markets** | Self-adjusting | Can fail in short run |
| **Employment** | Always full employment | Unemployment possible |
| **Government** | Laissez-faire (minimal) | Active intervention needed |
| **Focus** | Supply side | Demand side |
| **Key figures** | Smith, Ricardo, Say, Mill | J.M. Keynes |

**Great Depression (1929-33)**: Massive decline in income, output, employment; ~25% unemployment. Falsified classical assumptions. Keynes argued the cause was **inadequate aggregate demand**.

### Post-Keynesian Evolution
- **1970s Stagflation** → challenged Keynesian orthodoxy (simultaneous inflation + unemployment)
- **New Classical Economics** → revived classical views with rational expectations, microfoundations
- **New Keynesian Economics** → kept sticky prices but added microfoundations and rational expectations

---

## 1.2 Basic Features of Classical Theory

1. **Flexible wages and prices** — all markets clear automatically
2. **Laissez-faire** — minimal government intervention
3. **Perfect competition** in all markets (goods, labor, money)
4. **Supply creates its own demand** (Say's Law)
5. **Money neutrality** — money affects only prices, not real variables
6. **Full employment** — the normal state of the economy

---

## 1.3 Determination of Output and Employment

### Production Function
**Y = f(N, K̄)** — output depends on labor (N) and fixed capital (K̄)

- Short run: capital is fixed → output varies only with labor
- **Diminishing marginal returns**: each additional worker adds less output (MPₙ declines)
- MPₙ = ΔY/ΔN = slope of production function

### Labor Market

**Labor Demand**: Firms hire until **MPₙ = W/P** (real wage). Downward-sloping because of diminishing returns.

**Labor Supply**: Workers choose between labor and leisure. Higher real wage → more labor supplied (substitution effect dominates). Upward-sloping.

**Equilibrium**: Where Nᵈ = Nˢ → determines equilibrium real wage (W/P)* and employment N* → which determines output Y* through the production function.

> **Key Result**: In the classical system, output is determined entirely by the **supply side** (technology + labor market). Demand plays no role.

---

## 1.4 Quantity Theory of Money (QTM)

### Fisher's Equation of Exchange
**MV = PY** (or MV = PT)

- M = money supply
- V = velocity of circulation (assumed **constant** — institutional)
- P = general price level
- Y = real output (determined by labor market, so also fixed in short run)

### Implication
Since V and Y are constant: **P = (V/Y) × M**

Price level is **directly proportional** to money supply. Doubling M doubles P. Money only affects **nominal** variables (prices), not **real** variables (output, employment).

### Cambridge Approach
**Mᵈ = kPY** where k = 1/V

Focuses on money *demand* rather than transactions. People hold a fraction k of nominal income as money. Equivalent to Fisher but emphasizes the **store of value** function.

---

## 1.5 Say's Law of Market

> *"Supply creates its own demand"*

### The Logic
1. Production generates income equal to the value of output (circular flow)
2. All income is spent — either on consumption or saved
3. Savings are channeled to investment through the **interest rate mechanism**
4. Therefore: no **general glut** (economy-wide overproduction) is possible

### Interest Rate as the Equilibrating Variable
- Saving = increasing function of interest rate: S = S(r), S'(r) > 0
- Investment = decreasing function of interest rate: I = I(r), I'(r) < 0
- Equilibrium: S(r*) = I(r*) — the loanable funds market clears

> **Implication**: Aggregate demand can never be deficient. Recessions cannot be caused by demand shortfalls.

---

## 1.6 Classical Dichotomy

The economy separates into two independent sectors:

| Real Sector | Monetary Sector |
|-------------|-----------------|
| Determines: output (Y), employment (N), real wage (W/P), real interest rate (r) | Determines: price level (P), nominal wage (W), nominal values |
| Through: production function + labor market | Through: quantity theory (MV = PY) |
| **Independent of money supply** | Depends on M |

### AD-AS in Classical Model
- **AS curve**: Vertical at Y* (full employment output) — output doesn't respond to price changes
- **AD curve**: Derived from QTM → AD: P = MV/Y → downward-sloping
- Increase in M → AD shifts right → P rises → Y unchanged

### Neutrality of Money
A change in money supply changes **all nominal variables proportionally** but leaves **all real variables unchanged**:
- Double M → P doubles, W doubles
- But W/P unchanged → N unchanged → Y unchanged → r unchanged

### Saving-Investment Equilibrium
S = I achieved through the **interest rate**. If S > I → interest rate falls → I rises, S falls → equilibrium restored. No role for income adjustment (contrast with Keynes).

---

## 1.7 Long Run vs Short Run

- **Long run**: Prices and wages are fully flexible → classical model applies → economy at full employment
- **Short run**: Prices and wages may be sticky → output can deviate from full employment → Keynesian model applies

> The classical model is a **long-run benchmark**. The Keynesian model explains short-run deviations.

# UNIT 2: THE KEYNESIAN MODEL
*Pages 27-53 | Authors: Prof. Ananya Ghosh Dastidar & Prof. Kaustuva Barik*

---

## 2.1 Key Assumptions

1. **Short-run analysis** — wages and prices are sticky
2. **Excess capacity** exists — economy is below full employment
3. **Output is demand-determined** — firms produce whatever is demanded
4. **Homogeneous output** — single good economy
5. **Fixed price level** — nominal = real values

---

## 2.2 Components of Aggregate Demand

### Consumption Function
**C = a + bY** ... (Keynesian consumption function)
- **a** = autonomous consumption (a > 0) — spending independent of income
- **b** = MPC (Marginal Propensity to Consume), 0 < b < 1
- **APC = C/Y = a/Y + b** — declines as Y rises (but always > MPC)
- **MPC = ΔC/ΔY = b** — constant

> **Keynes's Psychological Law**: 0 < MPC < 1 — people consume more as income rises, but not all of the increase.

### Investment Function
**I = c̄ + dY − ei** ... (general form)
- **Autonomous investment** (c̄): independent of Y and i — driven by "animal spirits" (business confidence, expectations)
- **Induced investment** (dY): depends on income level
- **Interest sensitivity** (−ei): higher interest rate → higher cost of borrowing → less investment

#### Present Value Rule
A project is undertaken if: **PV of expected returns > Cost of investment**

PV = R₁/(1+i) + R₂/(1+i)² + ... + Rₙ/(1+i)ⁿ

Higher interest rate → lower PV → fewer profitable projects → less investment. This is why I is inversely related to i.

---

## 2.3 Determination of Equilibrium Output

### The Keynesian Cross (AD = Y)
**Equilibrium condition**: Y = AD = C + I (two-sector)

Y = a + bY + Ī → **Yₑ = (a + Ī)/(1 − b)**

At equilibrium: planned expenditure = output. Off equilibrium: **unplanned inventory changes** drive adjustment.
- If Y > AD → unplanned inventory accumulation → firms cut production
- If Y < AD → unplanned inventory depletion → firms increase production

### The Investment Multiplier
**k = 1/(1 − b) = 1/MPS**

A ₹1 increase in autonomous investment raises equilibrium income by ₹k.

**Process**: ΔI → ΔY = ΔI → ΔC = bΔI → ΔY = bΔI → ΔC = b²ΔI → ...
Total: ΔY = ΔI × (1 + b + b² + ...) = **ΔI/(1−b)**

Example: If MPC = 0.75, multiplier = 4. A ₹100 increase in investment raises income by ₹400.

---

## 2.4 Government: Expenditure and Tax Multipliers

### Three-Sector Model (with Government)
AD = C + I + G = a + b(Y − T) + Ī + G

**Yₑ = (a − bT + Ī + G)/(1 − b)**

| Multiplier | Formula | Value (b=0.75) | Explanation |
|-----------|---------|----------------|-------------|
| **Government Expenditure** | ΔY/ΔG = 1/(1−b) | 4 | Same as investment multiplier |
| **Tax** | ΔY/ΔT = −b/(1−b) | −3 | Negative; smaller than spending multiplier |
| **Balanced Budget** | = 1 | 1 | Equal ↑G and ↑T raises Y by exactly ΔG |

> **Why is the tax multiplier smaller?** A ₹1 tax increase reduces consumption by only b (since MPC < 1), while ₹1 of government spending adds directly to demand.

### Proportional Taxes (T = tY)
**Yₑ = (a + Ī + G)/{1 − b(1−t)}**

Multiplier = **1/{1 − b(1−t)}** — smaller than with lump-sum taxes because income taxes are an **automatic stabilizer**.

### Open Economy Multiplier
With imports M = h + mY (m = marginal propensity to import):

**Multiplier = 1/(1 − b + m)** — smaller than closed economy because imports are a leakage.

---

## 2.5 Alternative Equilibrium: S = I

### Saving Function
**S = −a + (1−b)Y** — mirror image of consumption function
- Slope = MPS = (1−b)
- MPC + MPS = 1
- **Break-even income**: where C = Y and S = 0

### Three-Sector: S + T = I + G
Or equivalently: **(S − I) = (G − T)** — if saving exceeds investment, the government is running a deficit.

### Paradox of Thrift ⚠️
> When everyone tries to save more, aggregate saving may stay the same or even **fall**.

**Case 1 (Autonomous Investment)**: Saving function shifts up → consumption falls → AD falls → income falls → saving falls back to equal unchanged I. **Result**: Same saving, lower income.

**Case 2 (Induced Investment)**: Even worse — lower output reduces induced investment too. **Result**: Lower saving AND lower income.

> This is a **short-run** phenomenon. In the long run, higher saving can finance higher investment and growth.

---

## 2.6 Liquidity Preference (Money Demand)

### Three Motives for Holding Money

1. **Transactions demand** — for daily purchases; increases with income
2. **Precautionary demand** — for emergencies; increases with income  
3. **Speculative demand** — for profiting from bond price changes; **decreases with interest rate**

### Money Demand Function
**L = kY − hi**
- k = income sensitivity; h = interest sensitivity
- Downward-sloping in i-L space

### Money Market Equilibrium
L = Mˢ/P (money supply set by central bank, vertical line)
- ↑Mˢ → excess supply → people buy bonds → bond prices ↑ → i ↓

### The Liquidity Trap 🪤
When interest rates hit their **floor** (i*):
- Everyone expects rates to rise (bond prices to fall)
- No one wants to hold bonds → infinite money demand
- Money demand curve becomes **horizontal**
- Any additional money supply is simply hoarded
- **Monetary policy becomes completely ineffective**
- Only **fiscal policy** can stimulate the economy

> **Real-world**: Post-2008 (USA, Japan, Europe) — zero lower bound. Led to quantitative easing and massive fiscal stimulus.

---

## 2.7 Role of Government

Keynes advocated **active government** because:
- Markets fail to self-adjust in the short run (sticky wages/prices)
- Lack of effective demand causes recessions
- Declining wages worsen recessions (reduce purchasing power)
- Government spending directly adds to AD with multiplier effects

**Success story**: Great Depression recovery through fiscal expansion.
**Concerns**: Budget deficits, rising public debt, crowding out of private investment.

# UNIT 3: THE NEOCLASSICAL SYNTHESIS (IS-LM)
*Pages 54-69 | Author: Prof. Kaustuva Barik*

---

## 3.1 The IS Curve (Goods Market Equilibrium)

### IS Equation
From Y = C(Yᵈ) + I(i) + G:

**Y = αG(Ā − ei)** where αG = 1/(1−b−d) and Ā = a + c + Ḡ − bT̄

### Properties
- **Downward-sloping**: Lower i → higher I → higher AD → higher Y
- **Slope** = −(1−b−d)/e
- **Flatter IS** = more interest-sensitive economy (large e, large multiplier)
- **Steeper IS** = less interest-sensitive

### IS Curve Shifts
| Change | IS Shift |
|--------|----------|
| ↑G or ↑a or ↑c | → Rightward (parallel) |
| ↑T | → Leftward |
| ↑MPC (b) | Slope change: IS becomes flatter |

### Disequilibrium
- **Above/right of IS** → excess supply of goods
- **Below/left of IS** → excess demand for goods

---

## 3.2 The LM Curve (Money Market Equilibrium)

### LM Equation
From L = kY − hi = M̄/P̄:

**Y = (1/k)(M̄/P̄) + (h/k)i**

### Properties
- **Upward-sloping**: Higher Y → more money demand → i must rise to maintain equilibrium
- **Slope** = k/h
- **Flatter LM** = high interest sensitivity (large h) — near liquidity trap
- **Steeper LM** = low interest sensitivity — near classical case
- **Horizontal LM** = liquidity trap (h → ∞)
- **Vertical LM** = classical case (h → 0)

### LM Curve Shifts
- **↑ Money supply** → LM shifts **right** (lower i at each Y)
- **↓ Money supply** → LM shifts **left**

### Disequilibrium
- **Left of LM** → excess money supply → buy bonds → i falls
- **Right of LM** → excess money demand → sell bonds → i rises

---

## 3.3 IS-LM Simultaneous Equilibrium

The intersection of IS and LM gives the **unique (i*, Y*)** where:
- Goods market is in equilibrium (AD = Y)
- Money market is in equilibrium (L = M/P)

### Shocks
- **IS shift right** (demand shock) → Y↑, i↑
- **LM shift right** (monetary expansion) → Y↑, i↓

### Adjustment Process
If the economy is not at IS-LM intersection, automatic forces push it toward equilibrium:
- Excess goods supply → firms cut output
- Excess money demand → people sell bonds → i rises
- Both markets are **interdependent** — real shocks affect money market and vice versa

### Supply Shocks
- IS is relatively stable during supply shocks (real quantities don't change with prices)
- Adverse supply shock → prices rise → real money balances fall → LM shifts left → i rises, Y falls

---

## 3.4 The AD-AS Model

### AD Curve (from IS-LM)
**Derivation**: Vary price level P → changes M/P → shifts LM → traces out different (Y, P) pairs

**Keynes Effect**: P↓ → M/P↑ → i↓ → I↑ → Y↑ — hence AD slopes downward

**Shifts**: Anything shifting IS or LM right → AD shifts right

### AS Curve — Three Views

| School | AS Shape | Implication |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| **Classical** | Vertical at Y* | Output fixed; price changes have no real effect |
| **Keynesian** | Horizontal | Output expands freely; no inflation |
| **New Classical (Lucas)** | Upward-sloping (short run) | Price increases partially raise output |

**SRAS**: Upward-sloping (short run); **LRAS**: Vertical (long run)

### AD-AS Equilibrium
AD ∩ SRAS → determines (Y₀, P₀). A demand shock shifts AD right → both Y and P rise in the short run → but in the **long run**, output returns to LRAS level with only higher prices.

## 4.1 National Income Identity in an Open Economy

**Y = C + I + G + NX** where NX = X − M (net exports)

- **NX > 0** → trade surplus
- **NX < 0** → trade deficit

### GDP vs GNP
- **GDP**: Income within country's borders
- **GNP = GDP + NFIA** (Net Factor Income from Abroad)
- **Current Account (CA) = NX + NFIA**

### The Twin Deficits Identity ⭐
**(S − I) + (T − G) = CA**

A current account deficit reflects:
- Private investment exceeding saving (S − I < 0), OR
- Budget deficit (T − G < 0), OR
- Both

### Open vs Closed Economy
- **Closed**: Expenditure = Income always; S − I = G − T
- **Open**: Can borrow from abroad; expenditure can exceed income (CA deficit)

---

## 4.2 Balance of Payments (BOP)

### Current Account
Records trade in goods, services, factor incomes, and transfers:
1. **Balance of Trade** (merchandise exports − imports)
2. **Invisibles**: Services trade, net investment income, unilateral transfers (remittances, aid)

### Capital Account
Records cross-border asset transactions:
- **FDI** (Foreign Direct Investment) — long-term, non-debt creating
- **FPI** (Foreign Portfolio Investment) — short-term, volatile
- **Borrowing** — debt-creating flows

### BOP Balance
BOP = Current Account + Capital Account + Official Reserve Transactions = 0

- BOP surplus → foreign exchange reserves ↑
- BOP deficit → reserves ↓

---

## 4.3 Exchange Rates

### Nominal Exchange Rate (e)
Units of domestic currency per foreign currency. e = ₹80/$ means ₹80 buys $1.
- e rises → domestic currency **depreciates**
- e falls → domestic currency **appreciates**

### Real Exchange Rate (r)
**r = (e × pf)/pd** — price of foreign goods relative to domestic goods
- r rises → real depreciation → boosts exports, reduces imports
- r falls → real appreciation → hurts exports

### Exchange Rate Regimes

| Regime | How e is Determined | Reserves Needed? |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Flexible (Float)** | Market demand & supply | No |
| **Managed Float** | Market with central bank intervention band | Yes |
| **Fixed** | Set by government | Yes (adequate) |

- **Depreciation/Appreciation**: Market-driven changes (float)
- **Devaluation/Revaluation**: Official changes (fixed)

# UNIT 5: OPEN ECONOMY MACROECONOMICS — II (Mundell-Fleming)
*Pages 81-93 | Author: Prof. Ananya Ghosh Dastidar*

---

## 5.1 Open Economy IS-LM Framework

### Open Economy IS Curve
**Y = C(Yᵈ) + I(i) + G + NX(Yf, Y, r)**

New determinants vs closed economy:
- **Foreign GDP (Yf)**: ↑Yf → ↑exports → IS shifts right
- **Real exchange rate (r)**: ↑r (depreciation) → ↑NX → IS shifts right

**Marshall-Lerner Condition**: Depreciation improves trade balance only if sum of export and import price elasticities > 1 (assumed to hold).

### LM Curve (unchanged from closed economy)
Upward-sloping. But in open economies, **capital flows affect money supply**.

### Perfect Capital Mobility
- Domestic and foreign bonds are perfect substitutes
- **i = if** must hold (interest rate parity)
- **BP curve**: Horizontal at i = if

---

## 5.2 Policy Effectiveness — The Mundell-Fleming Results ⭐⭐⭐

### Under FLEXIBLE Exchange Rates

#### Fiscal Policy → ❌ INEFFECTIVE
1. ↑G → IS shifts right → i rises above if
2. Capital inflows → currency **appreciates** (e↓, r↓)
3. NX falls → IS shifts back left
4. **Final**: Y unchanged, NX worsened, fiscal position deteriorated

#### Monetary Policy → ✅ VERY EFFECTIVE
1. ↑M → LM shifts right → i falls below if
2. Capital outflows → currency **depreciates** (e↑, r↑)
3. NX rises → IS shifts right
4. **Final**: Y rises significantly, NX improves

### Under FIXED Exchange Rates

#### Fiscal Policy → ✅ VERY EFFECTIVE
1. ↑G → IS shifts right → i rises above if
2. Capital inflows → excess supply of foreign exchange
3. Central bank buys dollars (to maintain fixed e) → reserves↑ → M↑
4. LM shifts right automatically
5. **Final**: Y rises, no crowding out (monetary policy accommodates)

#### Monetary Policy → ❌ INEFFECTIVE
1. ↑M → LM shifts right → i falls below if
2. Capital outflows → excess demand for foreign exchange
3. Central bank sells dollars (to maintain fixed e) → reserves↓ → M↓
4. LM shifts back left
5. **Final**: Everything returns to starting point

### Summary Table

| | **Flexible Exchange Rate** | **Fixed Exchange Rate** |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal Policy** | ❌ Ineffective (crowded out by appreciation) | ✅ Effective (accommodated by reserve changes) |
| **Monetary Policy** | ✅ Effective (amplified by depreciation) | ❌ Ineffective (reversed by intervention) |

---

## 5.3 The Policy Trilemma (Impossible Trinity) ⭐

A country **cannot** simultaneously have all three:
1. ✅ **Perfect capital mobility**
2. ✅ **Fixed exchange rate**
3. ✅ **Independent monetary policy**

**Pick any two:**
- Fixed rate + Capital mobility → lose monetary independence (money supply becomes endogenous)
- Flexible rate + Capital mobility → keep monetary independence ✅
- Fixed rate + Monetary independence → need capital controls
