## Section: Liquidity Preference (2.5)

### Core Idea

An important contribution of Keynes was identifying the **interest sensitivity of money demand**. Unlike classical economists who viewed money demand as solely transaction-based, Keynes argued that people demand money (liquidity) for three motives: transactions, precautionary, and speculative. Money demand refers to the demand for **real balances** (nominal money deflated by the price level). Since prices are constant in the Keynesian system, changes in nominal money demand equal changes in real money demand.

## Section: Components of Demand for Money (2.5.1)

### Core Idea

Keynes identified three motives for holding money, each generating a component of money demand. The **transactions** and **precautionary** demands depend positively on income, while the **speculative** demand depends negatively on the interest rate. The combined money demand function is: **L = qY − ti**, where L is real money demand, q captures income sensitivity, and t captures interest sensitivity.

### Key Concepts

#### Transaction Demand

People hold cash because money is a **medium of exchange** useful for making transactions. Individuals with higher income hold more money for transactions than those with lower income. Transaction demand is **directly proportional to income** — as income rises, the volume of transactions increases, requiring more money.

#### Precautionary Demand

People hold money as a buffer against unforeseen contingencies — medical emergencies, accidents, unexpected expenses. Precautionary demand is also an **increasing function of income**: wealthier individuals hold larger precautionary balances because they face larger potential contingencies and have more to protect.

#### Speculative Demand

This is Keynes's most distinctive contribution to monetary theory. In an economy with two financial assets — money (yields no return but is liquid) and bonds (yield interest income and potential capital gains/losses):

- **When interest rates are high**: People expect rates to fall and bond prices to rise → they reduce money holdings and buy bonds (to capture capital gains) → **low speculative money demand**
- **When interest rates are low**: People expect rates to rise and bond prices to fall → they sell bonds and hold money (to avoid capital losses) → **high speculative money demand**

The **inverse relationship** between money demand and interest rate also follows from the **opportunity cost** logic: money yields no return, so the interest rate represents the forgone income from holding money instead of bonds. Higher interest rates → higher opportunity cost → less money demanded.

#### Money Demand Function

**L = qY − ti** ... (2.15)

where:
- L = demand for real balances
- q > 0 = income sensitivity of money demand
- Y = aggregate income
- t > 0 = interest sensitivity of money demand
- i = rate of interest

The money demand curve is **downward-sloping** in interest rate–money demand space (Fig. 2.10). Movement along the curve represents the interest-rate effect. Shifts of the curve represent income changes: higher Y shifts L to the right; lower Y shifts L to the left.

#### Money Market Equilibrium

Money supply is determined by the **central bank** and is represented by a vertical line Mˢ/P (real money supply). Equilibrium occurs at the intersection of money demand and money supply. An increase in money supply (from (Mˢ/P)₀ to (Mˢ/P)₁) reduces the equilibrium interest rate (from i₀ to i₁) — this is a critical result for monetary policy transmission.

### Definitions

- **Liquidity Preference**: Keynes's term for the demand for money — the desire to hold wealth in liquid (money) form rather than in illiquid assets like bonds. It encompasses all three motives for holding money.
- **Real Balances**: Nominal money supply divided by the price level (M/P). Since agents care about purchasing power, money demand is expressed in real terms.
- **Speculative Demand for Money**: The component of money demand that arises from agents' desire to profit from expected changes in bond prices. Inversely related to the interest rate.
- **Opportunity Cost of Money**: The interest income forgone by holding money instead of interest-bearing bonds. Equal to the market interest rate.

## Section: Liquidity Trap (2.5.2)

### Core Idea

The **liquidity trap** is a special Keynesian situation where interest rates have fallen to their lowest possible level, and the demand for money becomes **infinitely elastic** (perfectly horizontal). At this point, everyone expects interest rates to rise (bond prices to fall), so no one wants to hold bonds — everyone prefers to hold money. Any additional money supply injected by the central bank is simply absorbed into money holdings without reducing the interest rate further. **Monetary policy becomes completely ineffective**.

### Key Concepts

#### Mechanism of the Liquidity Trap

When interest rates are extremely low:
1. Bond prices are correspondingly very high
2. Everyone expects interest rates to rise and bond prices to fall
3. No one wants to hold bonds (fear of capital losses)
4. Everyone wants to hold money instead
5. The money demand curve becomes **horizontal** at interest rate i* (Fig. 2.11)
6. The parameter t in the money demand function tends to infinity
7. Any increase in money supply is simply hoarded — it does not reduce the interest rate

### Definitions

- **Liquidity Trap**: The situation where the interest rate has reached its minimum level (i*), making money demand perfectly elastic. Any additional money supply is absorbed without reducing the interest rate. Monetary policy cannot stimulate the economy because it cannot lower interest rates further.
