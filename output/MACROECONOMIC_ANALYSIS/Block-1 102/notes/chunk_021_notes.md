## Section: Shocks in the IS-LM Model (3.4.1)

### Core Idea

The IS-LM model provides a framework for analyzing how **exogenous shocks** — both demand-side and monetary — affect equilibrium output and interest rates. IS curve shifts reflect real/demand shocks; LM curve shifts reflect monetary shocks. The effectiveness of monetary policy depends critically on the **slope of the LM curve**.

### Key Concepts

#### Demand Shock Example (IS Shift)

A **stock market boom** increases household wealth → higher consumption → aggregate expenditure rises → IS shifts rightward from IS₀ to IS₁ → output increases from Y₀ to Y₁ and interest rate rises. Conversely, a **decline in business confidence** reduces investment → IS shifts leftward → both output and interest rate fall.

#### Monetary Shock Example (LM Shift)

A rise in **financial fraud** discourages digital banking → demand for physical cash increases → money demand rises → LM shifts rightward from LM₀ to LM₁ → interest rate falls from i₀ to i₂ and output rises from Y₀ to Y₂.

#### Policy Effectiveness and LM Slope

- **Flatter LM** (high interest sensitivity h): Monetary policy is very effective — a small interest rate change produces large output changes
- **Steeper LM** (low interest sensitivity h): Monetary policy is less effective — large interest rate changes needed for small output changes

## Section: Adjustment Process in the Economy (3.4.2)

### Core Idea

When the economy is away from IS-LM equilibrium, an automatic adjustment process brings it back. The four quadrants created by the IS-LM intersection have distinct disequilibrium characteristics, and forces in both goods and money markets push the economy toward the equilibrium point E.

### Key Concepts

#### Four Disequilibrium Regions (Fig. 3.5)

| Region | Goods Market | Money Market | Adjustment |
|--------|-------------|--------------|------------|
| Above IS, Right of LM (G) | Excess supply | Excess money demand | i↑, Y↓ → toward E |
| Above IS, Left of LM (F) | Excess supply | Excess money supply | i↓, Y↓ → toward E |
| Below IS, Left of LM (H) | Excess demand | Excess money supply | i↓, Y↑ → toward E |
| Below IS, Right of LM (K) | Excess demand | Excess money demand | i↑, Y↑ → toward E |

#### Example: Point G

At point G (above IS, right of LM):
1. **Excess money demand**: People sell bonds → bond prices fall → interest rate rises (↑)
2. **Excess supply of goods**: Involuntary inventory accumulation → firms cut production → output falls (←)
3. Economy moves toward equilibrium point E

#### Interdependence of Markets

Both real and monetary sectors are **interrelated**:
- Real shock → affects monetary sector (changed income alters money demand)
- Monetary shock → affects real sector (changed interest rate alters investment)

## Section: Impact of Supply Shocks (3.4.3)

### Core Idea

In the IS-LM model, equilibrium need not occur at full employment. In the **long run**, prices adjust, shifting the LM curve. An adverse supply shock (e.g., drought) reduces potential output, causes prices to rise, and shifts the LM curve leftward, raising interest rates and reducing output.

### Key Concepts

#### Supply Shock Adjustment

IS is relatively stable during supply shocks (real quantities of demand don't change with prices). LM adjusts because it depends on **real balances** (M/P):
1. Adverse supply shock → potential output falls from Y* to Y**
2. Prices rise (scarcity)
3. Real money balances fall (M/P decreases)
4. LM shifts leftward (LM₀ → LM₁)
5. Interest rate rises; new equilibrium at F with lower output

### Connections

- The adjustment process demonstrates the **stability** of IS-LM equilibrium
- The supply shock analysis bridges toward the **AD-AS model** (Section 3.5) which explicitly incorporates price level changes
