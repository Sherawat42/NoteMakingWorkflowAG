## Section: Aggregate Supply Curve
<!-- See Chunk 005 for the labor market and production function derivation -->

### Core Idea

Since the classical model ensures full employment at all times, and the production function with a given level of technology and capital determines a unique level of output, the **Aggregate Supply (AS) curve is a vertical straight line** at the full employment output level. Changes in the price level do not affect output — they only affect nominal wages proportionally, leaving real wages, employment, and output unchanged. The AS curve shifts only when there is a change in the level of technology (the production function) or the level of capital stock.

### Key Concepts

#### Vertical AS Curve at Full Employment

The vertical AS curve is a direct implication of the classical labor market: with perfectly flexible wages, the real wage always adjusts to maintain full employment regardless of the price level. At full employment N₀, the production function yields Y₁ (full employment output). Whether the price level is P₁, 2P₁, or 3P₁, output remains at Y₁. This means **output is perfectly inelastic with respect to the price level** — only supply-side factors (technology, capital, labor force) can change Y₁.

### Connections

- Follows directly from the **labor market equilibrium and production function** in Section 1.4 (Chunk 005)
- Combined with the **Aggregate Demand curve** derived from the QTM (below), the vertical AS determines the equilibrium price level in the classical model (Fig. 1.4)

## Section: Quantity Theory of Money (1.5)
<!-- This section continues into Chunk 007 -->

### Core Idea

The Quantity Theory of Money (QTM) is the classical model's theory of the **price level**. It establishes a proportional relationship between the money supply and the price level, under the assumptions that velocity of circulation (V) and real output (Y) are constant. The QTM is fundamentally a theory about money market equilibrium: the total amount of money available for transactions (MV) must equal the total monetary value of transactions (PY). Since real output is determined by the labor market and production function (the real sector), and velocity is determined by institutional payment habits, changes in the money supply can only affect the price level.

### Key Concepts

#### Money as a Stock Variable

Money is a **stock variable** — its quantity is measured at a specific point in time, not as a flow over a period. Money is an asset demanded by the public for its holding and is supplied by the government and the banking system. The interaction of money demand and money supply creates the money market. In the classical model, the monetary authority **autonomously (exogenously) determines** the supply of money.

#### Money Demand in Classical Theory

Classical economists recognized four functions of money: (i) medium of exchange, (ii) standard of deferred payment, (iii) store of value, and (iv) unit of account. However, they held that money is demanded **primarily for its function as a medium of exchange** — people hold money to facilitate transactions, not as a store of wealth or for speculative purposes. This contrasts sharply with Keynes's theory, which added speculative demand for money (covered in Unit 2).

#### Fisher's Equation of Exchange (Transaction Version)

The transaction version of the QTM, known as Fisher's equation of exchange:

**MV = PT** ... (1.5)

where:
- **M** = quantity of money supply
- **V** = velocity of circulation of money (number of times a rupee changes hands during the period)
- **P** = average price level
- **T** = number of transactions of average size (proxy for national income)

The right-hand side (PT) represents the total amount of money required to facilitate all transactions in the economy. The left-hand side (MV) represents the total amount of money available for transactions during the period.

#### Income Version of QTM

An alternative formulation uses real output directly instead of number of transactions:

**MV = PY** ... (1.7)

where Y is real output and PY is nominal output (nominal GDP). The total money stock times velocity equals the nominal value of total output. This identity becomes the QTM under the assumptions that Y and V are stable or constant in the short run.

#### Proportionality of Money Supply and Price Level

With V and Y treated as constants, the QTM can be expressed as:

**P = (V̄ / Ȳ) × M** ... (1.8)

This shows that **P is directly proportional to M**. If M increases by four times, P rises by four times. The price level is "passive" — it depends on changes in M, not the other way around. This proportionality result holds only if all three assumptions are satisfied: (1) V is constant, (2) Y is at full employment, and (3) P responds passively to M. Relaxation of any one assumption breaks the proportionality.

#### Aggregate Demand Curve from QTM

The QTM gives rise to the classical **Aggregate Demand curve**. From MV = PY with M and V given, an increase in P must be accompanied by a decrease in Y for MV to remain constant — producing a downward-sloping AD curve. The intersection of this downward-sloping AD with the vertical AS determines the equilibrium price level at full employment output Ȳ (Fig. 1.4).

### Definitions

- **Velocity of Circulation (V)**: The number of times a unit of money (e.g., a rupee) changes hands during a given period. In classical theory, V is determined by institutional payment habits and is assumed constant in the short run.
- **Money Supply (M)**: The total stock of money in the economy, exogenously determined by the monetary authority (central bank). In the classical model, changes in M are the primary cause of changes in the price level.
- **Nominal Output (PY)**: The monetary value of total output, equal to the price level (P) multiplied by real output (Y). It represents the total value of goods and services produced at current prices.
- **Fisher's Equation of Exchange**: The identity MV = PT (or MV = PY), stating that the total amount of money times its velocity equals the total value of transactions. Named after economist Irving Fisher.

### Mechanisms / Processes

The classical mechanism for price determination:
1. **Labor market** clears → determines full employment N₀ and real wage (W/P)₀
2. **Production function** → N₀ produces full employment output Ȳ
3. **Velocity V** is constant (determined by payment habits)
4. **Money supply M** is exogenously given by the monetary authority
5. From MV = PȲ → **P = (V/Ȳ) × M** — price level is proportional to money supply
6. If M increases by 4× → P increases by 4× (proportionality)
7. Summary: **Real sector → Y; Money supply → P** — the classical dichotomy in action

### Examples

**Numerical illustration**: If the money supply M increases by four times (e.g., from 100 to 400), with V and Y constant, the price level P must also rise by four times (e.g., from 1 to 4). This demonstrates the purely proportional relationship.

**Figure 1.4: Equilibrium Output and Prices** — Shows the vertical AS at Ȳ and the downward-sloping AD₀ derived from the QTM. Their intersection at point E determines the equilibrium price level P. Any shift in AD (from changes in M) only changes P, not Y.

### Edge Cases & Caveats

- **Keynesian criticism**: Prices are sticky in the short run — they do not adjust instantaneously to changes in money supply. In recessions, increases in M may increase output rather than prices.
- **Monetarist criticism**: The velocity of money is **not constant** over time — it varies with financial innovation, interest rates, and payment technology. This weakens the strict proportionality result.
- Despite these criticisms, the QTM remains useful for understanding and controlling **inflation** — broadly, sustained money supply growth in excess of output growth leads to sustained price increases.

### Open Questions

1. If velocity is not constant (as empirical evidence shows), how reliable is the QTM for policy? Does it still serve as a useful approximation for long-run inflation dynamics?
2. Does the direction of causation always run from M to P? Or can price changes (e.g., from oil shocks) cause changes in money demand and hence in money supply?
