## Section: Equilibrium in the Monetary Sector (3.3)

### Core Idea

The LM curve represents all combinations of interest rate and output at which the **money market** is in equilibrium — where demand for real money balances equals the (fixed) real money supply. The LM curve is **upward-sloping**: higher income increases money demand for transactions, and with fixed money supply, the interest rate must rise to maintain money market equilibrium.

### Key Concepts

#### Money Demand Function

**L = kY − hi** ... (3.5)

where:
- L = demand for real balances
- k > 0 = income sensitivity (transactions + precautionary demand)
- h > 0 = interest sensitivity (speculative demand + opportunity cost)
- Y = aggregate income
- i = interest rate

Three key points about the monetary sector:
1. **Opportunity cost**: Holding money means forgoing interest income from bank deposits/bonds. Higher i → higher opportunity cost → less money demanded
2. **Income dependence**: Money demand curve is drawn for a given Y. Higher Y → more transactions → money demand curve shifts right
3. **Money supply**: Determined by the central bank; constant in the short run; represented as a vertical line Mˢ/P

#### LM Equation

Setting money demand equal to supply: M̄/P̄ = kY − hi ... (3.6)

Solving for Y:

**Y = (1/k)(M̄/P̄) + (h/k)i** ... (3.6a)

This shows a **positive relationship** between Y and i — the LM curve is upward-sloping.

#### LM Curve Slope

Slope (di/dY) = **k/h**

- **Flatter LM**: Large h (high interest sensitivity of money demand) or small k → money market adjusts easily to income changes with small interest rate movements
- **Steeper LM**: Small h (low interest sensitivity) or large k → income changes require large interest rate adjustments
- **Horizontal LM** (liquidity trap): h → ∞ (speculative demand perfectly elastic)
- **Vertical LM** (classical case): h → 0 (speculative demand perfectly inelastic)

### Section: Derivation of the LM Curve (3.3.1)

#### Graphical Derivation (Fig. 3.3)

**Panel (a)**: Money market diagram. At income Y₀, money demand Md/P(Y₀) intersects vertical money supply at point 'a', giving equilibrium interest rate i₀.

When income rises to Y₁:
1. Transactions demand increases → money demand shifts right to Md/P(Y₁)
2. With money supply unchanged → excess demand for money
3. People sell bonds → bond prices fall → interest rate rises to i₁
4. New equilibrium at point 'b'

**Panel (b)**: LM diagram. Plot 'a' (Y₀, i₀) and 'b' (Y₁, i₁). Connecting gives the **upward-sloping LM curve**.

#### Disequilibrium in the Money Market

- **Left of LM** (point 'c'): **Excess supply of money** → people buy bonds → bond prices rise → interest rate falls → returns to LM
- **Right of LM** (point 'd'): **Excess demand for money** → people sell bonds → bond prices fall → interest rate rises → returns to LM

### Section: Shift in the LM Curve (3.3.2)

- **Increase in money supply** → LM shifts **rightward**: at every Y, equilibrium i is lower
- **Decrease in money supply** → LM shifts **leftward**: at every Y, equilibrium i is higher

### Definitions

- **LM Curve**: The locus of all (i, Y) combinations at which the money market is in equilibrium (money demand = money supply). Upward-sloping.
- **Interest Sensitivity of Money Demand (h)**: How responsive money demand is to interest rate changes. Larger h → flatter LM curve → monetary policy less effective.
- **Income Sensitivity of Money Demand (k)**: How responsive money demand is to income changes. Larger k → steeper LM curve.

## Section: Simultaneous Equilibrium (3.4)

### Core Idea

When the IS and LM curves are combined in one diagram, their **unique intersection** determines the equilibrium interest rate (i₀) and output (Y₀) at which BOTH the goods market and the money market are simultaneously in equilibrium. This is the central result of the IS-LM model — it provides a complete short-run macroeconomic equilibrium.
