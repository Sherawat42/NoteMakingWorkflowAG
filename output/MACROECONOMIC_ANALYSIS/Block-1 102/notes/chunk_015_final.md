## Section: S = I Equilibrium — Three-Sector Extension (2.4.2 cont.)

### Core Idea

In a three-sector economy (households, firms, government), the equilibrium condition Y = C + I + G combined with Y = C + S + T yields:

**S + T = Ī + G** ... (2.14)

This can be rewritten as **S + (T − G) = Ī**, showing that planned investment must equal the sum of private saving (S) and public saving (T − G). Equivalently, **(S − I) = (G − T)**: if private saving exceeds investment, the government must be running a budget deficit, financed by borrowing from households.

## Section: Paradox of Thrift (2.4.3)

### Core Idea

The **paradox of thrift** is one of the most important and counterintuitive results in Keynesian economics. While saving is a virtue for an individual household (increasing wealth over time), increased saving by the economy as a whole can be a **vice** — it reduces aggregate demand, lowers equilibrium output, and may leave aggregate saving unchanged or even reduced. This paradox highlights the fallacy of composition in macroeconomics: what is good for the individual may be harmful for the economy.

### Key Concepts

#### Case 1: Autonomous Investment (S stays the same)

When the saving function shifts up from S₀ to S₁ (households desire to save more at every income level) but investment remains autonomous (I = Ī):
1. At the higher saving function, planned saving exceeds planned investment at the old income level
2. This means planned consumption has fallen → aggregate demand declines
3. Output falls from Y₀ to Y₁ (multiplier effect in reverse)
4. At the new equilibrium, saving = Ī (unchanged), but income is lower: OY₁ < OY₀
5. **Result**: Despite wanting to save more, **aggregate saving is the same** as before (S = Ī didn't change). Income simply fell until saving declined back to the original level.

#### Case 2: Induced Investment (S actually decreases)

When investment has an induced component (I = c + dY) and the saving function shifts up:
1. The reduction in consumption demand → lower output → lower induced investment
2. The decline in investment further reduces output and income
3. At the new equilibrium (OY₁ < OY₀): **planned saving is actually lower** than before (BY₁ < AY₀)
4. **Result**: The desire to save more leads to **lower aggregate saving** — an even more paradoxical outcome!

#### Short Run vs Long Run

This paradox is a **short-run phenomenon** applicable to economies with excess capacity and no supply constraints:
- **Short run**: Higher saving → lower consumption → lower AD → lower output → same or lower saving
- **Long run**: Higher saving → higher investment → creation of additional productive capacity → higher growth potential

### Definitions

- **Paradox of Thrift**: The Keynesian result that an economy-wide increase in the desire to save can lead to unchanged or even reduced aggregate saving, because the resulting decline in demand lowers equilibrium income.

**Figure 2.8: Paradox of Thrift (Autonomous Investment)** — S₀ shifts up to S₁ (parallel, same MPS). With horizontal Ī, the new equilibrium at point D gives lower income Y₁ < Y₀ but saving DY₁ = AY₀ = Ī. Aggregate saving is unchanged despite the increased desire to save.
