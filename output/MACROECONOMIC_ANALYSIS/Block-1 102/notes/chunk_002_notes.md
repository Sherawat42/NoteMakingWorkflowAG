## Section: Course Introduction

### Core Idea

Macroeconomics analyses and establishes functional relationships among economy-level aggregates. The course aims to develop both theoretical understanding (intuitive grasp of macroeconomic schools of thought) and practical application skills (analyzing real-world issues and applying theory to empirical data). Aggregative analysis has assumed great significance in recent times, making a prior understanding of macroeconomic theory essential for proper comprehension of economic issues and policies.

### Key Concepts

#### Course Learning Objectives

After completing MEC-102, students are expected to:
1. Have an **intuitive understanding** of the theoretical developments in macroeconomics
2. **Contrast** among various schools of macroeconomic thought (classical, Keynesian, new-classical, etc.)
3. **Analyse** real-world issues with the help of macroeconomic theories
4. **Apply** macroeconomic theory to empirical data

#### Block 1 — Traditional Approaches Overview

Block 1 is "somewhat introductory in nature." It begins with basic macroeconomic concepts that are used frequently throughout the discipline and form the building blocks of the subject. It presents the classical and Keynesian views on determination of output and employment, then synthesizes these through IS-LM curves. The block also addresses open economy macroeconomics, examining how international trade and capital flows affect domestic policy outcomes.

#### Block 2 — Expectations Overview

Block 2 deals with the relationship between inflation and unemployment, tracing the evolution of the Phillips Curve from its origins as a relationship between real and monetary variables, through its prime position in Keynesian economics, to its collapse in new-classical economics. The block covers two critical concepts: **adaptive expectations** (backward-looking) and **rational expectations** (forward-looking, using all available information).

#### Block 3 — Intertemporal Decision-Making Overview

Block 3 analyses the behavior of economic agents (households and firms) in an intertemporal framework. It covers the **Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model** of optimal growth and the **Overlapping Generations (OLG) Model**, as well as household decisions about work versus leisure over time.

#### Block 4 — Business Cycles Overview

Block 4 is devoted to the analysis of economic fluctuations, particularly business cycles. It begins with traditional theories and progresses to **Real Business Cycle (RBC) models** with intertemporal substitution and propagation mechanisms that explain how small shocks can generate persistent fluctuations.

#### Block 5 — Labour Markets Overview

Block 5 discusses unemployment through two lenses: **nominal and real rigidities** (why wages and prices don't adjust instantaneously) and **search theories** (why matching workers to jobs takes time and resources, generating equilibrium unemployment).

#### Block 6 — Monetary Policy Overview

Block 6 covers the mechanics and theory of monetary policy, including central bank operations and money supply creation, conduct of monetary policy, and the theory behind policy design. Important contemporary concerns include **inflation targeting**, **policy rules**, and **dynamic consistency** (the problem of time-inconsistent optimal plans).

#### Block 7 — Fiscal Policy Overview

Block 7 examines fiscal policy implications in the economy, focusing on two important issues: **debt sustainability** (whether government debt-to-GDP ratios can be maintained without disruptive adjustment) and **Ricardian equivalence** (the proposition that the method of financing government spending — taxes vs. debt — may be irrelevant under certain conditions).

### Mechanisms / Processes

The course follows a deliberate progression:
1. **Classical foundations** (supply-side, long-run, flexible prices) → establishes the benchmark
2. **Keynesian departure** (demand-side, short-run, sticky prices) → challenges the benchmark
3. **Neoclassical synthesis** (IS-LM) → integrates both approaches
4. **Open economy** → adds international trade and capital mobility
5. **Expectations** → enriches models with forward-looking behavior
6. **Microfoundations** → provides individual-level basis for aggregate phenomena
7. **Policy analysis** → applies theory to monetary and fiscal policy design
