## Section: Investment Multiplier — Graphical & Induced Investment (2.3.2 cont.)
<!-- See Chunk 012 for the core multiplier derivation -->

### Core Idea

Figure 2.5 shows the multiplier graphically: when autonomous investment rises from I₀ to I₁, AD shifts upward by ΔI (= I₁ − I₀), but the equilibrium income change ΔY is larger (ΔY > ΔI), demonstrating the multiplier effect. With **induced investment** (I = c + dY), the multiplier becomes 1/[1−(b+d)], which is larger than the pure consumption multiplier because investment leakage is reduced.

### Key Concepts

#### Induced Investment Multiplier

With I = c + dY (where d = MPI), the equilibrium becomes:

**Yₑ = (a + c) / [1 − (b + d)]** ... (2.7)

The multiplier = **1/[1 − (b + d)]**, which is larger than 1/(1−b) because the denominator is smaller. This reflects the additional spending through the investment channel. **Assumption**: (b + d) < 1 — the sum of MPC and MPI must be less than one for stability.

## Section: Government Expenditure and Tax Multipliers (2.3.3)

### Core Idea

In a three-sector economy, government spending (G) and taxation (T) introduce both additional demand injections and leakages. The government expenditure multiplier equals 1/(1−b) for lump-sum taxes. The tax multiplier is −b/(1−b) — negative because tax increases reduce disposable income and demand. The balanced budget multiplier is exactly **1**: equal increases in G and T raise income by exactly G. With proportional taxes (T = tY), the multiplier shrinks because income taxes represent automatic stabilizers.

### Key Concepts

#### Lump Sum Tax Case

With lump-sum tax T, equilibrium: **Yₑ = (a − bT + Ī + G) / (1 − b)** ... (from 2.4)

- **Government expenditure multiplier**: ΔY/ΔG = **1/(1−b)** — same as the investment multiplier
- **Tax multiplier**: ΔY/ΔT = **−b/(1−b)** — negative; tax increase reduces income

The tax multiplier is smaller in absolute value than the spending multiplier because a ₹1 tax increase reduces consumption by only b (since MPC < 1), whereas a ₹1 spending increase adds directly to demand. Taxes are a leakage: higher taxes → lower disposable income → lower consumption → lower output.

#### Balanced Budget Multiplier

If the government increases spending by ΔG and raises taxes by the same amount (ΔT = ΔG), the budget balance is unchanged (ΔB = ΔG − ΔT = 0). The multiplier:

ΔY = (−bΔT + ΔG)/(1−b) = (1−b)ΔG/(1−b) = ΔG

**ΔY/ΔG = 1** — the **balanced budget multiplier is exactly one**. Income increases by exactly the amount of government spending increase, even though the spending is fully financed by taxes. This works because the spending adds directly to demand (multiplier effect = 1/(1−b)), while the tax reduces demand through consumption (multiplier effect = b/(1−b)), and the net is exactly 1.

#### Proportional Taxes

With proportional tax rate t (0 < t < 1), tax revenue T = tY:

AD = a + b(1−t)Y + Ī + G

**Yₑ = (a + Ī + G) / {1 − b(1−t)}** ... (2.8)

The multiplier becomes: **ΔY/ΔG = 1/{1 − b(1−t)}**

This is **smaller** than 1/(1−b) because taxes take a fraction 't' of each increment to income, leaving less for consumption spending in each round. The proportional tax acts as an **automatic stabilizer** — it reduces the multiplier, dampening both booms and recessions.

#### Tax Cuts and Economic Revival

A decrease in the tax rate raises equilibrium income by increasing the multiplier — the same autonomous spending now generates a larger cumulative output effect. This is the logic behind tax cuts as a tool for reviving economic activity. **However**, this works only under the assumption of excess production capacity. Without excess capacity, increased demand leads to **price increases rather than output increases**.

### Definitions

- **Government Expenditure Multiplier**: ΔY/ΔG = 1/(1−b) — the multiple by which income rises when G increases by one unit.
- **Tax Multiplier**: ΔY/ΔT = −b/(1−b) — the (negative) multiple by which income changes when lump-sum taxes change.
- **Balanced Budget Multiplier**: Equal to 1 — when government spending and taxes increase by the same amount, income rises by that amount.
- **Budget Deficit (B)**: B = G − T − TR, where TR is transfer payments. When G > T + TR, the government runs a deficit.

## Section: Open Economy Multiplier (2.3.4)

### Core Idea

In an open economy, exports (X) are an additional autonomous injection, while imports (M) are a leakage that depends on domestic income. The open economy multiplier **1/(1−b+m)** is **smaller** than the closed economy multiplier because a fraction 'm' (marginal propensity to import) of each income increment leaks abroad through import spending.

### Key Concepts

#### Open Economy AD and Equilibrium

AD = C + I + G + X − M = a + bY + Ī + G + X − h − mY ... (2.9)

where M = h + mY (h = autonomous imports, m = MPM).

**Yₑ = (a − h + Ī + G + X) / (1 − b + m)** ... (2.10)

#### Open Economy Multiplier

**ΔY/ΔG = ΔY/ΔI = ΔY/ΔX = 1/(1 − b + m)**

Since m > 0, the denominator (1 − b + m) > (1 − b), so the multiplier is smaller. The import leakage means that part of each round's spending goes to foreign goods rather than stimulating domestic production.

#### General Multiplier Principles

From the analysis of all multiplier types:
1. The multiplier is **larger** when MPC (b) and MPI (d) are higher, and the tax rate (t) and MPM (m) are lower
2. The multiplier works **symmetrically** for both increases and decreases in autonomous spending
3. The multiplier mechanism requires **excess capacity and unemployed resources** — it is driven by the assumption that output is demand-determined

### Definitions

- **Marginal Propensity to Import (MPM)**: m = ΔM/ΔY — the fraction of additional income spent on imported goods.
- **Open Economy Multiplier**: 1/(1−b+m) — smaller than the closed economy multiplier due to import leakage.
