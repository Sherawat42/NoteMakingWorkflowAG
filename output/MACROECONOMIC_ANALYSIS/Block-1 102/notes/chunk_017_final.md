## Section: Liquidity Trap — Continued (2.5.2)
<!-- See Chunk 016 for the beginning of this section -->

### Core Idea

When interest rates hit a floor at i*, any additional liquidity injected into the system is simply absorbed as people willingly hold money rather than bonds. Monetary policy **cannot lower the interest rate** below i* and hence cannot affect real variables. This makes fiscal policy the only effective tool.

### Key Concepts

#### Real-World Relevance of the Liquidity Trap

The concept became practically important during the **global financial crisis of 2007-08**, when interest rates in the USA hit zero and in Japan approached zero. In this environment:
- Conventional monetary policy (lowering interest rates via open market operations) could not reduce government bond rates any further
- **Unconventional monetary policy** measures had to be explored (quantitative easing, forward guidance)
- **Fiscal policy** (increases in government spending) was employed to boost economic activity, but this resulted in widening budget deficits and high public debt levels
- High interest burden on public debt raised concerns about **debt sustainability**

The **COVID-19 crisis of 2020-21** again demonstrated the relevance of Keynesian policies: acute shortages in aggregate demand due to decreased economic activity prompted massive government intervention worldwide.

## Section: Role of Government in the Economy (2.6)

### Core Idea

The Keynesian view on the role of government stands in **sharp contrast** to the classical laissez-faire position. Keynes argued that the government must play an **active role** in managing the economy because rigidities in prices and wages prevent smooth market adjustment. When lack of effective demand causes recession, the government should intervene by increasing public expenditure and running a deficit budget. Fiscal and monetary policies inject additional demand into the economy, enhancing spending, output, income, and employment.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical vs Keynesian Views on Government

| Feature | Classical | Keynesian |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Government role | Minimal (law, order, defense) | Active demand management |
| Market mechanism | Self-adjusting | Fails in short run due to rigidities |
| Focus | Supply side (production) | Demand side (planned spending) |
| Unemployment | Impossible (flexible wages) | Normal (sticky wages) |
| Policy tools | None needed | Fiscal and monetary policy |

#### Keynesian Policy Prescription

When an economy faces recession due to inadequate effective demand:
1. Unemployment creates a tendency for wage rates to decline
2. Wage decline **worsens** the situation by reducing purchasing power and effective demand (a vicious cycle)
3. Government should **increase public expenditure** and run a deficit budget
4. Fiscal and monetary policies inject additional demand
5. The multiplier effect amplifies the initial spending increase, raising output, income, and employment
6. The economy emerges from recession

#### Effectiveness of Fiscal Policy

- During the **Great Depression (1929-33)**: Keynesian fiscal policies were successfully used to revive economies, establishing the case for activist government
- Keynesian economists preferred **discretionary fiscal policy** over monetary policy, especially since Keynes pointed out the limitations of monetary policy (liquidity trap)
- In a liquidity trap, conventional monetary policy is completely ineffective, but fiscal policy remains effective because government spending directly adds to aggregate demand

#### Policy Concerns and Limitations

Despite the effectiveness of fiscal policy, there are important concerns:
1. **Widening budget deficits**: Expansionary fiscal policy increases government borrowing
2. **Rising public debt**: High debt levels raise questions about sustainability
3. **Crowding out**: High government borrowing can push up interest rates, potentially displacing private investment — partially offsetting the expansionary effect

## Section: Unit 2 Summary — Let Us Sum Up (2.7)

### Core Idea

The Keynesian model is appropriate for an economy facing recessionary conditions with unemployed resources and lack of effective demand. Key results:
1. Markets are imperfect with wage and price rigidity → output is demand-determined
2. Autonomous spending changes have **multiplier effects** through successive rounds of induced spending
3. The **paradox of thrift**: increased desire to save can leave aggregate saving unchanged or lower
4. Money demand (**liquidity preference**) increases with income and decreases with interest rate
5. The **speculative demand** for money creates the inverse interest-money demand relationship
6. In a **liquidity trap**, monetary policy is ineffective; fiscal policy remains effective
7. Government has an active, essential role in managing aggregate demand
