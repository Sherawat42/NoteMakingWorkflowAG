## Section: Neutrality of Money — Continued (1.7.2)
<!-- See Chunk 007 for the beginning of this section -->

### Core Idea

Completing the neutrality of money analysis: when money supply expands, nominal wages rise proportionally to the price increase, restoring the original real wage. Employment (NF) and real output (YF) remain unchanged. The only variables that change are the nominal ones — price level and money wages increase proportionally to the money supply expansion.

### Key Concepts

#### Limitation of Neutrality of Money

The neutrality of money is a result derived from the **full-employment equilibrium** based on **full flexibility of prices**. However, the text explicitly acknowledges a crucial limitation: if money supply increases (causing higher prices) truly had no real effects, then **inflation would not be a serious concern**. But inflation IS a serious problem — it causes decreased quality of life, reduced economic growth, and other adverse real effects. Therefore, measures are taken to control inflation and maintain price stability. This reveals that the strict neutrality result breaks down in practice.

### Edge Cases & Caveats

- The neutrality result is an idealization. In reality, inflation imposes real costs even if all nominal variables adjusted perfectly (which they don't): **menu costs** (cost of changing posted prices), **shoe-leather costs** (costs of economizing on money holdings), and **distributional effects** (inflation transfers wealth from creditors to debtors, from fixed-income earners to variable-income earners).
- The text itself highlights this tension: "If increase in money supply had no real effects, then inflation would not have been a serious concern in an economy."

## Section: Saving-Investment Equilibrium (1.7.3)

### Core Idea

In the classical framework, money is demanded purely as a **medium of exchange** for transaction purposes. Since money demand is not related to interest rates, changes in money supply do not affect the **real rate of interest**. Consequently, the equilibrium levels of saving and investment remain unchanged when money supply changes. The capital market equilibrium — where saving equals investment at the market-clearing real interest rate — is undisturbed by monetary changes.

### Key Concepts

#### Capital Market Equilibrium

In the classical model, the real interest rate is determined in the capital market (also called the loanable funds market) where the supply of saving equals the demand for investment. Saving is an increasing function of the real interest rate (higher rates encourage more saving), and investment is a decreasing function (higher rates increase the cost of borrowing, discouraging investment). Their intersection determines the equilibrium real interest rate.

#### Neutrality in Capital Markets

When money supply increases and prices rise, nominal investment expenditure increases. However, this increase is **exactly proportional to the price increase**, so real investment is unchanged. Graphically:
- The nominal saving supply curve shifts rightward from SS to S′S′
- The investment demand curve shifts rightward from II to I′I′ by the **same amount**
- The intersection of S′S′ and I′I′ determines the same real interest rate
- Real values of saving and investment remain identical at the higher price level

### Mechanisms / Processes

1. **Money supply increases** → prices rise (from QTM)
2. **Nominal investment expenditure rises** proportionally to the price increase
3. **Nominal saving** also increases proportionally (households earn higher nominal incomes)
4. Saving supply curve shifts right: SS → S′S′
5. Investment demand curve shifts right: II → I′I′ (by the same amount)
6. **New intersection**: same real interest rate, same real saving and investment
7. **Result**: Capital market equilibrium is completely undisturbed by monetary expansion

### Examples

**Figure 1.6: Capital Market Equilibrium** — Real interest rate on the y-axis; nominal saving-investment on the x-axis. Original equilibrium at intersection of SS and II. After money supply increase, both curves shift rightward by equal amounts to S′S′ and I′I′, maintaining the same real interest rate and real saving-investment levels.

## Section: Long Run vs Short Run (1.8)

### Core Idea

The classical model with its assumptions of flexible prices and market clearing is most applicable to the **long run**. In the **short run**, most prices are "sticky" at predetermined levels — they do not adjust instantaneously to changes in supply or demand. This difference in price behavior means that economic events and policies have different effects across time horizons. The acknowledgment that short-run behavior differs from long-run behavior motivates the transition to the **Keynesian model** (Unit 2), which provides the analytical framework for short-run analysis.

### Key Concepts

#### Price Behavior Differences

- **Long run**: Prices are **fully flexible**. They adjust freely in response to changes in supply and demand, ensuring market clearing and full employment.
- **Short run**: Most prices are **sticky** at pre-determined levels. Firms set prices and don't change them immediately in response to every shift in demand. Wages are set by contracts and social norms, not continuously renegotiated.

This distinction is fundamental to modern macroeconomics — it reconciles the Classical and Keynesian views by assigning them to different time horizons.

#### Long-Run AS Curve (ASLR)

The long-run aggregate supply curve is a **vertical line** at potential output Y* (full-employment output). This represents the economy's maximum sustainable production capacity, determined by available technology, capital, and labor. In the long run, the economy always returns to Y* regardless of demand conditions.

#### Short-Run AS Curve

The short-run aggregate supply curve is **upward sloping** (AS₀ in Fig. 1.7). This reflects the fact that when demand increases, firms can temporarily produce more by running existing capacity harder, hiring more workers at existing nominal wages (which are sticky), and accepting higher prices. Output can thus deviate from Y* in the short run.

#### Short-Run Effects of AD and AS Shifts

The text describes two scenarios in the short-run AS-AD model:
1. **Supply-side expansion** (AS₀ → AS₁): Output rises (Y₀ → Y₁) and prices **fall** (P₀ → P₁) — the economy benefits from increased productive capacity
2. **Demand-side expansion** (AD₀ → AD₁): Output rises (Y₀ → Y₂) but prices **rise** (P₀ → P₂) — more demand pushes up both quantity and price when AS is upward-sloping

#### Money Supply Effects: Short Run vs Long Run

- **Short run**: An increase in money supply shifts AD rightward → **both prices and income increase** (since AS is upward-sloping). A decrease in money supply shifts AD leftward → both prices and income decrease.
- **Long run**: An increase in money supply shifts AD rightward → **only prices increase** (since AS is vertical at Y*). Classical neutrality holds.
- The text explicitly states: "According to the classical theory, an increase in money supply leads to increased prices without affecting the real output. **This may be applicable in the long run, not in the short run.**"

### Definitions

- **Long-Run Aggregate Supply (ASLR)**: The vertical supply curve at potential output Y*, reflecting the total productive capacity of the economy. In the long run, all prices have adjusted and the economy operates at full employment.
- **Short-Run Aggregate Supply**: The upward-sloping supply curve reflecting the fact that prices are partially sticky in the short run, allowing output to deviate temporarily from full employment.
- **Potential Output (Y*)**: The level of output produced when all resources are fully and efficiently employed — the economy's sustainable maximum. Also called full-employment output or natural level of output.

### Open Questions

1. **How long is the "long run"?** When do sticky prices become flexible? Keynes famously quipped: "In the long run we are all dead" — suggesting that waiting for prices to adjust may not be practical policy.
2. If the short-run AS is upward-sloping, can the government exploit this to increase output by boosting demand? This is precisely the Keynesian policy prescription.
