## Section: Determination of Output and Employment (1.4)
<!-- This section continues into Chunk 006 (Aggregate Supply Curve) -->

### Core Idea

In the classical model, output and employment are determined entirely by supply-side factors operating through competitive labor and product markets. Profit-maximizing firms equate the marginal product of labor to the real wage, generating a downward-sloping labor demand curve. Utility-maximizing workers supply more labor at higher real wages, generating an upward-sloping labor supply curve. The intersection determines full employment labor (N₀) and the equilibrium real wage (W/P)₀. This labor quantity, fed into the production function Y = F(K,N), determines the full employment output Y₀.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical Assumptions for Output Determination

The classical theory of output determination rests on three foundational assumptions:
1. **Firms and workers are optimizers** — firms maximize profit, workers maximize utility
2. **Perfect knowledge** — all agents have complete information about prices, wages, and market conditions
3. **Competitive (perfect) markets** — no individual agent can influence market prices

An implication of these assumptions is that wages and prices are fully flexible, adjusting instantaneously to clear markets.

#### Labour Demand — Marginal Product of Labour

Under perfect competition, the profit-maximizing condition for firms is:

**P = W / MPN** ... (1.1)

where P is the product price (equal to marginal revenue under perfect competition), W is the nominal wage, and MPN is the marginal product of labor. The term W/MPN represents the marginal cost of production — the cost of producing one additional unit of output. Rearranging gives:

**MPN = W/P** ... (1.2)

This states that the marginal product of labor equals the real wage. Therefore, the **labor demand curve in terms of the real wage is the marginal product of labor curve** — it slopes downward because of diminishing marginal returns to labor (adding more workers to a fixed capital stock yields progressively smaller increments to output).

Formally: **Nᵈ = f(W/P)** ... (1.3) — a decreasing function.

#### Labour Supply

The supply of labor varies **positively** with the real wage, based on utility maximization by individual workers (choosing between work and leisure):

**Nˢ = g(W/P)** ... (1.4)

At higher real wages, the opportunity cost of leisure rises, so workers supply more labor. The labor supply curve is upward-sloping in real wage–employment space.

#### Labour Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium in the labor market occurs where labor demand equals labor supply:

**Nᵈ = Nˢ at real wage (W/P)₀**

At this point, N₀ workers are employed — this is **full employment**. There is no involuntary unemployment in the classical model because any excess supply of labor would drive wages down until all willing workers are employed.

#### Nominal vs Real Wage Adjustment

When the price level increases (from P₁ to 2P₁ to 3P₁), the real wage can be maintained at the same level if the nominal wage adjusts proportionally:

- At P₁: nominal wage = W₁ → real wage = W₁/P₁
- At 2P₁: nominal wage = 2W₁ → real wage = 2W₁/2P₁ = W₁/P₁ (unchanged)
- At 3P₁: nominal wage = 3W₁ → real wage = 3W₁/3P₁ = W₁/P₁ (unchanged)

In the nominal wage panel, both the labor supply and demand curves shift outward proportionally with price level changes, but the equilibrium employment level remains at N₀. If prices rise but nominal wages do not adjust, real wages fall, leading to a decrease in labor supply — but under classical assumptions, nominal wages always adjust.

#### Production Function and Full Employment Output

The classical model uses a production function with two factors — labor and capital:

**Y = F(K, N)**

where K is the capital stock (assumed fixed in the short run) and N is labor. With the equilibrium labor supply N₀ determined by the labor market, the production function determines the full employment output:

**Y₀ = F(K, N₀)**

The economy always operates at this full employment output level. Since K is fixed in the short run, only changes in technology or capital stock can shift the production function and change Y₀.

### Definitions

- **Marginal Product of Labour (MPN)**: The additional output produced by employing one more unit of labor, holding capital constant. Under diminishing marginal returns, MPN declines as more labor is employed.
- **Real Wage (W/P)**: The nominal wage divided by the price level. It represents the purchasing power of wages — the quantity of goods a worker can buy with their wage. The real wage is what matters for both labor demand and labor supply decisions.
- **Nominal Wage (W)**: The wage expressed in monetary terms (e.g., rupees per hour). Distinct from the real wage, which adjusts for the price level.
- **Full Employment Output (Y₀)**: The level of output produced when the labor market is in equilibrium and all willing workers are employed at the market-clearing real wage. In the classical model, this is the economy's natural level of output.
- **Production Function Y = F(K,N)**: The technological relationship between inputs (capital K, labor N) and output (Y). It exhibits diminishing marginal returns to each factor — as more of one factor is added (holding the other constant), the incremental output diminishes.

### Mechanisms / Processes

The classical determination of output and employment follows a clear causal chain:
1. **Firms maximize profit**: Set MPN = W/P → generates downward-sloping labor demand
2. **Workers maximize utility**: Choose labor-leisure trade-off → generates upward-sloping labor supply
3. **Labor market clears**: Nᵈ = Nˢ at equilibrium real wage (W/P)₀
4. **Full employment determined**: N₀ workers employed
5. **Output determined**: Plug N₀ into Y = F(K,N) → Y₀ (full employment output)
6. **Price level changes**: Nominal wages adjust proportionally → real wage unchanged → employment unchanged → output unchanged
7. **Conclusion**: Real output is determined entirely by real factors (technology, capital, labor willing to work), not by monetary variables

### Edge Cases & Caveats

- If prices rise but nominal wages do **not** adjust (the Keynesian scenario), the real wage falls, workers withdraw labor, and employment and output decline. The entire classical result depends on the assumption of instantaneous nominal wage flexibility.
- The production function assumes diminishing returns to labor, which means that beyond some point, additional workers add very little to output — even at full employment.
