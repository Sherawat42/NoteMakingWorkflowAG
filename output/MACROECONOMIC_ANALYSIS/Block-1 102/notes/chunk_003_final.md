## Section: Unit 1 Structure & Objectives (1.0–1.1)

### Core Idea

Unit 1 presents the Classical Approach to macroeconomics, covering the foundational ideas that dominated economic thinking from the 18th century through the early 20th century. The unit traces classical theory's core assumptions, its approach to output and employment determination, the quantity theory of money, Say's Law, the classical dichotomy, neutrality of money, and the distinction between long-run and short-run price behavior.

### Definitions

- **Macroeconomic Theory**: A set of ideas and principles used to explain, analyse, and predict macroeconomic phenomena — events that are aggregative in nature, dealing with economy-wide variables like total output, employment, and the general price level.
- **Fallacy of Composition**: The principle that what holds true for individual units may not hold true for the economy as a whole. A single firm can hire labor at the going wage rate without affecting it, but if all firms simultaneously increase labor demand, wage rates will rise. This fundamental insight motivates the need for macroeconomics as a distinct discipline from microeconomics.

### Examples

**Example: Fallacy of Composition in the Labor Market**
A single cement firm can hire as many workers as it needs at the ongoing wage rate because its individual demand is negligibly small relative to the total labor market. However, if all firms in the country increase their labor demand simultaneously (say, due to an economic boom), there will be a shortage of labor and wage rates will rise. Moreover, the total supply of workers is limited — beyond this limit, higher wages attract no additional labor supply.

**Example: Global Energy Crisis of 2022**
The global energy crisis caused shortages and price increases in oil, gas, and electricity worldwide. It resulted from multiple factors: labor shortages, political disputes, climate concerns, and the Russia-Ukraine war. Oil-exporting countries experienced increased income inflows, while petroleum-importing countries faced rising production costs and severe inflation. Policy makers responded with interest rate increases and money supply reduction — demonstrating how macroeconomic events require coordinated policy responses guided by macroeconomic theory.

### Core Idea

Macroeconomic thought has evolved through distinct schools, each responding to real-world economic events. The two major schools — Classical and Keynesian — interpret economic data differently, offer different explanations for the same phenomena, and prescribe different policy responses. This disagreement, especially since the 1930s, arises from differing fundamental assumptions about how markets function, particularly regarding price and wage flexibility.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical Economics

Classical economics, prevalent during the 18th and early 19th centuries, held that the levels of economic variables are determined through **market forces** — supply and demand. Wages, prices, and interest rates are assumed to be **fully flexible**, adjusting automatically to ensure market equilibrium. Under these assumptions, the economy operates at its **full capacity** or **full employment** level with no deficiency of aggregate demand. The classical school attempted to explain and analyse the remarkable increase in wealth during the Industrial Revolution. Prominent classical economists include Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, Anne Robert Jacques Turgot, John Stuart Mill, Jean-Baptiste Say, and Eugen Böhm von Bawerk.

#### Keynesian Economics

Keynesian economics emerged as a direct response to the Great Depression. John Maynard Keynes, a British economist, rejected the classical assumption of flexible wages and prices, arguing instead that they are **sticky** (rigid, slow to adjust). Keynes coined the term "classical" to describe the ideas of economists who preceded him. Keynesian theory holds that actual output can fall below potential output, and this gap can be closed through **fiscal and monetary policy interventions**. The government should actively intervene in markets to counter recessionary conditions rather than relying on automatic market adjustment.

#### Great Depression (1929-1933)

The Great Depression was a catastrophic economic event that witnessed massive declines in income, output, employment, and the price level, accompanied by a stock market crash and banking panic. It affected all major advanced countries, with approximately one-fourth of the labor force becoming unemployed. This event fundamentally **falsified classical assumptions** about automatic market adjustment and full employment equilibrium, triggering a search for new explanations of periodic output and employment fluctuations. Keynes argued that this massive unemployment was caused by **inadequate aggregate demand**.

### Definitions

- **School of Macroeconomic Thought**: A group of macroeconomic thinkers who share similar views on the behavior of macroeconomic variables in an economy. Different schools arise because economists make different fundamental assumptions about market behavior.
- **Classical Economics**: The school of economic thought from the 18th-19th century that believed economic variables are determined by market forces, with fully flexible wages, prices, and interest rates ensuring automatic adjustment to full employment.
- **Keynesian Economics**: The school of thought founded by John Maynard Keynes, emphasizing sticky wages and prices, the possibility of persistent unemployment, the role of aggregate demand in determining output, and the necessity of government intervention during recessions.
- **Laissez-faire**: The policy philosophy of minimal state intervention in economic variables such as wages, prices, and interest rates, with market forces expected to naturally ensure equilibrium. Central to classical economics.

### Mechanisms / Processes

The evolution of macroeconomic schools followed a pattern driven by real-world events:
1. **Classical economics** (18th-19th century) — market forces ensure equilibrium; laissez-faire
2. **Great Depression** (1929-33) — classical theory fails to explain massive unemployment
3. **Keynesian economics** (1936 onwards) — government intervention to manage aggregate demand
4. **Stagflation of the 1970s** — Keynesian policies challenged by simultaneous inflation and unemployment
5. **New Classical Economics** — revival of classical views with rational expectations and microfoundations
6. **New Keynesian Economics** — response to New Classical, adding microfoundations and rational expectations to Keynesian framework while retaining sticky prices

### Edge Cases & Caveats

- The classical assumption of fully flexible wages and prices is described as "unrealistic" by the text itself — all governments maintain monetary and fiscal policies and amend them regularly, contrary to the laissez-faire ideal.
- In microeconomics there is "more or less some consensus," but in macroeconomics there are "sharp and often altogether different explanations and policy recommendations for the same phenomenon."
