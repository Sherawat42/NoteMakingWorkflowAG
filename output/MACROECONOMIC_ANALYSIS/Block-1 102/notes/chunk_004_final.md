## Section: Various Schools of Macroeconomic Thought — Continued (1.2)
<!-- See Chunk 003 for the beginning of this section -->

### Core Idea

The evolution of macroeconomic thought continued beyond the initial Classical-Keynesian divide. Attempts to integrate both schools produced the neoclassical synthesis (IS-LM model). Post-Keynesians extended Keynesian ideas to the long run. The stagflation of the 1970s then triggered a revival of classical ideas through New Classical Economics, which in turn prompted New Keynesian responses. Today, New Classical and New Keynesian are the two dominant mainstream schools, sharing common features (general equilibrium, microfoundations, rational expectations) but differing fundamentally on market structure assumptions and wage/price flexibility.

### Key Concepts

#### Neoclassical Synthesis

The neoclassical synthesis represents an attempt to integrate ideas from both the Classical and Keynesian schools. The IS-LM model (covered in Unit 3) is its primary analytical tool, combining the goods market (Keynesian insight about aggregate demand) with the money market (interest rate determination) within a unified framework. This synthesis sought to show that Classical and Keynesian economics could be reconciled — with the Classical model describing long-run behavior and the Keynesian model capturing short-run dynamics.

#### Post-Keynesian Economics

During the 1950s, economists such as **Harrod, Domar, and Kaldor** extended Keynesian ideas from the short-run to the long-run. They argued that large investments would increase production capacity over time, boosting economic growth. Post-Keynesian economists focused on understanding, explaining, and analyzing economic growth. Robert M. Solow later proposed the neoclassical growth model to overcome certain limitations of the Post-Keynesian growth models. These growth models are covered in the course "Economics of Growth and Development."

#### Stagflation of the 1970s

During the 1970s, most advanced economies experienced simultaneous **stagnation** (low or negative growth, high unemployment) and **inflation** (rising prices) — a combination called "stagflation." This was a devastating blow to Keynesian theory, which posited a trade-off between unemployment and inflation (the Phillips Curve). Keynesian frameworks had no appropriate policy response to stagflation: expansionary policy would worsen inflation, while contractionary policy would worsen unemployment. This failure led to the exploration of fresh explanations of economic behavior.

#### New Classical Economics

Robert E. Lucas, an American economist, revived classical ideas and countered the prevailing Keynesian thought. New Classical Economics retained the basic characteristics of classical economics — flexible wages and prices, market clearing, and supply-side focus — but introduced two crucial new concepts:
1. **Rational Expectations**: Economic agents use all available information optimally to form expectations about the future, not just past data
2. **Microfoundations**: Macroeconomic behavior is explicitly grounded in the optimizing behavior of individual agents (households maximizing utility, firms maximizing profits)

#### New Keynesian Economics

New Keynesian economics arose as a response to the New Classical school. It shares several features with New Classical economics — general equilibrium models, microfoundations, and rational expectations — but differs on two fundamental assumptions:
1. **Market Structure**: New Classical assumes perfect competition; New Keynesian assumes **market imperfection** (monopolistic competition, information asymmetries)
2. **Price/Wage Flexibility**: New Classical assumes full flexibility; New Keynesian assumes **rigidities** in wages and prices (menu costs, staggered contracts, efficiency wages)

#### Heterodox Economics

Beyond the mainstream, heterodox schools of thought include Marxist, Austrian, institutional, feminist, and social economics, among others. The text notes a growing need for promoting **pluralism in economics**, with researchers venturing into many new areas. However, the mainstream or conventional economics has been Classical and Keynesian, and the legacy of these two schools continues in the forms of New Classical and New Keynesian thought.

### Definitions

- **Stagflation**: A macroeconomic condition of simultaneous stagnation (low growth, high unemployment) and inflation (rising prices). First prominently observed in the 1970s, it contradicted the Keynesian Phillips Curve prediction of a stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
- **Rational Expectations**: The assumption that economic agents form expectations about future economic variables by optimally using all available information. Unlike adaptive expectations (which are backward-looking), rational expectations incorporate knowledge of how the economy actually works.
- **Microfoundations**: The practice of grounding macroeconomic models in explicit models of individual agent behavior — firms maximizing profits, households maximizing utility — rather than assuming aggregate relationships directly. Both New Classical and New Keynesian schools share this commitment.
- **Neoclassical Synthesis**: The integration of Classical and Keynesian ideas, primarily through the IS-LM model, which combines goods market and money market equilibrium in a single framework.

## Section: Basic Features of Classical Theory (1.3)

### Core Idea

Classical economics evolved in opposition to mercantilism, rejecting the mercantilist belief that national wealth depends on bullion stocks (gold and silver). Instead, classical economists held that the "wealth of nations" depends on **real factors** — labor, capital, land, and technology. Money is merely a medium of exchange, not a source of wealth. The classical framework rests on seven key features: microeconomic focus, laissez-faire, the invisible hand, continuous market clearing, perfect competition, Say's Law, and neutrality of money.

### Key Concepts

#### Mercantilism vs Classical Economics

The mercantilists believed that national wealth depended on the stock of bullion (gold and silver) and adopted policies that promoted exports and discouraged imports through subsidies and tariffs. Classical economists rejected this view, arguing that national wealth depends on the production of real goods and services. This fundamental shift — from a focus on monetary wealth to real productive capacity — underlies the entire classical framework and its treatment of money as a "veil" over real economic activity.

#### Laissez-Faire Philosophy

"Laissez-faire" is a French term meaning "leave alone" or "let you do." Classical economists advocated minimal government intervention in business affairs. Adam Smith specified three main duties of government:
1. **National defense**
2. **Administration of justice** (law and order)
3. **Establishing and maintaining certain public works** (infrastructure, education, etc.)

Beyond these, the government should not interfere with market outcomes — wages, prices, and interest rates should be determined by supply and demand alone.

#### Invisible Hand (Adam Smith)

Adam Smith introduced the concept of the "invisible hand" — the idea that individuals pursuing their own self-interest are led, as if by an invisible hand, to maximize the general welfare of everyone in the economy. Smith's famous illustration: *"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."* The producer sells not out of generosity but from self-interest; and the consumer buys not as a favor but for their own benefit. This philosophy led classical economists to focus on individual agent behavior, failing to see any conflict between the interests of individual agents and those of the economy as a whole.

#### Continuous Market Clearing

Classical economists assumed that prices and wage rates are fully flexible. If demand exceeds supply, price rises; if supply exceeds demand, price falls. This principle applies to both commodity markets and the labor market. If labor supply exceeds labor demand, wage rates will decline until all workers are employed. The implication is that **there is no unemployment** in the classical model — wages adjust to clear the labor market — and **there is no disequilibrium** in any market.

#### Perfect Competition Assumption

Classical economics assumes perfect competition in all markets, ensuring smooth functioning. Combined with full wage flexibility, this means production is always at the **full employment level**. Since the economy always operates at full capacity, there is no scope for ups and downs in the level of output. Following this logic, classical economists ruled out the possibility of **business cycles** — a prediction spectacularly contradicted by the Great Depression.

#### Say's Law of Market

Classical economists believed that production (supply) is the key to economic prosperity, emphasizing the supply side of the economy. Say's Law, named after J.B. Say, states: **"Supply creates its own demand."** Whenever production occurs, it generates a stream of income in the form of wages, rents, and profits, which in turn generates demand for goods. A person must have produced something to sell (e.g., labor services) and thereby earned income to spend. Therefore, in the classical view, there is no scope for deficiency of demand — the primary concern is production capacity, not demand.

#### Neutrality of Money

According to classical economists, economic growth comes from increases in the factors of production (labor, capital, land) and technological progress — not from monetary expansion. Money is just a medium of exchange that facilitates transactions among economic agents. Increasing the money supply does not affect the level of real output — it only leads to proportional increases in prices (as described by the Quantity Theory of Money). Classical economists posited a **dichotomy** between monetary variables (money supply, prices) and real variables (output, employment), stressing that only real factors determine real outcomes.

### Definitions

- **Laissez-Faire**: French term meaning "leave alone" or "let you do." The philosophy of minimal government intervention in economic affairs, with market forces expected to determine wages, prices, and interest rates.
- **Invisible Hand**: Adam Smith's metaphor for the self-regulating nature of markets — individuals pursuing self-interest inadvertently promote the general welfare without intending to do so.
- **Say's Law**: The proposition that "supply creates its own demand" — production generates income, which in turn generates demand, ruling out any general deficiency of demand in the economy.
- **Neutrality of Money**: The proposition that changes in the money supply affect only nominal variables (prices, nominal wages) and have no effect on real variables (output, employment, real wages).
- **Classical Dichotomy**: The separation of the economy into two independent spheres — the real sector (determined by technology and preferences) and the monetary sector (determined by money supply and velocity) — with no interaction between them.

### Examples

**Adam Smith's Butcher, Brewer, and Baker**: *"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."* This illustrates self-interested behavior leading to beneficial outcomes — the foundation of the invisible hand concept.

### Mechanisms / Processes

The classical adjustment mechanism for full employment:
1. Assume initial equilibrium at full employment with flexible wages
2. If labor supply exceeds labor demand → **wage rate declines**
3. Lower wages make hiring more profitable → **firms hire more workers**
4. Process continues until labor demand equals labor supply
5. Result: **all willing workers are employed** at the market-clearing wage
6. Full employment → economy operates at **full capacity output**
7. No government intervention needed — market forces achieve equilibrium automatically

### Edge Cases & Caveats

- The classical assumption that **business cycles cannot occur** was spectacularly disproven by the Great Depression of the 1930s, when about one-fourth of the labor force became unemployed.
- The invisible hand relies on the assumption that individual interests align with social welfare — but externalities, public goods, and market failures can cause divergence.
- Perfect competition is an idealization — real markets feature monopoly power, information asymmetries, and transaction costs.

### Open Questions

1. How realistic is the assumption of perfectly flexible wages? Real-world labor markets feature contracts, minimum wages, unions, and social norms that prevent instantaneous adjustment.
2. If the invisible hand always works, why do governments universally engage in economic policy? The text itself notes this tension — "all governments have in place certain economic policies."
