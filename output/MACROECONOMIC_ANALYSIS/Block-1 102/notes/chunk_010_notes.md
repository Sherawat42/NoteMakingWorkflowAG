## Section: Introduction to the Keynesian Model (2.1)

### Core Idea

The Keynesian model was first developed by John Maynard Keynes in his seminal 1936 work, *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*. This model directly challenges the classical approach by allowing for the existence of **unemployment**, recognizing **rigidity in wages and prices**, and emphasizing that a lack of **effective demand** may prevent the economy from reaching full employment. Unlike the classical self-adjusting economy, the Keynesian model argues that **government intervention** is necessary because the market mechanism alone cannot restore full employment when wages and prices are rigid.

### Key Concepts

#### Key Departures from Classical Model

The Keynesian model differs from the classical approach in four fundamental ways:
1. **Unemployment is possible**: The economy can operate below full employment for extended periods — involuntary unemployment can persist.
2. **Wages and prices are rigid (sticky)**: They do not adjust instantaneously to clear markets. Workers resist nominal wage cuts, and firms do not continuously adjust prices.
3. **Effective demand matters**: Output is determined by the level of aggregate demand, not aggregate supply. Lack of demand can prevent full resource utilization.
4. **Government intervention is necessary**: Since automatic market adjustment fails, fiscal policy (government spending, taxes) and monetary policy must actively manage aggregate demand.

#### Historical Context — Great Depression

Keynesian economics gained importance against the backdrop of the **Great Depression of the 1930s**, when developed economies like the USA and Great Britain faced a deep and prolonged recession. The key features were: rising unemployment, falling revenues and profits of firms, and a rise in unutilized productive capacity. The market mechanism **failed to restore equilibrium** on its own. The Keynesian policy prescription — **raising aggregate demand via increases in public expenditure** — played a crucial role in addressing the depression.

### Definitions

- **Effective Demand**: The actual level of aggregate demand in the economy that determines the level of output and employment. In the Keynesian model, if effective demand is deficient (below the level needed for full employment), the economy will operate with unemployment and unused capacity.

## Section: Components of Aggregate Demand (2.2)

### Core Idea

In the Keynesian framework, **aggregate demand (AD) is the planned expenditure** on goods and services by all economic agents. In a closed economy, AD has three components: planned consumption expenditure (C), planned investment expenditure (I), and government expenditure (G). All variables are assumed to be in **real terms** (i.e., no change in prices), consistent with the Keynesian fixed-price assumption.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Components of AD

1. **Planned Consumption Expenditure (C)**: Spending by households on goods and services — the largest component of AD
2. **Planned Investment Expenditure (I)**: Spending by firms on machinery, factories, and other capital goods that add to productive capacity
3. **Government Expenditure (G)**: Government spending on goods and services

In the simple model: **AD = C + I + G** (closed economy, real terms)

## Section: Consumption Function (2.2.1)

### Core Idea

Keynes proposed the **consumption function** — a fundamental behavioral relationship showing that planned consumption expenditure is a stable, positive function of aggregate income. The linear form C = a + bY has two components: an autonomous component 'a' (consumption even when income is zero) and an induced component 'bY' (consumption that responds to income). The parameter 'b' — the Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) — is a positive fraction between 0 and 1, meaning households consume only a fraction of each additional unit of income and save the rest.

### Key Concepts

#### Linear Consumption Function

The consumption function takes the form:

**C = a + bY** ... (2.1a) — without government/taxes

**C = a + b(Y − T) = a + bYd** ... (2.1b) — with government and lump-sum taxes T

where Yd = Y − T is disposable income. For the initial analysis, the unit assumes no government (T = 0), so Y = Yd. The consumption function is represented as an upward-sloping straight line with slope 'b' and intercept 'a'.

#### Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)

The MPC (denoted 'b') is defined as the **change in consumption induced by a unit change in income**:

**b = ΔC / ΔY** where 0 < b < 1

The MPC is assumed to be **less than one**, meaning the entire increment in income is NOT spent on consumption — a fraction (1 − b) is saved. Total saving from incremental income = (1 − b)ΔY.

#### Autonomous Consumption (a)

The intercept 'a' represents autonomous consumption — the level of consumption that occurs **even when income is zero** (a > 0). This consumption is financed by **drawing down past saving** (dis-saving). An increase in autonomous consumption (from a₀ to a₁) causes a **parallel upward shift** of the entire consumption function (C₀ to C₁). This can happen when, for instance, consumer confidence increases and households' expectations about the future turn buoyant.

#### Average Propensity to Consume (APC)

The APC is the ratio of total consumption to total income:

**APC = C/Y = a/Y + b**

Since a/Y decreases as Y rises (the autonomous component becomes a smaller share of income), APC **declines** as income increases. In contrast, the MPC remains **constant** at 'b'. This means wealthier economies consume a smaller proportion of total income.

#### Induced vs Autonomous Consumption

Aggregate consumption has two distinct components:
1. **Autonomous component 'a'**: Does not depend on income; represents baseline consumption needs
2. **Induced component 'bY'**: Depends on aggregate income; increases (decreases) when Y increases (decreases)

### Definitions

- **Consumption Function**: The relationship C = a + bY between planned consumption expenditure and aggregate income, where 'a' is autonomous consumption and 'b' is the MPC.
- **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)**: b = ΔC/ΔY — the fraction of each additional unit of income that is spent on consumption. Assumed to be a positive fraction (0 < b < 1).
- **Autonomous Consumption**: The level of consumption that occurs independently of income (when Y = 0). Represented by the intercept 'a' in the consumption function, with a > 0.
- **Disposable Income (Yd)**: Income after taxes: Yd = Y − T. Consumption depends on disposable income, not gross income, when taxes are present.
- **Average Propensity to Consume (APC)**: The ratio C/Y, representing the fraction of total income consumed. Unlike MPC, APC declines as income rises.
- **Dis-saving**: Drawing down past savings to finance consumption when current income is insufficient (or zero). Occurs when Y < a/b in the consumption function.

### Examples

**Numerical Example: C = 20 + 0.25Y**
- If aggregate income increases by ₹100 (ΔY = 100):
  - Additional consumption: ΔC = 0.25 × 100 = **₹25**
  - Additional saving: (1 − 0.25) × 100 = **₹75**
- If income decreases by ₹100 (ΔY = −100):
  - Consumption declines by ₹25 only
  - Saving declines by ₹75
- MPC = 0.25 (constant); MPS (Marginal Propensity to Save) = 0.75

## Section: Investment Function (2.2.2)

### Core Idea

Investment in the Keynesian model represents **planned expenditure by firms on new production equipment** — machinery, factories, and other capital goods that add to productive capacity. In the simplest version, investment is **autonomous** (independent of income): I = Ī. In an extended version, investment has both autonomous and income-induced components: I = c + dY, where 'd' is the Marginal Propensity to Invest (MPI). An important variable affecting investment decisions is the **rate of interest** — as interest rates rise, borrowing costs increase, reducing planned investment. This inverse relationship between investment and interest rate becomes central to the IS-LM model in Unit 3.

### Key Concepts

#### Autonomous vs Induced Investment

Two specifications of the investment function:
1. **Autonomous**: I = Ī — a fixed amount, independent of output level. Represented by a horizontal line in I-Y space (Fig. 2.2a).
2. **Induced**: I = c + dY — where c > 0 is the autonomous component, d > 0 is the MPI, and 'dY' represents investment induced by higher aggregate output. Represented by an upward-sloping line (Fig. 2.2b).

#### Marginal Propensity to Invest (MPI)

The MPI (denoted 'd') is the fraction of additional income that translates into additional investment. When aggregate output rises, firms find it profitable to expand capacity, so investment increases. However, typically d < b (MPI < MPC), meaning the income-investment response is weaker than the income-consumption response.

#### Investment and Interest Rate

There is an **inverse relationship** between investment and the rate of interest. As the interest rate rises, the cost of borrowing increases, reducing firms' planned investment expenditure. This relationship is formalized further in Chunk 011.

### Definitions

- **Investment (I)**: Planned expenditure by firms on new production equipment (machinery, buildings, etc.) that adds to productive capacity. **Important**: This does NOT refer to buying corporate bonds, shares, or opening bank fixed deposits — those are financial investments, not real investment.
- **Marginal Propensity to Invest (MPI)**: d = ΔI/ΔY — the fraction of additional output that is invested, in the induced investment function I = c + dY.
- **Autonomous Investment**: Investment that is independent of the level of aggregate income, determined by factors like business confidence, technological opportunities, and interest rates.

### Edge Cases & Caveats

- The term "investment" in macroeconomics has a **specific technical meaning** — it refers to real physical capital formation (machinery, buildings), NOT the purchase of financial assets like corporate bonds, shares, or bank deposits. Students commonly confuse these.
- In the simplest Keynesian model, investment is fully autonomous (I = Ī), which is a strong simplification. In reality, investment responds to both income levels and interest rates.

### Open Questions

1. What determines the autonomous component of investment besides interest rates? Business confidence, "animal spirits" (Keynes's term), technological change, and expectations about future demand all play roles.
2. Is the consumption function truly linear and stable over time? Empirical evidence suggests it may shift with wealth effects, credit availability, and demographic changes.
