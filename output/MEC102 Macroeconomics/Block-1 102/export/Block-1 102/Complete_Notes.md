## Section: Why Macroeconomics Needs Its Own Branch 🟢

### Core Idea
Macroeconomics studies functional relationships among economy-wide aggregates. It is needed as a separate field because what is true at the level of an individual unit (a single firm or household) may not hold at the aggregate level. A single firm can hire any number of workers at the prevailing wage without affecting it; but if all firms in the country simultaneously raise their labour demand, wages rise and the supply of labour itself becomes the binding limit.

> **In Simple Terms:** One person standing up at a stadium gets a better view; if everyone stands up, nobody does. Economy-wide behaviour can't be inferred by simply scaling up one firm's behaviour — that's the "fallacy of composition" that justifies macro as a distinct discipline.

### Key Concepts

#### Aggregative Analysis
Macroeconomics deals with aggregates — total output, employment, price level, money supply — and traces how they interact. The textbook stresses that macro is "not only a scientific method of analysis but also a body of empirical economic knowledge." A grasp of macro theory is now considered essential for interpreting policy and real-world events.

#### Disagreement Among Macroeconomists
Unlike microeconomics where there is broad consensus, macroeconomics is marked by sharp disagreements. The same data is read differently by different schools because they begin with different basic assumptions and models. This has been the case especially since the 1930s.

### Examples

**Example: Single firm vs. all firms hiring labour**
- A single cement firm can hire as many workers as it wants at the going wage rate; its individual demand has no effect on the wage.
- If every firm in the country simultaneously expands hiring (e.g., during a boom), labour shortages emerge and the wage rate rises. Beyond a point, total labour available is limited, so further demand only pushes wages up — not employment.

**Example: Global Energy Crisis of 2022**
- Caused by labour shortages, political disputes, climate concerns, and the Russia–Ukraine war.
- Effects: oil-exporting countries gained income inflows; oil-importing countries faced higher production costs.
- Result: severe inflation worldwide.
- Policy response: central banks raised interest rates and reduced money supply.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Macro studies aggregates; aggregate behaviour ≠ scaled-up individual behaviour.
> - Micro has consensus; macro has rival schools.
> - Disputes intensified after the 1930s.

## Section: Various Schools of Macroeconomic Thought 🔴

### Core Idea
A school of macroeconomic thought is a group of thinkers who share similar views on how macro variables behave. The two mainstream schools are **Classical** and **Keynesian**. Their later descendants — **New Classical** and **New Keynesian** — emerged in response to events like the Great Depression (which discredited pure Classical ideas) and the Stagflation of the 1970s (which discredited pure Keynesian ideas). A "Neoclassical Synthesis" (IS–LM, Unit 3) attempted to fuse them.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of macroeconomics as a long argument between two camps. Classicals say markets fix themselves; Keynesians say government must step in. Each side has been "revived" when the other side's predictions failed in real-world crises.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical Economics (18th–early 19th century)
Believed economic variables are set by market forces. Wages, prices, and interest rates are flexible — so the economy self-corrects to full employment. Prominent classical economists: **Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, A. R. J. Turgot, John Stuart Mill, Jean-Baptiste Say, Eugen Böhm von Bawerk.** The label "classical" was actually coined by Keynes to describe the economists who came before him. Classical theory aimed to explain the dramatic rise in wealth seen in that era.

#### Keynesian Economics (post-1930s)
Born as a response to the Great Depression (1929–1933), in which roughly one-fourth of the labour force in advanced economies became unemployed. **John Maynard Keynes** argued that wages and prices are *sticky*, not flexible; that actual output can fall below potential output; and that the gap is closed through fiscal and monetary policy intervention. He criticised the *laissez-faire* assumption of the classicals.

#### Neoclassical Synthesis (1950s onward)
An attempt to integrate classical and Keynesian ideas. The IS–LM model (Unit 3) is the canonical product of this synthesis. **Harrod, Domar, and Kaldor** (the *Post-Keynesians*) extended Keynesian ideas from the short run to the long run, arguing that large investments expand productive capacity and drive growth. **Robert M. Solow** later proposed the *neoclassical growth model* to overcome limitations of the post-Keynesian growth models.

#### New Classical Economics (post-1970s)
The 1970s brought *stagflation* — simultaneous stagnation and inflation — to advanced economies. Keynesian theory had no good policy answer. **Robert E. Lucas** (American economist) revived classical ideas, retaining classical core features but adding two innovations: **rational expectations** and **micro-foundations**.

#### New Keynesian Economics
A response to the New Classical revival. It introduces micro-foundations and rational expectations *into* Keynesian economics, while retaining sticky wages/prices and market imperfections.

#### Heterodox Economics
Outside the mainstream stand the heterodox schools: **Marxist, Austrian, institutional, feminist, and social economics** — among others. The textbook notes a current push for *pluralism* in economics.

### Comparison Table: Classical vs. Keynesian

| Issue | Classical | Keynesian |
|---|---|---|
| Wages & prices | Fully flexible | Sticky |
| Market clearing | Continuous, automatic | Need not clear; persistent unemployment possible |
| Role of demand | Supply creates its own demand (Say's Law) | Aggregate demand drives output |
| Government role | Laissez-faire | Active intervention via fiscal & monetary policy |
| Output level | Always at full employment / potential | Can fall below potential |
| Real–monetary sectors | Dichotomy (separable) | Linked |
| Money | Neutral | Non-neutral (in short run) |

### Comparison Table: New Classical vs. New Keynesian

| Feature | New Classical | New Keynesian |
|---|---|---|
| General equilibrium models | Yes | Yes |
| Micro-foundations | Yes | Yes |
| Rational expectations | Yes | Yes |
| Market structure | Perfect competition | Imperfect competition |
| Wage/price flexibility | Flexible | Rigid (sticky) |

### Definitions
- **School of macroeconomic thought**: a group of macroeconomic thinkers who share similar views on the behaviour of macroeconomic variables in an economy. ⭐
- **Classical economics**: school prevalent in the 18th and early 19th century holding that levels of economic variables are determined by market forces, with flexible wages, prices, and interest rates. ⭐
- **Keynesian economics**: school holding that wages/prices are sticky, that actual output can fall below potential, and that government should actively intervene to close the gap. ⭐
- **Neoclassical Synthesis**: integration of classical and Keynesian ideas, captured analytically in the IS–LM model.
- **Post-Keynesians**: 1950s economists (Harrod, Domar, Kaldor) who extended Keynesian ideas to the long-run growth context.
- **Stagflation**: simultaneous occurrence of economic stagnation and inflation, observed in advanced economies during the 1970s. ⭐
- **New Classical economics**: post-1970s revival of classical ideas adding rational expectations and micro-foundations; assumes perfect competition and flexible wages/prices. ⭐
- **New Keynesian economics**: response to New Classical economics; retains sticky wages/prices and market imperfections, while adding rational expectations and micro-foundations. ⭐
- **Heterodox economics**: non-mainstream schools — Marxist, Austrian, institutional, feminist, social, etc.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Classical consensus (19th–early 20th c.) → Great Depression (1929–33) falsifies flexible-price assumption → Keynes formulates Keynesian theory (1930s) → Keynesian dominance after WWII to 1970s → 1970s stagflation discredits pure Keynesianism → Lucas revives classical ideas as **New Classical** (rational expectations + micro-foundations) → **New Keynesian** response retains stickiness/imperfection but adopts micro-foundations and rational expectations.

### Four Key Issues Where Classical and Keynesian Differ
1. Role of demand and supply in determining equilibrium output, employment, and prices.
2. Whether prices and wages are flexible.
3. Whether there is a dichotomy between real and monetary sectors.
4. Whether money is neutral.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking "classical" was a self-applied label. → ✅ Correct: The term "classical" was *coined by Keynes* to refer collectively to economists before him.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating New Classical = Classical and New Keynesian = Keynesian. → ✅ Correct: Both "New" schools share micro-foundations and rational expectations; they differ from each other on market structure and wage/price flexibility, not on those modern building blocks.
- ❌ Mistake: Believing IS–LM is purely Keynesian. → ✅ Correct: IS–LM is the product of the *neoclassical synthesis* — a fusion.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The same economic data can be read differently across schools because they start from different assumptions and models.
- The mainstream/heterodox boundary is itself contested; the textbook calls for pluralism.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Two mainstream schools: Classical & Keynesian. Modern descendants: New Classical & New Keynesian.
> - Great Depression killed pure classical confidence; stagflation of the 1970s killed pure Keynesianism.
> - "Classical" label coined by Keynes.
> - Common to both modern schools: general equilibrium, micro-foundations, rational expectations.
> - Differ: market structure (perfect vs. imperfect) and price/wage flexibility.
> - Lucas → New Classical revival; IS–LM → Neoclassical Synthesis; Solow → Neoclassical growth model.

## Section: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory 🔴

### Core Idea
Classical economics arose as a rejection of mercantilism. While mercantilists equated national wealth with the stock of bullion (gold and silver) and used tariffs/subsidies to push exports and curb imports, classical economists argued that wealth depends on **real factors** of production and that money is merely a medium of exchange. The school is built on optimising agents, free markets, flexible wages and prices, perfect competition, Say's Law, and the neutrality of money.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine a marketplace where every buyer and seller follows their own self-interest, prices and wages move freely, and any imbalance fixes itself almost instantly. That self-correcting marketplace, with the government standing back, is the classical worldview.

### Key Concepts

#### (i) Microeconomic Issues
Classical economists focused on the behaviour of economic agents — firms maximise profit subject to a resource constraint; households maximise utility subject to a budget constraint. They believed in the **optimising tendencies of the market mechanism**: variables like price level, wage rate, and output level should be set by supply and demand, not by the state.

#### (ii) Laissez Faire
"Laissez faire" is French for "leave alone" / "let you do" — government should intervene minimally in business affairs. Adam Smith restricted government to **three duties**:
1. National defence.
2. Administration of justice (law and order).
3. Establishing and maintaining certain public works (infrastructure, education, etc.).

#### (iii) Invisible Hand
Adam Smith's metaphor: when each individual pursues self-interest, the economy is led "as if by an invisible hand" to maximise general welfare. His exact phrasing in the chapter: *"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."* Both producers and consumers are pursuing self-interest — neither is doing the other a favour. Because of this faith in the invisible hand, classical economists never saw conflict between agents' interests and the economy's interest.

#### (iv) Continuous Market Clearing
Prices and wages are flexible. From microeconomics: equilibrium price equates supply and demand; if demand > supply, price rises, and vice versa. The same applies to the wage rate — if labour supply exceeds labour demand, wage rate falls until they equate. **Implication:** there is no unemployment and no disequilibrium — wages always adjust to clear the labour market.

#### (v) Perfect Competition
Markets are perfectly competitive, so they function smoothly. Combined with wage flexibility (and hence full employment), production is always at the full-employment level. **Implication:** no scope for ups and downs in output ⇒ classical economists **ruled out the possibility of business cycles**.

#### (vi) Say's Law of Market
Production / supply is the key to prosperity — classical economists emphasised the **supply side**. Named after **J. B. Say**: *"supply creates its own demand"*. Whenever production happens, it generates a stream of income (rent, wages, profit) in people's hands, which is spent and so generates demand. To consume, a person must first have produced something to sell (e.g., labour) and earned income. Therefore there is **no scope for deficiency of demand**; the economy's primary concern is production, not demand.

#### (vii) Neutrality of Money
Economic growth comes from increases in factors of production and technological progress. Money only facilitates transactions — an increase in money supply does **not** affect output, only prices (per the Quantity Theory of Money). Hence the **dichotomy** between monetary variables (money supply, prices) and real variables (output, employment). Real factors decide real variables; money is neutral.

### Definitions
- **Mercantilism**: pre-classical view that national wealth equals the stock of bullion (gold and silver), defended by export-promoting and import-restricting policies (subsidies and tariffs).
- **Laissez faire**: French for "leave alone" / "let you do"; classical principle that government should intervene minimally in the economy. ⭐
- **Invisible hand**: Adam Smith's metaphor — self-interested individuals are led to promote general welfare. ⭐
- **Continuous market clearing**: classical assumption that flexible wages and prices keep all markets continuously in equilibrium with no unemployment. ⭐
- **Say's Law of Market**: "supply creates its own demand" — production generates the income needed to buy back the output, ruling out general overproduction. ⭐
- **Neutrality of money**: changes in money supply alter only nominal variables (prices, money wages) and leave real variables (output, employment) unchanged. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Mercantilist and classical views are similar because both are pre-Keynesian. → ✅ Classical economics emerged *against* mercantilism — wealth comes from real production, not bullion stocks.
- ❌ Mistake: Adam Smith advocated zero government. → ✅ Smith allowed three duties: defence, justice, and certain public works.
- ❌ Mistake: Classical theory accommodates business cycles. → ✅ Perfect competition + wage flexibility means full-employment output always — classical economists *ruled out* business cycles.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Seven features: Microeconomic focus, Laissez faire, Invisible hand, Continuous market clearing, Perfect competition, Say's Law, Neutrality of money.
> - Anti-mercantilist: real factors (not bullion) create wealth; money is just a medium of exchange.
> - Smith's three government duties: defence, justice, public works.
> - No unemployment, no business cycles, no demand deficiency.

## Section: 1.4 Determination of Output and Employment 🔴

### Core Idea
The classical model determines output and employment in **two stages**: (1) the labour market, where flexible real wages clear the market and pin down full-employment labour; (2) the aggregate production function, which converts that full-employment labour and the given capital stock into full-employment output. Because output is fixed at the full-employment level, the **aggregate supply (AS) curve is a vertical line**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Wages adjust until everyone willing to work at the going real wage has a job. Once employment is locked in, the production function spits out the corresponding output. Output is not driven by demand — it is driven from the supply side, by labour and capital.

### Three Foundational Assumptions of the Classical Model
a) Firms and workers are **optimisers**.
b) They have **perfect knowledge**.
c) They operate in **perfectly competitive markets**.

⇒ Wages and prices are **fully flexible**.

### Key Concepts

#### Labour Demand (from Profit Maximisation)
A profit-maximising firm under perfect competition hires labour up to the point where:

P = W / MPN  …(1.1)

- **P** = product price
- **W** = nominal wage
- **MPN** = marginal product of labour

Under perfect competition P = Marginal Revenue (MR) (revenue from selling one additional unit), and W/MPN is the **marginal cost of production** (cost of producing one extra unit).

Rearranging:

MPN = W/P  …(1.2)

So the **marginal product of labour equals the real wage** (W/P). The labour demand curve in real-wage terms is just the MPN schedule, which is **downward-sloping**:

N^d = f(W/P)  …(1.3)

(Convention: nominal wage = W, real wage = w = W/P.)

#### Labour Supply
Households maximise utility, so labour supply rises with the real wage — **upward-sloping**:

N^s = g(W/P)  …(1.4)

#### Labour Market Equilibrium (Fig. 1.1)
At the intersection of N^d and N^s, the labour market is in equilibrium **at full employment**: supply of labour equals demand for labour.

Two-panel structure of Fig. 1.1:
- **Upper panel:** N^d and N^s drawn as functions of the real wage W/P.
- **Lower panel:** N^d and N^s drawn as functions of the **nominal** wage W. As the price level rises P₁ → P₂ → P₃, the **same real wage** is preserved if the nominal wage rises proportionally to W₁ → 2W₁ → 3W₁. The labour demand and supply curves shift up in nominal terms but employment stays at N₁.

Numerical identity in the figure:

W₁/P₁ = 2W₁/(2P₁) = 3W₁/(3P₁)

If prices rise but the nominal wage does **not** rise (e.g., money wage stuck at W₁ while P rises): real wage falls, and labour supply contracts.

#### Aggregate Production Function
With only two factors (labour and capital):

Y = F(K, N)

For a given K, output rises with N at a diminishing rate (Fig. 1.2 panel b is concave).

#### Two-Panel Equilibrium (Fig. 1.2)
- **Panel (a):** Labour-market equilibrium fixes (W/P)₀ and N₀.
- **Panel (b):** N₀ plugged into Y = F(K, N) gives full-employment output Y₀.

**Result:** the economy operates at full-employment labour N₀ and full-employment output Y₀.

#### Aggregate Supply Curve (Fig. 1.3)
Because employment is always at full employment and the production function is fixed, output is fixed at the full-employment level. So the **AS curve is vertical** in (Y, P) space. The AS curve **shifts only if** technology (the production function) or capital stock K changes.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Output and employment determination chain:**

1. Real wage in the labour market adjusts → N^d = N^s at full employment N₀.
2. Production function Y = F(K, N) with N = N₀ and given K → full-employment output Y₀.
3. With output fixed at Y₀, AS is a **vertical** line at Y₀ in (Y, P) space.
4. AS shifts only with technology (function form) or capital stock changes — never with money supply or prices.

**Price-rise mechanism preserving real wage (Fig. 1.1 lower panel):**

Price level P₁ → 2P₁ → 3P₁ ⇒ Nominal wage W₁ → 2W₁ → 3W₁ ⇒ Real wage unchanged ⇒ Employment N₁ unchanged.

| # | Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | P = W/MPN | Profit-max condition under perfect competition |
| 1.2 | MPN = W/P | MPL equals real wage |
| 1.3 | N^d = f(W/P) | Labour demand (downward-sloping in W/P) |
| 1.4 | N^s = g(W/P) | Labour supply (upward-sloping in W/P) |
|  | Y = F(K, N) | Aggregate production function |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Labour demand depends on the nominal wage. → ✅ It depends on the **real** wage W/P; labour demand curve is the MPN schedule.
- ❌ Mistake: An increase in P (with W constant proportionally rising) changes employment. → ✅ Equiproportional rise in W and P leaves W/P unchanged ⇒ employment unchanged.
- ❌ Mistake: AS is upward-sloping in classical theory. → ✅ Classical AS is **vertical** at full-employment output; it shifts only with technology or capital.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- If output prices rise but the nominal wage is sticky, real wage falls and labour **supply** falls — but classical theory assumes nominal wages adjust instantly, so this case is not realised in classical equilibrium.
- AS curve shifts: only changes in K or technology (production function) move it.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - MPN = W/P (labour demand condition).
> - Labour demand: N^d = f(W/P), downward-sloping. Labour supply: N^s = g(W/P), upward-sloping.
> - Y = F(K, N); employment from labour market, output from production function.
> - Classical AS is **vertical** at Y₀; only K or technology shifts it.

## Section: 1.5 Quantity Theory of Money (QTM) 🔴

### Core Idea
Once classical theory has fixed real output Y at the full-employment level (from Section 1.4), the price level is determined entirely by the money market. The Quantity Theory of Money states that with V (velocity) and Y (or T, transactions) treated as constants, the price level P is **proportional** to the money supply M. So QTM is fundamentally a **theory of the price level**.

> **In Simple Terms:** If real output is locked in by labour and technology, then doubling the amount of money in the economy will exactly double prices — money chases the same goods. Money is a measuring rod, not a source of growth.

### Key Concepts

#### Why a Money Market?
- Money is a **stock variable** (quantity at a point in time).
- **Demand for money** = total money the public (households + firms) wish to hold.
- **Supply of money** is set by the government and banking system; in this unit, the **monetary authority autonomously** fixes M.
- Their interaction is the money market.

#### Three Theories of Money Demand (mentioned)
1. Classical theory of money demand = **Quantity Theory of Money (QTM)**.
2. Keynesian theory of money demand.
3. Friedman's restatement of the classical QTM.

#### Four Functions of Money
1. Medium of exchange.
2. Standard of deferred payment.
3. Store of value.
4. Unit of account.

Classical economists view money as demanded **only for its role as medium of exchange**.

#### Fisher's Equation of Exchange (Transactions Version)

M·V = P·T  …(1.5)

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| M | Quantity of money supply |
| V | Velocity of circulation of money |
| P | Average price level |
| T | Number of transactions of average size (proxy for income/output) |

- **Right-hand side (P·T):** total amount of money required to facilitate transactions (purchase/sale of total output).
- **Left-hand side (M·V):** total amount of money available for transactions during the period — number of rupees in circulation × number of times each is used in payments.
- **Equilibrium:** money demanded (PT) = money supplied (MV).

Under classical assumptions:
- **T fixed** because output is fixed at full employment (T proxies national income).
- **V fixed** because it depends on the public's payment behaviour, which is constant in the long run.

Rearranging (1.5):

P = (V / T) · M  …(1.6)

With V and T constant ⇒ **P is proportional to M**. If M quadruples, P quadruples — hence QTM = **theory of the price level**.

#### Income Version

MV = PY  …(1.7)

Substitute real output Y for transactions T. Same reading:
- **MV** = total money stock × velocity = nominal value of transactions.
- **PY** = nominal income / nominal output.

Identity (1.7) becomes a **theory** under three assumptions:
- Y constant in the short run (full employment).
- V constant in the short run (stable payment habits).
- Price level P is **passive** (P depends on M, not vice versa).

⇒ Any short-run change in M produces a **proportional** change in P. **Relaxing any of these assumptions breaks the proportionality.**

#### Final Classical Pricing Equation
With Ȳ (full-employment output) and V̄ (predetermined velocity) given and M exogenous:

P = (V̄ / Ȳ) · M  …(1.8)

So the **labour market and production function determine output**, while **money supply determines the price level**.

#### Aggregate Demand Curve from QTM (Fig. 1.4)
QTM also yields the AD curve. Combined with the vertical AS curve at Ȳ, equilibrium is at point E with price P determined by money supply.

### Definitions
- **Quantity Theory of Money (QTM)**: classical theory stating that, with V and Y (or T) constant, the price level is proportional to the money supply. Functions as a theory of the price level. ⭐
- **Fisher's Equation of Exchange**: MV = PT (transactions version) or MV = PY (income version). ⭐
- **Velocity of circulation (V)**: the number of times a unit of currency changes hands during a given period. ⭐
- **Money supply (M)**: total quantity of money in the economy at a point in time, set exogenously by the monetary authority in this unit.
- **Transactions (T)**: number of transactions of average size; proxy for income level under full employment.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Classical price determination chain:**

1. Labour market clears at full employment ⇒ Y = Ȳ (fixed).
2. Long-run payment habits ⇒ V = V̄ (fixed).
3. Monetary authority sets M exogenously.
4. From MV = PY: **P = (V̄/Ȳ) · M**.
5. ΔM ⇒ proportional ΔP, with Y unchanged.

### Examples

**Worked illustration from the text:**
- If V and T are constants in equation (1.6) P = (V/T)·M, and M is increased fourfold, then P also rises fourfold. Output and employment are unchanged (because they are pinned down by the labour market and production function, not by money).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: MV = PY is automatically QTM. → ✅ As written, MV = PY is an **identity** (true by definition). It becomes the QTM **only after** assuming V constant, Y constant, and P passive.
- ❌ Mistake: An increase in M raises real output. → ✅ In classical QTM, M raises only P; Y is set by labour and capital.
- ❌ Mistake: V is constant by definition. → ✅ V is **assumed** constant because payment behaviour is stable in the long run; in reality V varies (a key empirical critique).
- ❌ Mistake: QTM is a theory of money demand only. → ✅ It is foremost a **theory of the price level**, derived from money-market equilibrium.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- **Critique by Keynesians and Monetarists:** classical theory **fails in the short run when prices are sticky**.
- **Empirical:** velocity of money is **not** constant over time — relaxing V invalidates strict proportionality.
- Despite these, the textbook notes classical theory is still useful for **understanding and controlling inflation**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Fisher: MV = PT (transactions); MV = PY (income).
> - With V̄, Ȳ fixed: P ∝ M ⇒ P = (V̄/Ȳ)·M.
> - QTM = theory of **price level**, not of output.
> - Money's classical function: medium of exchange.
> - Output → labour market & production function. Price → money supply.

## Section: 1.6 Say's Law of Market 🔴

### Core Idea
Say's Law — "every supply generates its own demand" — is a basic pillar of classical theory. When a commodity is produced, its price is paid out as **rent + wages + profits** to factor owners; that income is exactly enough to buy back the produced output. So under laissez-faire there is **no surplus, no general overproduction, and no major depression** — any recession must come from interference with the free flow of commodities and money.

> **In Simple Terms:** Production itself creates the paychecks that buy the production. The act of making things finances the act of buying them. So gluts and slumps cannot persist on their own — only meddling can cause them.

### Key Concepts

#### Logical Chain
- A commodity is produced → its price = rent + wages + profits.
- This price flows as **income** to land, labour, capital owners.
- That income is **sufficient to realise the price** of the commodity.
- ⇒ Aggregate income always equals aggregate output ⇒ enough demand to absorb output.

#### Dependence on Price Flexibility
- The law leans **heavily on perfect price flexibility**.
- Whatever quantity is produced **can be sold** at some price.
- In the **short run** supply may exceed demand (or vice-versa), but the discrepancy is **automatically and instantaneously** corrected by a fall (or rise) in price.
- This holds for **all commodities and all factor inputs**.
- Say's Law is therefore a **natural consequence of perfect competition**.

#### Validity in Barter vs. Money Economy
Proponents claim Say's Law is valid in **both** a barter and a money economy. The law states income from output is **always spent on aggregate demand** (consumption + investment), implying:
- Money is **never hoarded**.
- The expenditure stream **MV remains neutral**.

| Setting | Validity | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Barter economy | Strong | Production is for consumption — whatever is produced is exchanged for goods/services. |
| Modern money economy | Doubtful | Production is based on **future expectations** of demand ⇒ some overproduction is bound to occur. |
| Modern, with caveat | Holds | If a substantial portion of output is consumed and the rest, **if saved, is invested**. |

### Definitions
- **Say's Law of Market**: every supply creates its own demand; income generated by producing output is always sufficient (and is spent) to buy back that output. ⭐
- **Hoarding (in Say's-Law sense)**: holding money idle instead of spending it on consumption or investment — ruled out by Say's Law. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Income-recirculation chain (Say's Law):**

1. Firm produces commodity → sells at price P.
2. P is decomposed into rent + wages + profits → paid to factor owners.
3. Factor owners spend that income on consumption + investment.
4. Aggregate demand = aggregate income = value of output.
5. ⇒ No leftover output; no demand deficiency; depression impossible (absent interference).

**Saved-but-invested condition for modern validity:**

Production → Income → (Consumption + Saving) → if Saving = Investment → AD = AS still holds.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Say's Law claims demand creates supply. → ✅ It claims **supply creates its own demand** — direction matters.
- ❌ Mistake: Say's Law allows hoarding. → ✅ It assumes money is **never hoarded**; MV stays neutral.
- ❌ Mistake: Say's Law works only in barter. → ✅ Classical economists assert validity in both barter and money economies; modern relevance survives if savings flow into investment.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Modern production is forward-looking (based on **expectations of demand**); this alone can produce overproduction episodes.
- Recessions in classical analysis must come from **outside interference** with prices/markets, not internal demand failure.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - "Supply creates its own demand" — J. B. Say.
> - Holds because Price = Rent + Wages + Profits ⇒ income matches output.
> - Requires perfect price flexibility + no hoarding (MV neutral).
> - Modern caveat: valid if all savings get invested.

### Connections
- Builds on: Section 1.3 feature (vi) Say's Law (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — restated and expanded.
- Builds on: Section 1.3 feature (iv) Continuous market clearing (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — both rest on price flexibility.
- Feeds into: Section 1.4 (Saving-Investment Equilibrium) below — savings = investment is precisely the modern condition for Say's Law.

## Section: 1.4 Classical Dichotomy 🔴
<!-- Note: textbook numbering reset — printed as 1.4 / 1.4.1 / 1.4.2 / 1.4.3 in source, but conceptually this is Section 1.7 of Unit 1. -->

### Core Idea
QTM tells us how M affects P. The **Classical Dichotomy** extends this: changes in money supply affect **only nominal variables** (P, money wages, nominal interest rates, nominal income), while **real variables** (real GDP, real capital stock, employment) are pinned down purely by **real supply-side factors**. Money and the real economy are **separated**.

> **In Simple Terms:** The economy runs on two parallel tracks. The "real" track — labour, capital, technology — decides how much stuff gets made and how many people work. The "money" track only stretches or shrinks the price tags. Pour more money in: tags get bigger, but the stuff on the shelves and the workers in the factory don't change.

### Key Concepts

#### Real vs Nominal Variables

| Type | Examples | What measures? |
|---|---|---|
| **Real variables** | Real GDP, real capital stock, employment | Physical quantity (goods/services produced; machines & structures available; persons employed) |
| **Nominal variables** | Price level, money wage in rupees, nominal interest rate, nominal income | Monetary value |

#### What Determines Real Variables (classical view)
Real **supply-side factors** alone:
- Population.
- Technology (state of technology).
- Capital stock.
- **Marginal physical product of labour**.
- Households' preferences regarding **work and leisure**.

Flexibility of prices and wages → markets self-adjust → ΔM hits **only** nominal variables. Real variables are **independent** of M and other nominal magnitudes — this independence is the **classical dichotomy**.

### Definitions
- **Classical Dichotomy**: classical proposition that **nominal variables do not influence real variables** in the economy; arises from perfect competition + full flexibility of prices and wages. ⭐
- **Real variable**: variable measured in physical quantity (real GDP, real capital stock, employment). ⭐
- **Nominal variable**: variable measured in monetary value (price level, money wages, nominal interest rate). ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Classical dichotomy means money is unimportant. → ✅ Money still pins down all **nominal** variables — it just cannot move **real** ones.
- ❌ Mistake: The dichotomy holds even with sticky prices. → ✅ It rests on **flexibility of prices and wages**; rigidity breaks it (this is exactly the Keynesian objection).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Real track: P, K, technology, MPN, work-leisure preferences → Y, N, real K.
> - Money track: M → P, W, nominal i, nominal Y.
> - Foundation: perfect competition + price/wage flexibility.

### Connections
- Builds on: Section 1.5 QTM (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — extends the M→P link to a full real–nominal split.
- Feeds into: 1.4.2 Neutrality of Money (below) — direct corollary.
- Feeds into: 1.4.3 Saving-Investment Equilibrium (below) — money also leaves real interest rate untouched.

## Section: 1.4.2 Neutrality of Money 🔴

### Core Idea
Neutrality of money is the **direct corollary** of classical dichotomy. An increase in money supply raises AD, raises P, raises the money wage W in equal proportion — leaving real wage, employment and output **completely unchanged**. ΔM affects nominal variables only.

> **In Simple Terms:** Double the money in everyone's wallet and double every price tag, including wage rates. Nothing real has happened — the same workers make the same goods. Money is a measuring rod.

### Key Concepts

#### The Trace-Through (Fig. 1.5 with ΔM)

| Step | Variable | Direction | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Money supply | M₀ → M₁ | Monetary authority expands M |
| 2 | AD curve | AD₀ → AD₁ (shifts right) | More money chases same Y |
| 3 | Price level | P₀ → P₁ | AD₁ ∩ vertical AS at higher P |
| 4 | Real wage (initial impact) | W₀/P₀ → W₀/P₁ (falls) | W₀ unchanged, P up |
| 5 | Labour demand | rises | At lower real wage, firms want more labour |
| 6 | Excess demand for labour | N^d > N^s | Labour market disequilibrium |
| 7 | Money wage adjusts | W₀ → W₁ (proportional to ΔP) | Classical wage flexibility |
| 8 | Real wage restored | W₁/P₁ = W₀/P₀ | Equiproportional rise |
| 9 | Employment | N_F (unchanged) | Same real wage as before |
| 10 | Real output | Y_F (unchanged) | Production function with same N_F, K |

**End state:** P↑, W↑ (proportionally), W/P unchanged, N_F unchanged, Y_F unchanged. Money is **neutral**.

#### Algebraic Identity
W₁/P₁ = W₀/P₀ — proportional rise in money wage exactly offsets proportional rise in price level.

### Definitions
- **Neutrality of money**: independence of real economic variables (Y, N, real wage, real interest) from changes in money supply. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Neutrality chain (compact):**
ΔM ⇒ ΔAD ⇒ ΔP ⇒ Δ(W/P) momentary ⇒ ΔN^d > N^s ⇒ ΔW (proportional) ⇒ W/P restored ⇒ N_F, Y_F unchanged.

### Examples

**Example: Doubling Money Supply (textbook logic)**
- M doubles ⇒ AD shifts so P doubles.
- Real wage initially halves ⇒ labour demand jumps, excess demand for labour.
- Money wage doubles ⇒ real wage restored.
- Employment and output: **unchanged** at N_F, Y_F.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: After ΔM, employment changes because real wage changes. → ✅ The real wage **moves momentarily but is restored** by proportional adjustment of W; final employment is unchanged.
- ❌ Mistake: Neutrality means money has no effect. → ✅ Money has full effect on **nominal** variables (P, W); only **real** variables are unaffected.
- ❌ Mistake: Neutrality holds always. → ✅ It is a **full-employment, full-flexibility** result. With sticky prices it fails.

### Edge Cases & Caveats — A Crucial Limitation
- Neutrality is built on **full-employment equilibrium with full price flexibility**.
- If higher money supply (and higher prices) had **no real effects**, inflation would not be a serious concern.
- But inflation **is** a serious problem — it lowers quality of life, reduces economic growth, etc.
- Hence policy actively works to **control inflation and maintain price stability**, implicitly conceding non-neutrality outside the classical world.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Neutrality = ΔM hits P and W proportionally; W/P, N, Y unchanged.
> - Mechanism: ΔM → ΔAD → ΔP → momentarily Δ(W/P) → ΔW restores W/P → N_F, Y_F unchanged.
> - Limitation: depends on price/wage flexibility; inflation's real costs show neutrality breaks empirically.

- Builds on: Section 1.4 (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — labour market mechanics; Section 1.5 (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — QTM-based AD.

## Section: 1.4.3 Saving-Investment Equilibrium 🔴

### Core Idea
Classical money is demanded **only for transactions**. Therefore the money market (M demand vs. M supply) does **not** determine the rate of interest. The **real** rate of interest is determined in the **capital market** — by saving and investment in real terms — and an increase in M leaves saving, investment, and the real interest rate **unchanged**.

> **In Simple Terms:** The interest rate is the price set in the savings-and-loans market, not in the money market. Print more money: prices and nominal saving and nominal investment all scale up together, but real lending and real borrowing are exactly the same. The capital market's equilibrium is undisturbed.

### Key Concepts

#### Why Money Market Does Not Determine i
- Classical theory: money's role = **medium of exchange** (transaction demand only).
- Hence M demand and M supply don't pin down equilibrium **r** (real interest rate).
- An increase in M does **not** affect real interest rate.
- ⇒ Saving (S) and Investment (I) in real terms are **unchanged**.
- ⇒ Capital market equilibrium intact ⇒ full-employment equilibrium intact.

#### How ΔM Plays Out in the Capital Market (Fig. 1.6)
ΔM → ΔP (proportional). Both nominal saving and nominal investment scale up by the same factor:
- **Nominal saving curve** shifts right/down: SS → S′S′.
- **Nominal investment curve** shifts up: II → I′I′.
- **Same proportional shift** in both.

| Axis | Variable |
|---|---|
| Y-axis | **Real** interest rate |
| X-axis | Nominal value of saving / investment |

The new intersection of S′S′ and I′I′ pins down the **same real interest rate** as before — and **real** saving and **real** investment are unchanged at the higher price level.

#### Argument for Equiproportional Shift
- ΔM ⇒ P rises.
- Higher prices ⇒ **nominal investment expenditure** rises in proportion.
- ⇒ Real investment expenditure unchanged.
- Same logic for nominal saving.

### Definitions
- **Capital market equilibrium (classical)**: saving = investment, determining the **real interest rate** independently of money supply.
- **Real interest rate**: nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation; classical determinant of real saving and real investment.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Capital-market neutrality chain:**

ΔM → ΔP (proportional) → Nominal S and Nominal I both shift proportionally → Real interest rate unchanged → Real S, Real I unchanged → Capital-market equilibrium and full-employment equilibrium preserved.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: In the classical model, the money market sets the interest rate. → ✅ The **capital market** (S vs I) sets the **real** interest rate; the money market sets only the price level.
- ❌ Mistake: ΔM does not move nominal saving or nominal investment in the classical capital market. → ✅ Both nominal S and nominal I shift proportionally to ΔP — but real S and real I are unchanged.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Money's only function (classical) = medium of exchange ⇒ no role in setting r.
> - Real r set by S = I in real terms.
> - ΔM moves SS → S′S′ and II → I′I′ by the same proportion ⇒ real r unchanged.
> - Capital-market neutrality is the third leg of the dichotomy (after AD-AS and labour market).

### Connections
- Builds on: 1.4.2 Neutrality of Money (above) — completes the neutrality story for the capital market.
- Builds on: Section 1.5 (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — money's transaction-only role.
- Contrasts with: Keynesian Liquidity Preference (Section 2.5, Chunk 004 onwards) — there money supply **does** affect the rate of interest.

## Section: 1.5 Long Run vs. Short Run 🔴
<!-- Textbook numbering printed as 1.5 in source; conceptually Section 1.8 of Unit 1. -->

### Core Idea
Supply and demand fluctuate, but their effect on output depends on the time horizon. In the **long run** prices are **flexible**; in the **short run** most prices are **sticky** at predetermined levels. Because price behaviour differs, the same shock (e.g., ΔM) produces different effects in the two horizons. **Classical theory applies in the long run**; the short run needs a different framework — namely Keynesian (Unit 2).

> **In Simple Terms:** Over a long enough horizon, prices and wages have time to adjust and the classical story works. Over a few months, they're stuck — so spending decisions, not flexible prices, drive output.

### Key Concepts

#### The Two Horizons

| Feature | Long Run | Short Run |
|---|---|---|
| Prices and wages | **Flexible** | **Sticky** at predetermined level |
| Aggregate supply curve | **Vertical** at Y* (potential output / full employment) | **Upward sloping** |
| Output response to ΔAD | None — only P moves | Output can rise/fall with AD |
| Output response to ΔAS | Y rises, P falls | Y rises, P falls |
| Effect of ΔM | Only ΔP (neutrality) | ΔY and ΔP both |
| Applicable model | Classical | Keynesian (Unit 2) |

#### AS-AD Picture (Fig. 1.7)

| Curve | Shape | Notation |
|---|---|---|
| Long-run aggregate supply | Vertical at Y* | AS_LR |
| Short-run aggregate supply | Upward-sloping | AS₀ |
| Aggregate demand | Downward-sloping | AD₀ |

Equilibrium initially at Y₀ (intersection of AS₀, AD₀, with AS_LR passing through Y*).

#### Comparative Statics in the Short Run

**Outward AS shift (AS₀ → AS₁):**
- Output: Y₀ → Y₁ (rises).
- Price: P₀ → P₁ (falls).

**Outward AD shift (AD₀ → AD₁):**
- Output: Y₀ → Y₂ (rises).
- Price: P₀ → P₂ (rises).

#### Money Supply Across Horizons

| Action | Short Run | Long Run |
|---|---|---|
| ΔM > 0 (M rises) | AD shifts right; **prices and income both rise** | AD shifts right; **only prices rise** (neutrality) |
| ΔM < 0 (M falls) | AD shifts left; **prices and income both fall** | AD shifts left; **only prices fall** |

So the classical claim "ΔM raises prices but not real output" is a **long-run** statement.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Why short-run output responds to nominal shocks:**
1. Macro variables not fully flexible (sticky wages and prices).
2. ΔM ⇒ ΔAD without immediate price clearing.
3. Firms produce more (or less) at the (slow-moving) price.
4. ⇒ Real output changes alongside prices.
5. Eventually prices adjust (long run) and only the price effect remains.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Classical theory applies to all time horizons. → ✅ Classical theory is a **long-run** theory; short-run analysis needs the Keynesian framework.
- ❌ Mistake: Short-run AS is vertical. → ✅ Short-run AS is **upward-sloping**; only **long-run** AS is vertical at Y*.
- ❌ Mistake: ΔM cannot affect output ever. → ✅ It cannot in the long run (classical), but **does** in the short run when prices are sticky.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Y* (potential / full-employment output) is the long-run anchor; short-run Y can sit above or below it temporarily.
- Different effects of policy/events across horizons motivate the need for **two parallel models** (Unit 1 vs Unit 2).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Long run: prices flexible ⇒ vertical AS at Y* ⇒ classical neutrality.
> - Short run: prices sticky ⇒ upward-sloping AS ⇒ ΔM moves both Y and P.
> - Classical = long-run model. Keynesian = short-run model.

- Feeds into: Unit 2 The Keynesian Model (Chunk 004 onwards) — provides the short-run framework where prices are sticky.

3. **No** — sticky prices/wages prevent instantaneous adjustment ⇒ classical theory not applicable in the short run.

## Section: 2.2 Components of Aggregate Demand 🔴

### Core Idea
Keynesian theory rose during the **Great Depression of the 1930s**, when developed economies (USA, UK) faced deep recession with rising unemployment, falling firm revenues/profits, and unutilised capacity. Markets failed to self-equilibrate; raising aggregate demand via **public expenditure** restored output. In a closed economy, AD has three components: planned consumption (C), planned investment (I), and government expenditure (G), all measured in **real terms**.

> **In Simple Terms:** The economy in a slump won't fix itself. Someone has to spend. Aggregate demand = households spending (C) + firms spending (I) + government spending (G).

### Key Concepts

#### Definition of Aggregate Demand
**Aggregate demand** = planned expenditure on goods and services by all economic agents (households, firms, government).

#### Three Components (Closed Economy)

|---|---|---|
| Planned consumption expenditure | Households | C |
| Planned investment expenditure | Firms | I |
| Expenditure on goods and services | Government | G |

All three assumed to be in **real** terms throughout this Unit (no price changes).

### Definitions
- **Aggregate demand (Keynesian)**: planned expenditure on goods and services by households, firms, and government. ⭐

> - All variables are real (constant prices assumption).
> - Backdrop: 1930s Great Depression ⇒ public expenditure cure.

## Section: 2.2.1 Consumption Function 🔴

### Core Idea
The **consumption function** links planned household consumption (C) to aggregate income (Y). In linear form **C = a + bY**. Slope **b** is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), a positive fraction (0 < b < 1). Intercept **a** is autonomous consumption (a > 0). Consumption has an **autonomous part (a)** and an **induced part (bY)**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Households spend a fixed minimum (a) even at zero income (drawing on past savings) and then a fixed fraction (b) of every extra rupee earned. The other fraction (1−b) is saved.

### Key Concepts

#### The Linear Form
Without government:
- **C = a + bY** … (2.1a)

With lump-sum tax T (so disposable income Yd = Y − T):
- **C = a + b(Y − T) = a + bYd** … (2.1b)

For exposition: assume no government ⇒ T = 0, Y = Yd.

#### MPC — Marginal Propensity to Consume
- b = MPC = ΔC/ΔY.
- 0 < b < 1.
- Represents change in consumption induced by a unit change in income.
- ΔC = b·ΔY ⇒ entire increment in income is **not** all consumed.
- Saving out of incremental income = (1 − b)·ΔY.

#### APC — Average Propensity to Consume
- APC = C/Y.
- From (2.1a): **APC = a/Y + b**.
- As Y rises, a/Y falls ⇒ **APC declines as Y increases**.
- **MPC stays constant** at b.

| Concept | Formula | Behaviour as Y rises |
|---|---|---|
| MPC | b = ΔC/ΔY | Constant |
| APC | C/Y = a/Y + b | Declines (toward b) |

#### Autonomous vs Induced Consumption

| Component | Symbol | Drives | Diagram effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autonomous | a | Independent of Y; financed by past savings / dis-saving when Y = 0 | Intercept; ↑a shifts C upward in parallel (a₀→a₁ ⇒ C₀→C₁) |
| Induced | bY | Depends on Y | Slope b; rotates if MPC changes |

#### Triggers for Δa
Increase in **a** (parallel upward shift of C) ⇐ rise in **consumer confidence**, buoyant household expectations about the future.

#### Diagram (Fig. 2.1)
- Axes: Y (horizontal), C (vertical).
- Upward-sloping straight line, intercept = a, slope = b.
- ↑a from a₀ to a₁ ⇒ parallel upward shift C₀ → C₁.

### Definitions
- **Consumption function**: linear relation C = a + bY between planned consumption and aggregate income. ⭐
- **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) = b**: change in consumption induced by a unit change in income; 0 < b < 1. ⭐
- **Average Propensity to Consume (APC) = C/Y**: ratio of aggregate consumption to aggregate income; falls as Y rises. ⭐
- **Autonomous consumption (a)**: consumption that occurs even at zero income, financed by drawing down past saving (dis-saving); a > 0. ⭐
- **Dis-saving**: drawing down past saving to finance current consumption when income is too low. ⭐

### Examples

**Example: C = 20 + 0.25Y (textbook)**
- ΔY = +Rs.100 ⇒ ΔC = 0.25 × 100 = **Rs. 25** (additional consumption).
- ΔS = (1 − 0.25) × 100 = **Rs. 75** (additional saving).
- Symmetric on the downside: ΔY = −Rs.100 ⇒ ΔC = −Rs. 25, ΔS = −Rs. 75.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ MPC and APC are the same. → ✅ MPC = ΔC/ΔY (slope, constant); APC = C/Y (ratio, falls as Y rises).
- ❌ Consumption is zero when income is zero. → ✅ At Y = 0, **C = a** (financed by dis-saving).
- ❌ ↑a rotates the C line. → ✅ ↑a is a **parallel** upward shift; only Δb (MPC) rotates the line.
- ❌ ↑Y ⇒ ↑MPC. → ✅ MPC is **constant** (= b); APC declines.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - C = a + bY; 0 < b < 1; a > 0.
> - MPC = b (constant); APC = a/Y + b (falls as Y↑).
> - Saving out of ΔY = (1 − b)ΔY.
> - Δa shifts C parallel; Δb rotates it.

### Connections
- Builds on: Section 2.2 (closed-economy AD components, above).
- Feeds into: Section 2.3.1 Equilibrium Output (below); Section 2.4.1 Saving Function (Chunk 005: 2.3.2 Investment Multiplier (continuation: Induced Investment)).
- Contrasts with: Classical Y → C transmission via Say's Law (Chunk 003: 1.6 Say's Law of Market) — there income flows automatically into demand; here only fraction b does.

## Section: 2.2.2 Investment Function 🔴

### Core Idea
The **investment function (I)** is planned firm spending on machinery, factories, and other items that add to **productive capacity**. In the simplest Keynesian setup, **I = Ī** (autonomous, fixed). Allowing income-induced investment gives **I = c + dY** (c, d > 0; d = MPI). Adding interest sensitivity gives **I = c + dY − ei** (e > 0). Investment varies **inversely with the rate of interest** because higher i lowers the present value of future returns.

> **In Simple Terms:** Investment = firms putting money into capacity-building stuff. It can be a fixed amount (Ī), grow with income (dY), or shrink with the cost of borrowing (−ei). Higher interest = costlier to fund a project = fewer profitable projects = less I.

### Key Concepts

#### What "investment" means here
- Planned **expenditure by firms on new production equipment** (machinery, buildings, etc.) that adds to productive capacity.
- **Excludes** buying corporate bonds/shares or opening fixed deposits.

#### Three Specifications

| Form | Equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomous only | I = Ī … (2.2a) | Bar = fixed amount; independent of Y |
| Income-induced | I = c + dY … (2.2b) | c > 0 autonomous part; d > 0 = MPI; dY induced by income |
| With interest rate | I = c + dY − ei … (2.2d) | e > 0 captures interest sensitivity |

#### Diagrams (Fig. 2.2a, 2.2b)
- **Fig. 2.2a (Autonomous Investment)**: horizontal line at Ī in (Y, I) space.
- **Fig. 2.2b (Induced Investment)**: upward-sloping line, intercept c, slope d.

#### Why I depends on interest rate (PV argument)
For project costing A, lasting T periods, returning Rt in period t at interest rate i:

**P = R₁/(1+i) + R₂/(1+i)² + R₃/(1+i)³ + … + R_T/(1+i)^T** … (2.2c)

Project is undertaken **only if (P − A) ≥ 0** (profit motive).

Logic chain:
- At i₀: aggregate investment I₀ = sum of all projects with P ≥ A.
- ↑i to i₁ (with Rt and A unchanged) ⇒ P falls.
- Projects that just satisfied P ≥ A may now have P < A ⇒ rejected.
- ⇒ I falls to I₁.
- Hence: i₀ < i₁ ⇒ I₀ > I₁ (inverse relation).

(Note: textbook prints "i0 > i1 we have I0 < I1" — same inverse relation.)

#### Worked PV illustration
Lend Rs. 100 at 10% for 1 year ⇒ receive Rs. 110.
**PV of Rs. 110** at i = 10% = 110 / (1 + 0.10) = **Rs. 100**.

### Definitions
- **Investment (I)**: planned firm expenditure on new production equipment that adds to productive capacity. ⭐
- **Autonomous investment (Ī)**: investment independent of the level of aggregate income; treated as a fixed amount. ⭐
- **Induced investment (dY)**: portion of investment driven by aggregate income. ⭐
- **Marginal Propensity to Invest (MPI) = d**: change in induced investment per unit change in income; d > 0. ⭐
- **Present value (P)**: discounted value of a stream of future returns from a project at the prevailing interest rate. ⭐
- **Interest responsiveness of investment (e)**: coefficient capturing the inverse impact of i on planned investment.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Why ↑i ⇒ ↓I:**
1. ↑i ⇒ denominator (1+i)^t in PV formula rises.
2. P falls for any given stream of Rt and A.
3. Some projects flip from (P ≥ A) ⇒ (P < A).
4. Those projects are dropped.
5. ⇒ Aggregate investment falls.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Investment includes buying shares or fixed deposits. → ✅ Investment in this model = **only physical capacity-building expenditure**; financial purchases are excluded.
- ❌ Autonomous investment cannot vary at all. → ✅ It is independent of **Y**, but can shift with sentiment, technology, or interest rate.
- ❌ Higher interest rates raise investment (savers earn more). → ✅ Higher i raises the **cost of borrowing** and lowers PV of future returns ⇒ I falls.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Investment here is real expenditure — financial portfolio choices are NOT investment in macro accounting.
- Profit motive is the criterion; non-profit motives (e.g., strategic capacity) are abstracted away.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Autonomous: I = Ī; Induced: I = c + dY; With interest: I = c + dY − ei.
> - MPI = d > 0.
> - i ↑ ⇒ PV ↓ ⇒ fewer profitable projects ⇒ I ↓ (inverse relation).

### Connections
- Builds on: Section 2.2 (AD components).
- Feeds into: Section 2.3.1 Equilibrium Output (below); Section 2.3.2 Investment Multiplier (below).
- Contrasts with: Classical investment determined by S = I in capital market (Section 1.4.3, Chunk 003).

## Section: 2.3 Determination of Output in the Keynesian Model 🔴

### Core Idea
The Keynesian model rests on three core assumptions: **unemployed resources / excess capacity**, **wage and price rigidity**, and **homogeneous output** used for both consumption and investment. With these, **output is demand-determined** — supply can passively rise to match any rise in AD at the given price level. Initial setup: a closed three-sector economy (households, firms, government), with autonomous investment.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine factories with idle machines and workers willing to work at the existing wage. Firms can simply produce more if buyers show up. So how much gets made = how much people want to buy.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Assumptions of the Keynesian Model

| # | Assumption | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unemployed resources / underutilised capacity (idle factories, machines, labour) | Output is **demand-determined**; can rise with AD |
| 2 | Money wages and prices are not flexible (wage and price rigidity); workers willing to work at going wage | Increase in supply possible at the given price level |
| 3 | Output is a homogenous commodity used both for consumption and investment | Single Y serves both C and I |

#### Setup of the Simple Keynesian Model
- Three-sector closed economy: households + firms + government.
- No external trade initially (open economy comes later).
- Investment **fully autonomous** initially (relaxed later).
- Government collects taxes — both lump-sum and proportional cases considered.
- Government transfer payments (e.g., pensions, unemployment dole) considered later.

#### AD Equation in the Three-Sector Closed Economy
- **AD = C + I + G**.
- Substituting C = a + b(Y − T), I = Ī:
- **AD = a + b(Y − T) + Ī + G** … (2.3)

### Definitions
- **Demand-determined output**: under excess capacity and price rigidity, equilibrium output is set by the level of aggregate demand, not by supply-side factors. ⭐
- **Wage and price rigidity**: assumption that nominal wages and prices do not adjust freely in the short run. ⭐
- **Homogeneous output assumption**: single composite good usable for both consumption and investment.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Keynesian output is supply-determined like the classical model. → ✅ With excess capacity + price rigidity, output is **demand-determined**.
- ❌ Wage rigidity means wages never change. → ✅ It means wages do not adjust **fast enough** in the short run to clear the labour market.

> **Quick Recall:** Three assumptions = excess capacity, sticky W & P, homogeneous output. Implication: Y is set by AD.

### Connections
- Contrasts with: Classical model (vertical AS at Y_F, output supply-determined; Chunk 002–003).
- Feeds into: Section 2.3.1 Equilibrium Output (below).

## Section: 2.3.1 Determination of Equilibrium Output 🔴

### Core Idea
Equilibrium occurs where **aggregate demand = aggregate supply (= Y)**. At this point household and firm plans (on consumption and investment) are fulfilled and **no unplanned change in inventories** occurs. Solving Y = a + b(Y − T) + Ī + G gives **Yₑ = (a − bT + Ī + G) / (1 − b)**. Equilibrium is **unique** and **stable** — disequilibrium self-corrects via inventory adjustments.

> **In Simple Terms:** The economy settles where what people want to buy equals what's being made. If too little is being made, firms see inventories drop and ramp up. If too much, inventories pile up and firms cut back. Either way, output drifts back to equilibrium.

### Key Concepts

#### Equilibrium Condition
- **Aggregate Supply (Y) = Aggregate Demand (AD)**.
- At Yₑ: household + firm plans realised; no unplanned ΔInventory.

#### Algebra (Three-Sector, Lump-Sum Tax T, Autonomous Ī)
Starting from (2.3): Y = a + b(Y − T) + Ī + G.

Rearranging:
- Y − bY = a − bT + Ī + G.
- (1 − b)Y = a − bT + Ī + G.
- **Yₑ = (a − bT + Ī + G) / (1 − b)** … (2.4)

Since 0 < b < 1, denominator (1 − b) is positive.

#### Comparative Statics — what raises Yₑ
Yₑ is **higher** when:
- ↑ Autonomous investment Ī.
- ↑ Government expenditure G.
- ↑ MPC (b).
(↑T lowers Yₑ.)

#### Two-Sector Special Case (No Government)
Set G = T = 0 in (2.4):
- **Yₑ = (a + Ī) / (1 − b)** … (2.5)

#### Graphical Representation (Keynesian Cross)

**Fig. 2.3 (Two-Sector Economy):**
- Horizontal axis: Y (output / income — used interchangeably).
- Vertical axis: AD and components C, Ī.
- **OL line**: 45° line from origin — locus where vertical distance = horizontal distance (AD = Y).
- **C line**: C = a + bY (slope b, intercept a).
- **AD line**: AD = C + Ī = (a + Ī) + bY — parallel to C, shifted up by Ī.
- **Equilibrium at E**: AD ∩ OL ⇒ AD (= EYₑ) = Y (= OYₑ).
- At point A (where C ∩ OL): consumption AY₁ = income OY₁ ⇒ **saving = 0**.

**Fig. 2.4 (Three-Sector Economy):**
- Same construction; AD = C + Ī + G.
- Equilibrium at A: AD (= AYₑ) = Y (= OYₑ).
- Position of AD line set by autonomous components (Ī, G, a) — higher autonomous components ⇒ higher Yₑ.

#### Disequilibrium Adjustment (Stability)

| Position | Y level | AD vs Y | Inventory effect | Firm response | Movement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Left of E (Y < Yₑ) | OY₁ | AD (GY₁) > Y (AY₁) | Inventory **runs down** | Firms **raise** production next period | Y rises toward Yₑ |
| At E (Y = Yₑ) | OYₑ | AD = Y | Inventory unchanged | No change | Stable |
| Right of E (Y > Yₑ) | OY₂ | AD (JY₂) < Y (HY₂) | Unplanned inventory **accumulation** | Firms **cut** production next period | Y falls toward Yₑ |

⇒ Equilibrium is **unique and stable**; disequilibrium is self-correcting.

### Definitions
- **Equilibrium output (Yₑ)**: level of Y at which AD = Y; plans of households and firms are fulfilled; no unplanned inventory change. ⭐
- **45° line (OL)**: graphical device — every point has vertical = horizontal distance; locus of AD = Y. ⭐
- **Unplanned inventory accumulation**: rise in firm stocks because output exceeds planned demand; signals firms to cut production. ⭐
- **Keynesian Cross**: diagram of AD vs Y intersected by 45° line; equilibrium at intersection.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Self-correction below equilibrium:**
Y < Yₑ → AD > Y → inventories ↓ → firms ↑ production → Y rises → Y → Yₑ.

**Self-correction above equilibrium:**
Y > Yₑ → AD < Y → inventories ↑ (unplanned) → firms ↓ production → Y falls → Y → Yₑ.

### Examples

**Example: Reading Fig. 2.3**
- C cuts OL at A (consumption = income, S = 0).
- AD = C + Ī cuts OL at E ⇒ Yₑ.
- At Y = OY₁ (< Yₑ): AD = GY₁ > Y = AY₁ ⇒ inventory ↓ ⇒ firms produce more.
- At Y = OY₂ (> Yₑ): AD = JY₂ < Y = HY₂ ⇒ inventory ↑ ⇒ firms produce less.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Equilibrium means firms sell zero inventory. → ✅ It means **no unplanned change** in inventories; planned holdings are fine.
- ❌ The slope of AD differs from the slope of C in a two-sector economy. → ✅ AD = C + Ī with autonomous Ī ⇒ AD is **parallel** to C; same slope b.
- ❌ Higher T raises Yₑ. → ✅ ↑T lowers disposable income ⇒ ↓C ⇒ ↓Yₑ (coefficient on T in (2.4) is negative).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Eqm condition: Y = AD = C + I + G.
> - Yₑ = (a − bT + Ī + G) / (1 − b) [three-sector lump-sum].
> - Yₑ = (a + Ī) / (1 − b) [two-sector].
> - 45° line OL ⇔ locus AD = Y.
> - Equilibrium unique and stable; inventories drive adjustment.

### Connections
- Builds on: Section 2.2.1 Consumption (above), Section 2.2.2 Investment (above), Section 2.2 AD components.
- Feeds into: Section 2.3.2 Investment Multiplier (below).
- Contrasts with: Classical Y_F set by labour market + production function (Chunks 002–003).

## Section: 2.3.2 Investment Multiplier 🔴

### Core Idea
A **one-time rise in autonomous investment ΔI** triggers successive rounds of induced consumption (b·ΔI, b²·ΔI, b³·ΔI, …). The total income rise is a **multiple** of ΔI: **ΔY = [1/(1−b)] · ΔI**. The factor **1/(1−b)** is the **investment multiplier**, and it exceeds 1 because 0 < b < 1. Multiplier operation requires the demand-determined-output assumption (excess capacity).

> **In Simple Terms:** One rupee of new investment is income for someone — who spends b of it, becoming income for someone else, who spends b of that. Each round is smaller (because b < 1) but they add up to 1/(1−b) rupees of total income.

### Key Concepts

#### The Result (Two-Sector, Autonomous Investment)
Starting from Yₑ = (a + Ī)/(1 − b):

ΔY = [1/(1 − b)] · ΔI ⇒ **ΔY/ΔI = 1/(1 − b)**.

Since 0 < b < 1: **1/(1 − b) > 1**.

#### The Round-by-Round Mechanism

| Round | Source of demand increase | Magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial ΔI | ΔI |
| 2 | Induced ΔC (income from round 1 spent at MPC b) | b·ΔI |
| 3 | Induced ΔC (round 2 income spent at b) | b²·ΔI |
| 4 | Induced ΔC | b³·ΔI |
| … | … | … |

Total: **ΔY = ΔI · (1 + b + b² + b³ + …) = ΔI · [1/(1 − b)]** … (2.6)

(Geometric series with |b| < 1 sums to 1/(1 − b).)

#### Why Each Round Is Smaller
- MPC < 1 (b < 1) ⇒ b^n → 0 as n grows.
- Process **dies down gradually** ⇒ converges.

#### Diagram (Fig. 2.5)
- Initial equilibrium at A: AD₀ (= C + Ī₀) ∩ OL.
- ↑Ī to Ī₁ ⇒ AD shifts up to AD₁ (= C + Ī₁); ΔI = DE.
- New equilibrium at D: AD₁ ∩ OL.
- ΔY = Y₀Y₁ = AF = DF.
- **DF > DE** ⇒ ΔY > ΔI ⇒ multiplier in action.

#### Induced Investment Case
With I = c + dY [eq. (2.2b)]:
- Y = AD = a + bY + c + dY.
- Solving: **Yₑ = (a + c) / [1 − (b + d)]** … (2.7).
- Multiplier with respect to autonomous Δc: **1 / [1 − (b + d)]**.
- Requires (b + d) < 1 (sum of MPC and MPI less than one).

| Specification | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| Autonomous I only (two-sector) | 1 / (1 − b) |
| Induced I (I = c + dY) | 1 / [1 − (b + d)] |

### Definitions
- **Investment multiplier**: ratio ΔY/ΔI = 1/(1 − b); shows the multiple by which equilibrium income rises in response to a one-time rise in autonomous investment. ⭐
- **Geometric sum (here)**: 1 + b + b² + b³ + … = 1/(1 − b) for 0 < b < 1.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Compact multiplier chain:**
ΔI → 1st-round ΔY = ΔI → induced ΔC = bΔI → 2nd-round ΔY = bΔI → induced ΔC = b²ΔI → … → ΣΔY = ΔI/(1 − b).

### Examples

**Example (textbook logic, b = 0.75)**
- Multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.75) = **4**.
- ΔI = +Rs. 100 ⇒ ΔY = +Rs. 400.
- Round-by-round: 100 + 75 + 56.25 + 42.19 + … → 400.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Multiplier works only when income rises. → ✅ It applies symmetrically to **rises and falls** in autonomous demand.
- ❌ Multiplier of 1/(1 − b) holds even with induced investment. → ✅ With I = c + dY, the multiplier becomes **1/[1 − (b + d)]** — larger.
- ❌ Multiplier process never ends. → ✅ Each round shrinks by factor b; series **converges** because b < 1.
- ❌ Multiplier works in any economic situation. → ✅ It requires **excess capacity** so that supply can passively rise.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- (b + d) < 1 must hold for the induced-investment multiplier to be finite and positive.
- Without excess capacity (full employment), extra demand raises prices, not output ⇒ multiplier story breaks down.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Investment multiplier = 1/(1 − b).
> - Higher MPC ⇒ larger multiplier.
> - With induced I: multiplier = 1/[1 − (b + d)].
> - Mechanism: each rupee of ΔI cycles through induced consumption rounds; geometric series converges.

## Section: 2.3.3 Government Expenditure and Tax Multipliers 🔴

### Core Idea
In a three-sector economy with autonomous I and constant autonomous G, the government collects either a **lump-sum tax T** or a **proportional tax T = tY**. Each tax regime gives different multipliers. Lump-sum: ΔY/ΔG = ΔY/ΔI = 1/(1−b); ΔY/ΔT = −b/(1−b). Balanced-budget multiplier = 1. Proportional tax shrinks the multiplier to 1/[1 − b(1−t)].

> **In Simple Terms:** Government spending shoves more money into the demand stream (full multiplier). Taxes pull money out (negative, smaller-magnitude multiplier). Even if G and T move together by the same amount, GDP rises by exactly that amount (balanced-budget multiplier = 1). And if the tax is on income (not lump sum), every round of the multiplier loses some to taxes — so the multiplier shrinks.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup
- Three-sector closed economy.
- Investment **autonomous** (I = Ī).
- G = constant autonomous.
- Two tax regimes: (i) lump-sum T; (ii) proportional T = tY.
- **Budget deficit B = G − T − TR**, with TR (transfer payments) = 0 here.

#### Lump-Sum Tax — Multipliers

From (2.4): **Yₑ = (a − bT + Ī + G) / (1 − b)**.

| Multiplier | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Government expenditure multiplier | ΔY/ΔG | **1 / (1 − b)** |
| Investment multiplier | ΔY/ΔI | **1 / (1 − b)** |
| Tax multiplier | ΔY/ΔT | **− b / (1 − b)** |

Logic for tax multiplier:
- **T is a leakage** from the income stream.
- ↑T ⇒ ↓Yd ⇒ ↓C ⇒ ↓Y (output is demand-determined).
- Two-way relationship: C ← Y (consumption function) and Y ← C (demand-determined output).

#### Balanced Budget Multiplier (BBM)

Set-up: ↑G and ↑T by **equal amounts** (ΔG = ΔT) ⇒ budget balance unchanged (ΔB = ΔG − ΔT = 0).

Naive guess: zero net effect — injection = leakage.

Result: **ΔY/ΔG = 1** (income rises exactly by ΔG).

Derivation:
- ΔY = (− bΔT + ΔG)/(1 − b).
- Set ΔG = ΔT:
- ΔY = (1 − b)ΔG / (1 − b) = ΔG.
- ⇒ **ΔY/ΔG = 1**. ⭐

#### Proportional Tax — Multipliers

T = tY where 0 < t < 1.
- AD = a + b(Y − tY) + Ī + G = a + b(1 − t)Y + Ī + G.
- Equilibrium: Y = a + b(1 − t)Y + Ī + G … (2.7) [textbook re-uses the (2.7) label]
- **Yₑ = (a + Ī + G) / [1 − b(1 − t)]** … (2.8)

| Multiplier | Lump-sum tax | Proportional tax |
|---|---|---|
| ΔY/ΔG = ΔY/ΔI | 1 / (1 − b) | **1 / [1 − b(1 − t)]** |

Why smaller under proportional tax: in **each multiplier round**, fraction **t** of incremental income is paid as tax ⇒ less feeds the next round of consumption.

#### Tax cuts as policy
- ↑t ⇒ ↓Yₑ ; ↓t ⇒ ↑Yₑ.
- Logic of tax-cut stimulus: lower t ⇒ more disposable income per round ⇒ larger multiplier ⇒ ↑output.
- **Caveat:** requires excess capacity. Without it, ΔAD raises **prices**, not output.

### Definitions
- **Government expenditure multiplier**: ΔY/ΔG = 1/(1 − b) under lump-sum tax. ⭐
- **Tax multiplier**: ΔY/ΔT = − b/(1 − b); negative because T is a leakage. ⭐
- **Balanced Budget Multiplier (BBM)**: ΔY/ΔG = 1 when ΔG = ΔT. ⭐
- **Proportional tax**: T = tY; 0 < t < 1; reduces autonomous-expenditure multiplier to 1/[1 − b(1 − t)]. ⭐
- **Budget deficit B**: B = G − T − TR; with TR = 0 ⇒ B = G − T. ⭐
- **Transfer payments (TR)**: government payments without reciprocal goods/services (e.g., pensions, unemployment dole); set to 0 here.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Tax multiplier chain:**
ΔT ↑ → ΔYd ↓ = ΔT → ΔC ↓ = bΔT → multiplier rounds → total ΔY = − [b/(1 − b)] · ΔT.

**BBM intuition:**
Initial round = full ΔG (injection) − bΔT (induced-consumption loss from tax). Net first-round = (1 − b)ΔG. Multiplied by 1/(1 − b) ⇒ ΔY = ΔG.

### Examples

**Example: Check Your Progress 2 (Q2, textbook answer)**
Given C = 10 + 0.75Y, I = 20, closed economy.
- (a) AD = 30 + 0.75Y; Yₑ = **120**.
- (b) ↑Ī to 25 ⇒ Yₑ = **140**.
- (c) Multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.75) = **4**.
- (d)(i) Lump-sum tax: multiplier still **4**.
- (d)(ii) Proportional tax t = 0.1: multiplier = 1/[1 − 0.75 × 0.9] = **3.08**.
- (e) Open economy with MPM = 0.3: multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.75 + 0.3) = **1/0.55 ≈ 1.8**.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Tax multiplier = government expenditure multiplier in absolute size. → ✅ |Tax multiplier| = b/(1 − b) **<** 1/(1 − b) = G multiplier.
- ❌ BBM = 0 (since injection = leakage). → ✅ **BBM = 1**: ΔG enters fully but ΔT only induces a fraction (b) drop in C, leaving net ΔY = ΔG.
- ❌ Proportional tax raises the multiplier. → ✅ It **lowers** it because fraction t leaks out each round.
- ❌ Tax cut always raises output. → ✅ Only when there is **excess capacity**; else it raises prices.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- BBM = 1 result depends on lump-sum tax + autonomous I + simple Keynesian setup.
- Proportional-tax multiplier requires b(1 − t) < 1 (always true since 0 < b < 1 and 0 < t < 1).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - ΔY/ΔG = ΔY/ΔI = 1/(1 − b) [lump-sum].
> - ΔY/ΔT = − b/(1 − b).
> - **BBM = 1** (ΔG = ΔT case).
> - Proportional-tax multiplier = 1/[1 − b(1 − t)] < 1/(1 − b).

## Section: 2.3.4 Open Economy Multiplier 🔴

### Core Idea
Adding **exports (X)** and **imports (M)** to AD: X is autonomous (depends on foreign income); M is income-induced (M = h + mY) where **m = MPM**. Imports leak demand from domestic stream. Resulting open-economy multiplier = **1/(1 − b + m)** — strictly **smaller** than the closed-economy 1/(1 − b).

> **In Simple Terms:** Exports come in like a gift from abroad (autonomous injection). But every rupee of new domestic income leaks fraction m out to foreign sellers via imports. So domestic demand circulates less powerfully — multiplier shrinks.

### Key Concepts

#### New Variables in the Open Economy
| Variable | Type | Function | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exports (X) | Autonomous | X = X̄ | Depends on foreign income, not domestic Y |
| Imports (M) | Induced | **M = h + mY** | h = autonomous part; m = MPM > 0; leakage |

(Exchange-rate effects on X and M treated as autonomous shifts here — full treatment deferred to International Economics course.)

#### Aggregate Demand & Equilibrium

AD = C + I + G + X − M
   = a + bY + Ī + G + X − h − mY.

Equilibrium: **Y = a + bY + Ī + G + X − h − mY** … (2.9)

⇒ **Yₑ = (a − h + Ī + G + X) / (1 − b + m)** … (2.10)

#### Multipliers in the Open Economy

| Shock | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| ΔI, ΔG, or ΔX | **1 / (1 − b + m)** |

Because m > 0: **(1 − b + m) > (1 − b) ⇒ open-economy multiplier < closed-economy multiplier**.

Reason: fraction m of every income increment is spent on imports ⇒ demand for **domestic** goods rises by less.

### Definitions
- **Marginal Propensity to Import (MPM, m)**: ΔM/ΔY; m > 0; fraction of incremental income spent on imports. ⭐
- **Import demand function**: M = h + mY; h = autonomous import component. ⭐
- **Open-economy multiplier**: 1/(1 − b + m); smaller than the closed-economy 1/(1 − b) due to import leakage. ⭐

### General Multiplier Takeaways (textbook three points)

| # | Principle |
|---|---|
| (i) | Multiplier is **larger** when MPC (b) is larger, MPI (d) larger, t lower, m smaller |
| (ii) | Multiplier applies **symmetrically** to rises and falls in autonomous demand |
| (iii) | Multiplier requires **excess capacity** — output must be demand-determined |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Exports depend on domestic income. → ✅ Exports depend on **foreign** income; from domestic standpoint, autonomous.
- ❌ Imports cancel exports in the multiplier. → ✅ Imports enter as a **per-round leakage** via m, not as a one-time autonomous offset.
- ❌ Open-economy multiplier > closed because of more sources of demand. → ✅ It is **smaller** because every round leaks fraction m.

> **Quick Recall:** Open-economy multiplier = 1/(1 − b + m). m enters in **denominator** as a leakage, like t.

## Section: 2.3.5 Limitations of the Keynesian Model 🟡

### Core Idea
Three boundary conditions on the model:
1. **Recessionary economy with excess capacity required** — supply-constrained economies see **price rise**, not output rise, in response to ΔAD.
2. **Does not apply near full employment** — ΔY at full employment requires ↑factor & goods prices.
3. **Short-run framework** — wage contracts and printed prices are sticky for limited periods only.

### Key Concepts

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Supply bottlenecks | ↑AD ⇒ ↑P, not ↑Y |
| Full employment | ↑Y needs ↑factor prices ⇒ leaves Keynesian world |
| Short run only | Wage/price rigidity rationalised by contracts and catalogues, valid for "some time" only |

### Definitions
- **Supply constraints / bottlenecks**: limits on output expansion that force ΔAD to manifest as ΔP rather than ΔY. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:** Keynesian model = recession + excess capacity + short run. Outside this, multiplier story breaks down.

## Section: 2.4 An Alternative View of Equilibrium 🔴

### Core Idea
Equilibrium in the Keynesian model can be **equivalently** stated as **planned saving = planned investment** (S = I). The decision to consume more is the same as the decision to save less. Saving function mirrors consumption.

> **In Simple Terms:** Same equilibrium, different lens. Instead of "AD = Y", say "leakages (S) = injections (I)". Two equivalent ways of expressing the same fixed point.

## Section: 2.4.1 Saving Function 🔴

### Core Idea
In the two-sector model, Y = C + S. Subtracting the consumption function C = a + bY from Y gives **S = − a + (1 − b)Y**. Negative intercept = dis-saving at low Y. Slope (1 − b) = **MPS**. Mirror image of consumption function. **MPS = 1 − MPC**.

### Key Concepts

#### Identity & Derivation
- Two-sector identity: **Y = C + S** … (2.11).
- "Decision to consume more = decision to save less."
- S = Y − (a + bY) = **− a + (1 − b)Y** … (2.12).

#### Saving function properties

| Property | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Intercept | − a (negative) | Dis-saving at Y = 0 |
| Slope | 1 − b | **MPS** |
| MPS = 1 − MPC | (1 − b) | Higher MPC ⇒ lower MPS |
| Empirical assumption | Richer households higher MPS | Standard Keynesian distributional claim |

#### Diagrammatic Derivation (Fig. 2.6)

Two-panel diagram:
- **Top panel**: consumption function C = a + bY with 45° line OL.
- **Bottom panel**: saving function S = − a + (1 − b)Y; intercept − a; crosses x-axis at Y₂.

Three-income-level reading:

| Income level | C vs Y (top) | S in bottom panel |
|---|---|---|
| Y = OY₁ (low) | C = AY₁ > Y = BY₁ | S = − Y₁G (negative; dis-saving) |
| Y = OY₂ (mid) | C = DY₂ = Y = OY₂ (C ∩ OL) | **S = 0** (function crosses x-axis) |
| Y = OY₃ (high) | C < Y | S = HY₃ > 0 |

### Definitions
- **Saving function**: S = − a + (1 − b)Y; mirror image of consumption function. ⭐
- **Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)**: 1 − b = ΔS/ΔY; slope of saving function; MPS = 1 − MPC. ⭐
- **Dis-saving** (re-iterated): negative saving at low Y; financed by drawing down past savings.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ S can be negative only if income is negative. → ✅ S is negative whenever **Y < a/(1 − b)**, i.e., below the break-even income (where C cuts the 45° line).
- ❌ MPS is constant only when MPC varies. → ✅ With linear C, **both MPC and MPS are constant**; what falls is APC (and rises is APS).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Y = C + S.
> - S = − a + (1 − b)Y.
> - MPS = 1 − MPC.
> - S = 0 at Y = a/(1 − b) (where C cuts OL).

## Section: 2.4.2 Determination of Equilibrium Output (S = I View) 🔴

### Core Idea
**Two-sector**: equilibrium ⇒ S = Ī. **Three-sector**: S + T = Ī + G, equivalently **S + (T − G) = Ī**. Saving here = household saving + public saving (T − G). When private saving exceeds private investment (S > I), the government runs a **budget deficit (G > T)**, financed by borrowing from households.

> **In Simple Terms:** Equilibrium = leakages (saving + taxes) match injections (investment + spending). When households save more than firms invest, the government soaks up the difference by running a deficit and borrowing it.

### Key Concepts

#### Two-Sector Derivation
- AD-equilibrium: Y = C + Ī.
- Income identity: Y = C + S.
- Combine: C + S = C + Ī ⇒ **S = Ī** … (2.13).
- "Saving = abstaining from current consumption ⇒ frees resources for producing investment goods."

#### Fig. 2.7 — Saving-Investment Equality
- Axes: Y (X), S/I (Y).
- Saving function S = − a + (1 − b)Y upward-sloping (intercept − a).
- Investment Ī horizontal line.
- **Equilibrium Yₑ at S ∩ Ī**.

#### Three-Sector Equilibrium
- Y = C + I + G (output uses).
- Y = C + S + T (income uses).
- Combine: C + S + T = C + Ī + G ⇒ **S + T = Ī + G** … (2.14).
- Rewrite: **S + (T − G) = Ī** ⇒ planned investment = household saving + **public saving (T − G)**.
- Or: **(S − I) = (G − T)**.

#### Implication of (S − I) = (G − T)

| Sign of (S − I) | Sign of (G − T) | Government position |
|---|---|---|
| > 0 (private saving exceeds private I) | > 0 | **Budget deficit**; financed by household borrowing |
| < 0 (private I exceeds private S) | < 0 | **Budget surplus** |

### Definitions
- **S = I equilibrium (two-sector)**: closed economy without government clears at planned saving = planned investment. ⭐
- **Public saving**: T − G; positive in surplus, negative in deficit. ⭐
- **(S − I) = (G − T)**: identity linking private saving-investment gap to government deficit. ⭐
- **Budget deficit financing (Keynesian)**: government borrows from households to fund deficit.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 2-sector: **S = Ī**.
> - 3-sector: **S + T = Ī + G** ⇔ **S + (T − G) = Ī** ⇔ **(S − I) = (G − T)**.

## Section: 2.4.3 Paradox of Thrift 🔴

### Core Idea
**At the household level**, saving is a virtue. **At the aggregate level**, an increase in the desire to save can leave aggregate saving unchanged (with autonomous I) or even **lower** (with induced I). Reason: ↑desired saving ⇒ ↓planned C ⇒ ↓AD ⇒ ↓Y ⇒ at lower Y, actual saving is no higher / lower than before.

> **In Simple Terms:** Each household saving more is good for that household. But if everyone simultaneously decides to save more, they all spend less, firms see weaker demand, output falls, incomes fall, and the total saving pool either doesn't grow at all — or actually shrinks. What's virtuous individually is vicious collectively.

### Key Concepts

#### Case (i) — Autonomous Investment (Fig. 2.8)
- Initial: equilibrium A; **S = Ī**; output = Y₀.
- Shock: autonomous saving ↑ ⇒ saving function shifts **upward in parallel** from S₀ = − a₀ + (1 − b)Y to S₁ = − a₁ + (1 − b)Y (a₁ > a₀; same MPS ⇒ parallel).
- New equilibrium D at lower Y₁ (where S₁ = Ī).
- **Planned saving at new equilibrium DY₁ = Ī = AY₀** (unchanged).
- **Income falls to OY₁** to restore S = I.
- **Result: aggregate saving unchanged, output lower.**

#### Case (ii) — Induced Investment (Fig. 2.9)
- I = c + dY (induced).
- Saving shifts S₀ → S₁ ⇒ ↓C ⇒ ↓Y.
- ↓Y ⇒ ↓induced **investment** as well (since I = c + dY).
- Equilibrium income falls further.
- **Saving at new lower equilibrium BY₁ < AY₀**.
- **Result: aggregate saving actually DECREASES.** ⭐

#### Comparison Table

| Investment regime | Effect of ↑desired saving on Y | Effect on aggregate S |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomous (I = Ī) | Falls (Y₁ < Y₀) | **Unchanged** (S new = Ī = S old) |
| Induced (I = c + dY) | Falls more (Y₁ < Y₀; I also falls) | **Falls** (S new < S old) |

### Definitions
- **Paradox of thrift / saving paradox**: in the Keynesian short run, ↑propensity to save leaves aggregate saving unchanged or lower. ⭐
- **Parallel saving shift**: ↑autonomous saving raises intercept (− a₀ → − a₁) without changing MPS ⇒ parallel upward shift. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Paradox-of-thrift chain (autonomous I):**
↑desire to save → ↓planned C → ↓AD → ↓Y → S returns to original I level.

**Paradox of thrift (induced I):**
↑desire to save → ↓planned C → ↓Y → ↓induced I → further ↓AD → further ↓Y → **S falls below original**.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ The paradox holds in the long run too. → ✅ **Short-run only**. In long run, higher saving funds higher investment and capacity (covered in Growth course).
- ❌ With induced investment, saving still ends unchanged. → ✅ With induced I, saving **falls** because ↓Y also reduces induced I.
- ❌ The paradox refutes individual saving advice. → ✅ It refutes the **aggregation** of individual saving advice — a fallacy of composition.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Paradox depends on **excess capacity**. With supply constraints, ↓AD wouldn't translate fully into ↓Y.
- Long-run growth perspective: higher saving ⇒ higher investment ⇒ more productive capacity (different model).

> **Quick Recall:**

> - Short-run phenomenon only.

## Section: 2.5 Liquidity Preference 🔴

### Core Idea
Demand for money in the Keynesian system is the demand for **real balances** (nominal money / price level). Since prices are constant in the simple Keynesian model, money demand changes = real-balance changes. Keynes's contribution: money demand is **interest-sensitive**.

> **In Simple Terms:** "Liquidity preference" = how much cash people want to hold instead of buying interest-bearing assets. Keynes's twist: interest rates affect this — high interest = costly to hold cash, so demand for cash drops.

### Key Concepts
- **Real balances** = M/P. With P fixed ⇒ ΔM = ΔReal balances.
- Demand for money = function of Y and i.

### Definitions
- **Liquidity preference**: Keynesian demand for money. ⭐
- **Real balances**: nominal money supply deflated by price level (M/P). ⭐

### Connections
- Contrasts with: Classical money demand (transactions only, MV = PT) (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory).
- Feeds into: Section 2.5.1 (below).

## Section: 2.5.1 Components of Demand for Money 🔴

### Core Idea
Three motives: **transactions**, **precautionary**, **speculative**. Transactions and precautionary demand rise with **income**. Speculative demand falls with **interest rate**. Combining: **L = qY − ti**, q > 0, t > 0. Money market equilibrium at L = M/P; ↑M/P ⇒ ↓i.

> **In Simple Terms:** People hold cash for three reasons: to buy stuff (transactions), in case of emergencies (precautionary), and to bet against bond prices (speculative). The first two scale with income. The third moves opposite to interest rates: high i = bonds tempting = less cash.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Motives — Comparison Table

| Motive | Driver | Direction | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transactions** | Income (Y) | ↑Y ⇒ ↑L | Money is medium of exchange; richer people make more transactions |
| **Precautionary** | Income (Y) | ↑Y ⇒ ↑L | Need cash for contingencies (medical, accident) |
| **Speculative** | Interest rate (i) | ↑i ⇒ ↓L | Inverse of bond-price expectations / opportunity cost |

#### Speculative Demand — Two-Asset World (Money + Bonds)

**Bonds**: pay interest + offer capital-gain potential.
**Money**: zero return; only useful for transactions.
**Inverse relation**: ↑bond price ⇒ ↓i (and vice versa).

**High i scenario:**
- Expectation: i will fall ⇒ bond prices will rise.
- Strategy: hold bonds in expectation of capital gains.
- ⇒ Money demand **low**.

**Low i scenario:**
- Expectation: i will rise ⇒ bond prices will fall.
- Strategy: sell bonds (avoid capital loss) ⇒ hold money.
- ⇒ Money demand **high**.

⇒ Speculative L is **inversely related** to i.

#### Alternative Justification — Opportunity Cost
- ↑i ⇒ ↑opportunity cost of holding money (forgone interest).
- ⇒ ↓money demand.
- (Inverse i–L relation derivable **without** speculative motive.)

#### Money Demand Function
**L = qY − ti** … (2.15)

- q > 0 (income sensitivity).
- t > 0 (interest sensitivity, slope magnitude).
- L = real balances.

#### Fig. 2.10 — Money Demand Curve
- Axes: i (vertical), L and M/P (horizontal).
- Downward-sloping line **L(Y₀) = qY₀ − ti** for given Y₀.
- Movement **along** L(Y₀): ↓i (i₀ → i₁) ⇒ ↑L (speculative-demand response).
- **Shifts:** ↑Y ⇒ rightward shift (transactions + precautionary up); ↓Y ⇒ leftward shift.
- Money supply: vertical line at **M^s/P** (real money supply). Central bank sets M^s.
- Equilibrium: intersection of L and M^s/P.
- ↑M^s/P from (M^s/P)₀ to (M^s/P)₁ ⇒ **i falls** from i₀ to i₁.

### Definitions
- **Transactions demand for money**: cash held to make payments; rises with Y. ⭐
- **Precautionary demand for money**: cash held against contingencies; rises with Y. ⭐
- **Speculative demand for money**: cash held in anticipation of asset-price (bond-price) movements; inversely related to i. ⭐
- **Money demand function**: L = qY − ti; q, t > 0. ⭐
- **Opportunity cost of holding money**: interest income forgone by holding cash. ⭐
- **Real money supply**: M^s/P; set by central bank in nominal terms. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Speculative chain (high i):**
i high → expect i ↓ → expect bond prices ↑ → buy bonds for capital gain → ↓money demand.

**Speculative chain (low i):**
i low → expect i ↑ → expect bond prices ↓ → sell bonds to avoid loss → ↑money demand.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Money demand depends only on income. → ✅ Also on **interest rate** (Keynesian innovation).
- ❌ Bond prices and interest rates move together. → ✅ **Inverse relation**: ↑i ⇒ ↓bond price.
- ❌ Speculative demand is the only reason for inverse i–L relation. → ✅ Opportunity-cost argument also delivers it.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three motives: transactions (Y↑), precautionary (Y↑), speculative (i↓).
> - **L = qY − ti**.
> - ↑Y ⇒ rightward shift; ↓i ⇒ movement along the curve (rightward).
> - ↑M^s ⇒ ↓i (in non-trap equilibrium).

### Connections
- Builds on: Classical QTM money demand (Chunk 002: 1.3 Basic Features of Classical Theory) — rejected here.
- Feeds into: Section 2.5.2 Liquidity Trap (below); Unit 3 LM curve (Chunk 006: 2.5.1 (closing) — Speculative Demand & Money Demand Function).

## Section: 2.5.2 Liquidity Trap 🔴

### Core Idea
At a sufficiently low rate of interest **i\***, demand for money becomes **infinitely elastic** (horizontal). Everyone expects i to rise (bond prices to fall) ⇒ no one wants to hold bonds. Any extra liquidity is absorbed by money-holding ⇒ **monetary policy cannot lower i below i\*** ⇒ monetary policy ineffective. **Fiscal policy remains effective.**

> **In Simple Terms:** When interest rates hit rock bottom, everyone is convinced rates can only go up — meaning bond prices can only fall. So nobody buys bonds. Central bank floods the system with cash; cash gets hoarded; interest rate doesn't budge. The central bank's main lever stops working.

### Key Concepts

#### Conditions for the Trap
- i has fallen so low and bond prices so high that **everyone expects i to rise**.
- Fear of capital loss on bonds.
- All extra liquidity willingly absorbed at i\*.
- In (2.16) terms: **t → ∞** (infinite interest sensitivity).

#### Fig. 2.11 — Liquidity Trap
- Axes: i (vertical), L and M/P (horizontal).
- Money demand curve: **horizontal at i\***.
- Money supply (M^s/P)₀ → (M^s/P)₁ shifted rightward.
- **i unchanged at i\***.

#### Implications

| Policy | Effective in trap? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional monetary policy (↑M^s) | **Ineffective** | i stuck at i\*; no transmission to investment |
| Fiscal policy (↑G) | **Very effective** | Direct demand injection; no leakage via i |

#### Real-World Episode
- **Global financial crisis 2007–08**.
- USA: i hit **zero** floor.
- Japan: i hit **almost zero**.
- ⇒ Conventional monetary policy unable to lower i further.
- Resorted to **unconventional monetary policy** (e.g., QE-type measures).
- Also used fiscal stimulus, which raised public-debt-sustainability concerns.

### Definitions
- **Liquidity trap**: situation at very low i where money demand is infinitely interest-elastic; ↑M^s does not lower i; conventional monetary policy is ineffective. ⭐
- **Infinitely elastic money demand**: L curve horizontal at i\*; t → ∞. ⭐
- **Unconventional monetary policy**: non-rate-based tools used when i is at the floor (e.g., asset purchases). ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ In a trap, monetary policy still raises Y via lower i. → ✅ i is **stuck**; conventional MP fails.
- ❌ Fiscal policy is also ineffective in a trap. → ✅ Fiscal policy is **most effective** in a trap (no crowding out via i).
- ❌ Liquidity trap is purely theoretical. → ✅ USA 2008 (i = 0) and Japan documented real-world episodes.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Trap = horizontal L at i\*; t → ∞.
> - Monetary policy ineffective; **fiscal policy effective**.
> - Real episode: GFC 2007–08, USA & Japan.

## Section: 2.6 Role of Government 🔴

### Core Idea
Sharp contrast with classical view: Keynes argues government must play an **active role**. Wage/price rigidities and effective-demand deficiency justify intervention. Recommended: ↑public expenditure, run deficits, use fiscal + monetary policy to inject AD. Especially crucial in a **liquidity trap**, where monetary policy fails and **fiscal policy is the only viable tool**. Real-world precedent: Great Depression, GFC 2007–08, COVID-19 (2020–21).

> **In Simple Terms:** Classicals say "government, get out of the way." Keynes says "government, get in there and spend." When recession bites, falling wages don't fix it (they make it worse by reducing purchasing power). The cure is for the government to write big cheques.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical vs Keynesian Government Role — Side-by-Side

| Aspect | Classical | Keynesian |
|---|---|---|
| Government scope | Law/order, justice, defence only | **Active demand management** |
| Price/wage assumption | Fully flexible | Rigid in short run |
| Market clearing | Always at full employment | May fail (effective-demand deficiency) |
| Recession remedy | Wait for prices to adjust | Public expenditure / deficit budget / fiscal & monetary policy |
| View of falling wages in slump | Helps clear labour market | **Worsens** recession (↓purchasing power ⇒ ↓effective demand) |

#### Why Falling Wages Worsen a Recession (Keynesian)
1. High unemployment ⇒ tendency for wages to fall.
2. ↓W ⇒ ↓household income ⇒ ↓purchasing power.
3. ↓effective demand ⇒ ↓Y ⇒ ↑unemployment.
4. ⇒ **Self-reinforcing downward spiral**.

#### Recommended Government Tools
- **Fiscal**: ↑G (deficit budget); tax cuts.
- **Monetary**: ↑M^s.
- **Note**: Keynes warned about monetary-policy limits, esp. in liquidity trap ⇒ **discretionary fiscal policy preferred** in deep slumps.

#### Historical Episodes Cited

| Episode | Year(s) | Keynesian application |
|---|---|---|
| Great Depression | 1929–33 | Made the case for activist government |
| Global Financial Crisis | 2007–08 | Expansionary fiscal policy worldwide; widening deficits, high public debt |
| COVID-19 | 2020–21 | Acute AD shortage; aggressive fiscal response |

#### Side-Effects / Concerns of Deficit Spending

| Concern | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Public debt build-up | Persistent deficits accumulate |
| Interest burden | High debt service ⇒ wider deficits ⇒ debt-sustainability worry |
| **Crowding out** | High interest rate ⇒ ↓private investment |

### Definitions
- **Activist fiscal policy**: discretionary use of G and T to manage AD; central Keynesian prescription. ⭐
- **Deficit budget**: G > T; financed by borrowing; Keynesian counter-recession tool. ⭐
- **Crowding out**: high interest rates from public borrowing displace private investment. ⭐
- **Debt sustainability concern**: rising public debt + high interest burden raises doubts about future repayment. ⭐
- **Effective demand deficiency**: shortfall in planned spending; underlying cause of recession in Keynesian view. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Falling wages cure unemployment in Keynesian model. → ✅ They **deepen** the recession via ↓purchasing power ⇒ ↓effective demand.
- ❌ Keynes preferred monetary over fiscal policy. → ✅ He flagged monetary-policy limits (esp. liquidity trap); **discretionary fiscal** preferred.
- ❌ Crowding out is a Keynesian central concern. → ✅ It's the side-effect critics raise; arises from high interest rate due to public borrowing.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Deficit-financed expansion exhausts policy room over time (debt sustainability).
- Crowding out limits the multiplier in non-trap states; in liquidity trap, no crowding out (i can't rise).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Keynes ⇒ active government (G ↑, deficits OK).
> - Liquidity trap ⇒ fiscal > monetary.
> - Side-effects: debt burden, crowding out.
> - Episodes: 1929–33; 2007–08; 2020–21 COVID.

- **Liquidity preference (L)**: ↑Y ⇒ ↑L; ↑i ⇒ ↓L. Inverse i–L explained by speculative motive.
- **Liquidity trap**: at very low i, L infinitely elastic; conventional MP ineffective.

## Section: 3.2 Equilibrium in the Real Sector — Algebra 🔴

### Core Idea
Real-sector equilibrium ⇒ AD = Y. Combining linear C, interest-sensitive I, and exogenous G/T yields a closed-form **IS equation** linking Y and i with an inverse slope. Slope and intercept depend on b, c, d, e, G, T.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup (closed economy, lump-sum tax)
- **Y = C + I + G**.
- **C = a + b(Y − T) = a + b·Y_d** … (3.1).
- **I = c + dY − e·i** … (3.2) (induced + interest-sensitive).
- **G = Ḡ** (exogenous, constant).
- **T = T̄** (lump-sum, exogenous).

> Note: with P fixed, **nominal i = real i**.

#### Equilibrium Equation Derivation

Substitute into Y = AD:
**Y = a + b(Y − T̄) + (c + dY − e·i) + Ḡ** … (3.3)

Re-arrange:
Y − bY − dY = a − bT̄ + c − e·i + Ḡ

⇒ **Y = (a + c + Ḡ − bT̄)/(1 − b − d) − [e/(1 − b − d)] · i** … (3.4)

#### General-Form IS Equation

**Y = α_G · (Ā − e·i)** … (3.4a)

where:
- **α_G = 1 / (1 − b − d)** (autonomous-spending multiplier).
- **Ā = a + c + Ḡ − bT̄** (aggregate autonomous spending).

#### Slope of IS

Express equation as i in terms of Y:
- **Slope di/dY = − (1 − b − d)/e** ⭐ (negative).

> Magnitude = (1 − b − d)/e.

#### Slope Determinants

| Parameter change | Effect on |di/dY| | IS slope |
|---|---|---|
| ↑b (MPC) or ↑d (MPI) | ↓ | **Flatter** |
| ↑e (interest sensitivity of I) | ↓ | **Flatter** |
| ↓b, ↓d, ↓e | ↑ | **Steeper** |

> **Flatter IS** = economy more sensitive to i.
> **Steeper IS** = economy insensitive to i.

### Definitions
- **IS curve**: locus of (Y, i) combinations where **real (goods) sector is in equilibrium** (S = I, or AD = Y). ⭐
- **Aggregate autonomous spending Ā**: a + c + Ḡ − bT̄. ⭐
- **Autonomous spending multiplier α_G**: 1/(1 − b − d).
- **Interest-sensitivity coefficient e**: ΔI per unit Δi (with negative sign).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Y = α_G(Ā − e·i)**.
> - α_G = 1/(1 − b − d).
> - Ā = a + c + Ḡ − bT̄.
> - Slope di/dY = − (1 − b − d)/e.

- Feeds into: 3.2.1 Geometric IS derivation (below).

## Section: 3.2.1 Derivation of the IS Curve (Geometric) 🔴

### Core Idea
Two-panel Fig. 3.1 shows: at lower i, autonomous expenditure (via I) rises ⇒ Keynesian-Cross equilibrium output rises. Plotting (Y, i) pairs from two interest rates produces a **downward-sloping IS curve**. Each point on IS = real-sector equilibrium with **I = S**.

### Key Concepts

#### Fig. 3.1 — Two-Panel Construction

| Panel | Axes | Curve | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | Y horizontal; AE vertical | AE₀, AE₁ vs 45° line | At i₀ ⇒ AE₀ ∩ 45° = a (Y₀); at i₁ ⇒ AE₁ ∩ 45° = b (Y₁) |
| (b) | Y horizontal; i vertical | IS | Plot a (Y₀, i₀) and b (Y₁, i₁); join ⇒ downward-sloping IS |

#### Mechanism (↓i ⇒ ↑Y)

↓i (i₀ → i₁) → ↑I (interest-sensitive component) → ↑AE (AE₀ → AE₁) → 45°-line equilibrium shifts a → b → ↑Y (Y₀ → Y₁).

⇒ Y and i **inversely related** ⇒ IS slopes downward.

#### What IS Represents
- Each point on IS ⇒ **equilibrium in the real (goods) sector**.
- ⇒ **I = S** at every point on IS.
- ⇒ AD = Y at every point on IS.

> **In Simple Terms:** The IS curve is just a "scoreboard" of all (Y, i) pairs at which the goods market clears. Lower i → cheaper to invest → bigger AD → bigger Y. Plot the (i, Y) pairs and join them.

### Definitions
- **IS curve**: locus of (Y, i) where I = S (real-sector equilibrium). ⭐
- **Real (goods) sector equilibrium**: AD = Y; equivalently I = S.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ IS is upward-sloping. → ✅ **Downward-sloping** (↓i ⇒ ↑Y).
- ❌ IS plots interest rate against money supply. → ✅ Plots i against output Y.
- ❌ Each IS point is a unique market state. → ✅ Each point is **goods-market equilibrium**; needs LM for full equilibrium.

## Section: 3.2.2 Shift in the IS Curve 🔴

### Core Idea
**Position** and **slope** of IS depend on parameters in (3.4). Shifts come from changes in **G, T, a, c**. Slope changes from changes in **b, d, e**.

### Key Concepts

#### Position Shifts (parallel)

| Change | Effect | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| ↑Ḡ | **IS shifts up/right (parallel)** | Intercept (a + c + Ḡ − bT̄) rises |
| ↓Ḡ | IS shifts down/left | Intercept falls |
| ↑T̄ | **IS shifts down/left** | Intercept falls (− bT̄) |
| ↓T̄ | IS shifts up/right | Intercept rises |
| ↑a or ↑c | IS shifts up/right (parallel) | Intercept rises |
| ↓a or ↓c | IS shifts down/left (parallel) | Intercept falls |

#### Slope Changes (rotation)

| Change | Effect on slope di/dY |
|---|---|
| ↑MPC (b) | |slope| ↓ ⇒ **flatter** |
| ↓MPC (b) | |slope| ↑ ⇒ steeper |
| ↑MPI (d) | flatter |
| ↑e | flatter |

> Textbook explicitly notes: "A change in marginal propensity to consume affects the **slope** of the IS curve."

#### CYP 1 Q1 logic — ↓MPC (b) ⇒ steeper IS
- |slope| = (1 − b − d)/e rises when b falls.
- IS pivots **steeper**.

#### CYP 1 Q2 logic
- (i) ↓Ḡ ⇒ IS shifts **down/left**.
- (ii) ↓tax rate (or ↓T̄) ⇒ IS shifts **up/right**.

### Definitions
- **IS shift**: parallel movement caused by change in autonomous components (a, c, Ḡ, T̄).
- **IS rotation (slope change)**: pivot caused by change in b, d, or e.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Up-right shifters**: ↑Ḡ, ↑a, ↑c, ↓T̄.
> - **Down-left shifters**: ↓Ḡ, ↑T̄, ↓a, ↓c.
> - **Flatter** IS: ↑b, ↑d, ↑e.

## Section: 3.3 Equilibrium in the Monetary Sector — Setup 🔴

### Core Idea
LM-curve derivation uses Keynesian split: **transactions + precautionary** demand depend on Y; **speculative** demand depends on i. Combined money demand: **L = kY − h·i**. Money supply set by **central bank**, vertical line at M^s/P. Three textbook reminders: (1) **opportunity cost** of holding money rises with i; (2) money demand is for a **given Y**; ↑Y shifts L right; (3) supply M^s **fixed in short run**.

### Key Concepts

#### Money Demand Function (Eq. 3.5)
**L = kY − h·i**, where k > 0, h > 0.
- L = demand for **real balances** (M^d/P).
- Y = aggregate income.
- i = rate of interest.

> Same shape as L = qY − ti from Unit 2; **textbook now uses (k, h) in place of (q, t)**.

#### Three Textbook Reminders About the Monetary Sector

| # | Reminder | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Opportunity cost of holding money = forgone interest | ↑i ⇒ ↓L (downward-sloping demand curve) |
| (ii) | Money demand specified for **given Y**; ↑Y ⇒ parallel rightward shift | ↑transactions + wealth ⇒ more cash desired |
| (iii) | Money supply set by **central bank**; vertical line; constant in short run | ↑M^s ⇒ vertical line shifts right |

> Textbook re-flags **liquidity trap** here: at very low i, opportunity cost is **negligible** ⇒ people hold all their money in cash.

### Definitions
- **L = kY − h·i** (Eq. 3.5): real money demand function used in Unit 3.
- **Real balances (L)**: M^d/P.
- **Real money supply**: M^s/P; vertical line; set by central bank.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - L = kY − h·i; both k and h positive.
> - ↑Y shifts L right; ↑i moves along L (rightward as i falls).
> - M^s vertical; ↑M^s shifts right.

## Section: 3.3.1 Derivation of the LM Curve 🔴

### Core Idea
Money-market equilibrium: **L = M^s/P = M̄/P̄**. Setting L = M̄/P̄ and rearranging gives an **upward-sloping** LM in (Y, i). Geometric derivation: at higher Y, money demand shifts right ⇒ equilibrium i rises. Plotting (Y, i) pairs traces LM. Slope = k/h.

### Key Concepts

#### Equilibrium Condition (Eq. 3.6)
**M̄/P̄ = kY − h·i**

Re-arranged (Eq. 3.6a):
**Y = − (1/h)·(M̄/P̄) + (h/k)·i**

> Wait — textbook prints: Y = − (1/h)·M̄/P̄ + (h/k)·i. (Verbatim from p. 59. Treated as the LM equation in this textbook's algebra.)

#### Slope of LM
- Express in terms of i, take di/dY.
- **Slope = k/h** ⭐ (positive).

#### Slope Cases (limit cases)

| Speculative-demand sensitivity | h value | LM slope k/h | LM shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfectly elastic to i (liquidity trap) | h → ∞ | → 0 | **Horizontal** |
| Perfectly inelastic to i | h = 0 | → ∞ | **Vertical** |
| Intermediate | finite h > 0 | finite positive | **Upward-sloping** |

#### Fig. 3.3 — Two-Panel Construction

| Panel | Axes | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | x: M/P, y: i | M^d/P(Y₀) ∩ M̄/P at point a (i₀, Y₀); ↑Y ⇒ shifts to M^d/P(Y₁); intersects M̄/P at point b (i₁, Y₁) |
| (b) | x: Y, y: i | Plot a (Y₀, i₀) and b (Y₁, i₁); join ⇒ **upward-sloping LM** |

#### Mechanism (↑Y ⇒ ↑i)

↑Y → ↑transactions/precautionary L → money demand curve shifts right (M^d/P(Y₀) → M^d/P(Y₁)) → with M^s constant → ↑i (i₀ → i₁) clears market.

⇒ Y and i **positively related** ⇒ LM slopes upward.

> **In Simple Terms:** As income rises, people want more cash for daily transactions. Money supply is fixed, so the only way to "ration" the limited supply is for the interest rate to rise — which dampens speculative demand. Plot (Y, i) pairs ⇒ upward-sloping LM.

### Off-LM Points (Disequilibrium in Monetary Sector)

| Position vs LM | State of money market | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Left of LM** (e.g., point c) | **Excess supply** of money (L < M^s/P) | People buy bonds ⇒ ↑bond prices ⇒ **↓i** |
| **Right of LM** (e.g., point d) | **Excess demand** for money (L > M^s/P) | People sell bonds ⇒ ↓bond prices ⇒ **↑i** |

### Definitions
- **LM curve**: locus of (Y, i) where money market is in equilibrium (L = M^s/P). ⭐
- **Slope of LM**: k/h.
- **Vertical LM** (h = 0): money demand insensitive to i.
- **Horizontal LM** (h → ∞): liquidity trap.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - LM equilibrium: **M̄/P̄ = kY − h·i**.
> - Slope di/dY = **k/h** > 0.
> - Vertical if h = 0; horizontal in liquidity trap.
> - **Left of LM** ⇒ excess supply of money.
> - **Right of LM** ⇒ excess demand for money.

- Feeds into: 3.3.2 LM shifts; 3.4 IS-LM.

## Section: 3.4.1 Shocks in the IS-LM Model 🔴

### Core Idea
Demand shocks shift IS; some shocks (e.g., money-demand shifts from changing payment habits) shift LM. Slope of IS and LM determine **size** of Y and i response. **Flatter LM ⇒ monetary policy more effective**; steeper LM ⇒ less effective.

### Key Concepts

#### Worked Examples (textbook)

| Shock | What shifts | Direction | Effect on Y | Effect on i |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stock-market boom** ⇒ ↑household wealth ⇒ ↑C | IS | Right (IS₀ → IS₁) | ↑ (Y₀ → Y₁) | ↑ |
| **↓business confidence** ⇒ ↓I | IS | Left | ↓ | ↓ |
| **Online financial fraud** ⇒ ↓online transactions ⇒ ↑demand for money | LM | Right (LM₀ → LM₁) | ↑ (Y₀ → Y₂) | ↓ (i₀ → i₂) |

> **Subtle point:** the textbook says fraud causes LM to shift **right** because demand for cash (cash-preference) rises but it then says output rises and i falls — this is the textbook's framing; treat as given (Fig. 3.4 reading).

#### Effectiveness Depends on Slope

| LM slope | Monetary policy effectiveness | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Flatter** LM | **High** — small ↓i ⇒ large ↑Y | Speculative demand very interest-elastic |
| **Steeper** LM | **Low** — large ↓i needed for small ↑Y | Money demand interest-inelastic |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Stock-market boom shifts LM. → ✅ It shifts **IS** (via consumption / household wealth).
- ❌ Steeper LM ⇒ stronger MP. → ✅ Steeper LM ⇒ **weaker** monetary policy.

## Section: 3.4.2 Adjustment Process in the Economy 🔴

### Core Idea
If economy is **not at IS ∩ LM**, an **adjustment process** drives it back. Two driving forces: (a) excess money demand/supply ⇒ Δi (via bond market); (b) excess goods demand/supply ⇒ ΔY (via inventories). Real and monetary sectors are **inter-related**: real shocks spill into monetary, and vice versa.

### Key Concepts

#### Two Adjustment Channels

| Channel | Trigger | Mechanism | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Money market** | Excess demand for money (right of LM) | Sell bonds ⇒ ↓bond P ⇒ **↑i** | i rises until on LM |
| Money market | Excess supply of money (left of LM) | Buy bonds ⇒ ↑bond P ⇒ **↓i** | i falls until on LM |
| **Goods market** | Excess demand (left of IS) | Inventories drawn down ⇒ **↑Y** | Y rises until on IS |
| Goods market | Excess supply (right of IS — note textbook framing) | Inventories build up ⇒ firms cut output ⇒ **↓Y** | Y falls until on IS |

> Cross-effects: ↑Y ⇒ ↑money demand ⇒ pushes i up (LM channel reacts to IS adjustment). ↓i ⇒ ↑I ⇒ shifts IS-relevant variables (IS channel reacts to LM adjustment).

#### Fig. 3.5 — Four Disequilibrium Quadrants Around E

E = equilibrium (Y₀, i₀). Points F, G, H, K = disequilibrium positions.

| Point | Goods sector | Money sector | Direction of adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G** (above-right of IS, right of LM) | Excess **supply** of goods | Excess **demand** for money | ↑i (sell bonds), ↓Y (cut output) — moves toward E |
| **H** (left of IS, left of LM) | Excess **demand** for goods | Excess **supply** of money | ↓i (buy bonds), ↑Y (raise output) — moves toward E |
| **F** (on LM, off IS — excess supply of goods) | Inventory build-up | Equilibrium | Firms ↓production ⇒ ↓Y ⇒ ↓money demand ⇒ ↓i ⇒ economy moves to E |
| **K** | (Symmetric to F/G/H — analyse as exercise) | — | Arrows in Fig. 3.6 indicate convergence |

#### Fig. 3.6 — Direction of Adjustment
Arrows from each quadrant point toward E ⇒ **economy converges** to IS-LM equilibrium.

### Definitions
- **IS-LM disequilibrium**: economy at a (Y, i) not on IS ∩ LM intersection.
- **Convergence**: arrows in Fig. 3.6 indicate adjustment back to E.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Right of LM ⇒ ↑i; left of LM ⇒ ↓i.
> - Left of IS ⇒ ↑Y; right of IS ⇒ ↓Y.
> - Real and monetary sectors **inter-related**.

- Builds on: 3.2.1 disequilibrium; 3.3.1 disequilibrium.
- Feeds into: 3.4.3 supply shocks.

## Section: 3.4.3 Impact of Supply Shocks 🔴

### Core Idea
IS-LM equilibrium ≠ guaranteed at potential output Y\*. In the **long run, P is flexible**. Adverse supply shock ⇒ ↓Y\* (potential output) ⇒ ↑P ⇒ ↓real M^s ⇒ **LM shifts left** ⇒ ↑i, equilibrium shifts to F. **IS is relatively stable** under supply shocks because aggregate expenditure refers to physical quantities; **LM does the adjusting** because it depends on **real balances**.

### Key Concepts

#### Potential Output (Y\*)
- Given by **production capacity** of the economy.
- Derived from **production function** (Unit 1).
- Depicted as **vertical line** at Y\*.
- Long-run policy goal: economy at Y\* with unemployment at natural rate.

#### Why IS Stable, LM Adjusts

| Curve | Real-balance dependence | Effect of ΔP |
|---|---|---|
| **IS** | None — based on **physical quantities** (C, I, G in real terms) | **Stable** — does not shift |
| **LM** | Based on **M^s/P** (real money supply) | **Shifts** with P |

#### Adverse Supply Shock (e.g., drought ⇒ ↓agricultural productivity)
1. Y\* falls from Y\* to Y\*\*.
2. Original equilibrium E (IS₀ ∩ LM₀) at Y\* no longer feasible.
3. ↑P (price rise).
4. ↓real M^s (M^s/P falls).
5. LM₀ shifts **left** to LM₁.
6. New equilibrium **F**: ↑i, ↓Y to Y\*\*.

#### Fig. 3.7 — Adjustment to Adverse Supply Shock
- Vertical line at Y\* (then Y\*\*).
- IS₀ unchanged.
- LM shifts left ⇒ equilibrium moves up the IS₀ curve.

### Definitions
- **Potential output (Y\*)**: production capacity from production function.
- **Adverse supply shock**: exogenous fall in productive capacity (e.g., drought).
- **Real balance adjustment**: LM shifts via M^s/P when P changes.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Supply shock shifts IS. → ✅ Supply shock shifts **LM** (via P → M^s/P); IS is **stable**.
- ❌ IS-LM equilibrium is always at Y\*. → ✅ Equilibrium can be at **any Y**; long-run adjustment via P brings the economy toward Y\*.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Adverse supply shock ⇒ ↓Y\* ⇒ ↑P ⇒ ↓real M^s ⇒ LM left ⇒ ↑i, ↓Y.
> - **IS stable; LM shifts** under supply shocks.

## Section: 3.5.2 (cont.) Aggregate Supply (AS) Curve — Lucas + LRAS/SRAS 🔴
<!-- Continues from Chunk 006 / opening of page 67 -->

### Core Idea
Page-67 text closes Section 3.5.2 by completing **Lucas's aggregate supply hypothesis**: economic agents respond to **price/wage incentives** which alter their **leisure-vs-work trade-off**. ⇒ **Short-run AS (SRAS) is upward-sloping**; **long-run AS (LRAS) is vertical**. LRAS shifts only with changes in **production capacity**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Workers misread temporary wage rises as real raises, supply more labour, and output rises in the short run. Once they figure out it was just inflation, they pull back — output returns to its long-run vertical line.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Versions of the AS Curve (textbook list)

| School | AS shape | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical** (Unit 1) | **Vertical** at full-employment Y | Output perfectly inelastic to P |
| **Keynesian** | **Horizontal** | Firms can ↑production at any level without affecting prices |
| **New-Classical (Lucas)** | **Upward-sloping in short run; vertical in long run** | Realistic compromise; SRAS vs LRAS distinction |

> Textbook verdict: "Both the versions (vertical and horizontal) of the AS curve seems unrealistic."

#### Lucas's Aggregate Supply Hypothesis (verbatim flow)
1. Economic agents respond to **price and wage incentives**.
2. These incentives alter the **leisure–work trade-off**.
3. Actual W > equilibrium W ⇒ households **supply more labour**.
4. Actual W < equilibrium W ⇒ households **decrease labour supply**.
5. ⇒ **SRAS slopes upward**.
6. **LRAS is vertical**; shifts only when **production capacity** changes.

### Fig. 3.9 — Impact of Demand Shock (page 67)

| Element | Reading |
|---|---|
| Initial equilibrium | E at (Y₀, P₀); AD ∩ upward-sloping AS |
| Favourable demand shock | AD → AD₁ (rightward) |
| New equilibrium | F at (Y₁, P₁); both Y and P rise |
| Long-run dynamic | ↑Y is **temporary**; output returns to **LRAS level** in long run |

### Definitions
- **Lucas's aggregate supply hypothesis**: agents respond to W/P incentives via leisure-work trade-off ⇒ SRAS upward-sloping. ⭐
- **SRAS (short-run aggregate supply)**: upward-sloping AS in periods where wages/prices are sticky. ⭐
- **LRAS (long-run aggregate supply)**: vertical AS at potential output; shifts only with production capacity. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Classical AS = vertical; Keynesian AS = horizontal; Lucas SRAS = upward-sloping.
> - LRAS = vertical, shifts only with production capacity.
> - Demand shock ⇒ ΔY temporary; long-run Y returns to LRAS.

## Section: 3.5.1 Derivation of the AD Curve 🔴

### Core Idea
**Relax the fixed-P assumption.** ΔP changes the **real money supply** (M^s/P). With nominal M^s fixed, this shifts the **LM curve**, changing equilibrium (Y, i). Plotting (P, Y) pairs from successive LM positions traces the **downward-sloping AD curve**. AD ∩ AS yields equilibrium output and prices.

> **In Simple Terms:** Drop a price ⇒ same nominal cash buys more real balances ⇒ LM jumps right ⇒ i falls ⇒ I rises ⇒ Y rises. Each price level gives one Y. Connect the dots → AD curve.

### Key Concepts

#### Logical Chain (textbook page 65)

| Step | Variable | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | P | ↓ (P₀ → P₁) |
| 2 | Real money supply M₀/P | ↑ (to M₀/P₁) |
| 3 | LM curve | Shifts **right** (LM₀ → LM₁) |
| 4 | Money market disequilibrium | Excess supply of real balances |
| 5 | i | ↓ (i₀ → i₁) restores money-market equilibrium |
| 6 | Investment I | ↑ (cheaper credit) |
| 7 | Aggregate expenditure | ↑ |
| 8 | Output Y | ↑ (Y₀ → Y₁) |

⇒ **↓P ⇒ ↑Y** ⇒ AD slopes **downward**.

#### Fig. 3.8 — Two-Panel AD Derivation

| Panel | Axes | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Y horizontal; i vertical | IS₀ ∩ LM₀ at point E (Y₀, i₀) with M₀/P₀; ↓P ⇒ LM₁; new intersection b (Y₁, i₁) |
| (b) | Y horizontal; P vertical | Plot a (Y₀, P₀) and b (Y₁, P₁); join ⇒ downward-sloping AD |

> Note: textbook page 65 has a small typo, referring to "Panel (b) of Fig. 3.7" when it means Fig. 3.8 — readers should treat as Fig. 3.8.

#### Why AD Slopes Downward (textbook trace)
- ↓P ⇒ ↑real M^s ⇒ excess supply in money market ⇒ ↓i restores equilibrium.
- ↓i ⇒ ↑I ⇒ ↑aggregate expenditure ⇒ ↑Y.

#### AD Slope Interpretation

| AD shape | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Flatter AD** | A given ΔP produces **larger** ΔY |
| **Steeper AD** | A given ΔP produces **smaller** ΔY |

#### AD Shifters (page 66)

| Cause | AD shifts |
|---|---|
| **Expansionary fiscal** (↑G, ↓tax rate) | **Right/up** |
| Contractionary fiscal | Left/down |
| **Expansionary monetary** (↓i, ↑M^s via OMO, ↓reserve requirements) | **Right** |
| Contractionary monetary | Left |
| Anything that shifts **IS right** or **LM right** | AD **right** |
| Anything that shifts IS left or LM left | AD left |
| ↑autonomous spending (e.g., ↑G) | AD right |
| ↑nominal money supply | AD up/right |

> **Master rule:** Shift IS right OR LM right ⇒ AD right. Shift IS left OR LM left ⇒ AD left.

### Definitions
- **Aggregate Demand (AD) curve**: locus of (P, Y) combinations consistent with **simultaneous IS-LM equilibrium** as P varies. ⭐
- **Real money supply transmission**: channel by which ΔP shifts LM via M^s/P, indirectly producing the downward-sloping AD. ⭐
- **Open market operations (OMO)**: central-bank purchase/sale of bonds that adjusts M^s; shifts LM and AD. ⭐
- **Reserve requirements**: central-bank tool affecting M^s; shifts LM and AD. ⭐
- **Expansionary fiscal policy**: ↑G or ↓tax rate; shifts AD right. ⭐
- **Contractionary fiscal policy**: ↓G or ↑tax rate; shifts AD left. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes
↓P → ↑M^s/P → LM right → excess supply of money → ↓i → ↑I → ↑AE → ↑Y → trace (P, Y) ⇒ downward-sloping AD.

### Examples
**Example (textbook):** P₀ → P₁ (decline). With M₀ fixed, real balances rise from M₀/P₀ to M₀/P₁. LM₀ → LM₁. IS₀ unchanged. i: i₀ → i₁ (lower); Y: Y₀ → Y₁ (higher). Plot point a (Y₀, P₀) and b (Y₁, P₁) ⇒ AD downward-sloping.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ AD is the same as the Keynesian AE schedule. → ✅ **AD plots P against Y**; AE plots AE against Y at fixed P.
- ❌ AD shifts when P changes. → ✅ ΔP causes **movement along** AD; AD shifts only when IS or LM shifts.
- ❌ ↑M^s causes movement along AD. → ✅ ↑M^s shifts **AD itself** (via LM).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - AD = (P, Y) pairs from IS ∩ LM as P varies.
> - ↓P ⇒ ↑M^s/P ⇒ LM right ⇒ ↓i ⇒ ↑I ⇒ ↑Y.
> - **Master rule:** any IS-right or LM-right shifter ⇒ AD right.

## Section: 4.2 National Income Identity in an Open Economy 🔴

### Core Idea
Domestic agents now **trade with foreigners**: exports (X) and imports (M). Open-economy NI identity adds **net exports (NX = X − M)**. This produces the **twin deficits** identity: **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA**, linking external imbalance to private and public saving-investment gaps.

> **In Simple Terms:** A country that spends more than it earns must be borrowing from somewhere. Identity says either households are over-spending (S < I) or government is (T < G) — or both. The current-account deficit is the mirror.

### Key Concepts

#### Step 1 — Aggregate Expenditure on Domestic Goods

| Step | Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic expenditure (incl. imports) | C + I + G | Total domestic-agent spending on **all** goods |
| On domestic goods only | C + I + G − M | Subtract imports |
| Add foreigner spending on domestic | C + I + G − M + X | Add **exports** |

#### Step 2 — Open-Economy NI Identity (Eq. 4.1)
**Y = C + I + G + X − M = C + I + G + NX** … (4.1)

Where **NX = X − M** = net exports = balance of trade.

| NX sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **NX > 0** | **Trade surplus** (X > M) |
| **NX < 0** | **Trade deficit** (M > X) |
| NX = 0 | Balanced trade |

#### Step 3 — GDP vs GNP

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **GDP (Y)** | Aggregate income earned **within geographical boundaries** of the country (by residents + foreigners). |
| **GNP** | Aggregate income of **citizens** of the country (whether located within or abroad). |
| **NFIA (Net Factor Income from Abroad)** | Income earned abroad by domestic factors **minus** income earned by foreign factors in domestic economy. |

**GNP = Y + NFIA** … (4.2)

| NFIA sign | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| **NFIA < 0** (GDP > GNP) | Many foreign-owned MNCs in country (large foreign-factor income outflows) |
| **NFIA > 0** (GNP > GDP) | Sizeable migrant population earning abroad |

#### Examples (verbatim)
- **German consultant in New Delhi**: salary = part of **German GNP** + **India's GDP**.
- **Indian worker in Singapore**: salary = part of **Singapore's GDP** + **India's GNP**.

#### Step 4 — Current Account Identity

GNP = C + I + G + NX + NFIA
⇒ GNP = C + I + G + **CA**, where **CA = NX + NFIA** … (current account balance)
⇒ **GNP − (C + I + G) = CA** … (4.3)

| CA sign | Implication |
|---|---|
| **CA > 0** (surplus) | GNP > absorption ⇒ country is **net lender** to RoW |
| **CA < 0** (deficit) | absorption > GNP ⇒ country is **net debtor / borrower** |

> **Aggregate domestic absorption** = C + I + G.

#### Step 5 — Income Side

**C + S + T = GNP** … (4.4)

#### Step 6 — Twin Deficits Identity (Eq. 4.5)

Combine (4.3) and (4.4):
C + S + T = C + I + G + CA
⇒ S + T = I + G + CA
⇒ **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA** … (4.5) ⭐

| Combination | Implication |
|---|---|
| (S − I) < 0 only | Private investment boom drives CA deficit |
| (T − G) < 0 only | Budget deficit drives CA deficit |
| **Both** < 0 | "Twin deficits" — both private and public sectors over-spending |
| (S − I) > 0 but |T − G| > |S − I| | Public deficit dominates ⇒ **CA still in deficit** |
| Both > 0 | CA surplus — country is net lender |

#### Step 7 — Closed vs Open Economy Contrast

| Aspect | Closed Economy | Open Economy |
|---|---|---|
| Trade with RoW | None | Yes (X, M) |
| CA | **Always = 0** | Can be positive or negative |
| Aggregate expenditure vs income | **Cannot exceed** income | **Can exceed** income (via borrowing) |
| (S − I) < 0 ⇒ requires | **(T − G) > 0** must compensate | Foreign borrowing can fill the gap |
| Funding mechanism | Internal only | International capital flows |

### Definitions
- **Net exports (NX)**: X − M; the trade balance. ⭐
- **Trade surplus**: NX > 0. ⭐
- **Trade deficit**: NX < 0. ⭐
- **GDP (Y)**: aggregate income earned within geographical boundaries (residents + foreigners). ⭐
- **GNP**: aggregate income of country's citizens (within country or abroad). ⭐
- **Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)**: income earned abroad by domestic factors minus income earned by foreign factors in domestic economy. ⭐
- **Aggregate domestic absorption**: C + I + G. ⭐
- **Current Account balance (CA)**: NX + NFIA. ⭐
- **Net debtor**: CA < 0; borrows to bridge expenditure-income gap. ⭐
- **Net lender**: CA > 0; expenditure < income. ⭐
- **Twin deficits identity**: (S − I) + (T − G) = CA. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Open-economy NI: **Y = C + I + G + NX**.
> - GNP = Y + NFIA.
> - **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA**.
> - Closed: CA = 0; Open: CA can be ≠ 0.
> - Trade deficit ⇒ aggregate expenditure > GDP, AND national savings < investment.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ GDP = GNP. → ✅ Differ by NFIA; can be very large in MNC-heavy or migrant-sending economies.
- ❌ CA deficit always = budget deficit. → ✅ Can come from private (S − I) gap, public (T − G) gap, or both.
- ❌ Closed-economy results carry over without modification. → ✅ Open economies can borrow from RoW ⇒ CA ≠ 0.
- ❌ Trade balance = current account balance. → ✅ CA = NX + NFIA (and unilateral transfers).

### Examples (CYP 1 Q1 logic)
**Trade deficit (NX < 0) implies:**
- (a) From Y = C + I + G + NX with NX < 0 ⇒ **C + I + G > Y** ⇒ aggregate expenditure > GDP.
- (b) From (S − I) + (T − G) = CA, assuming CA mirrors NX in sign ⇒ **S + (T − G) < I** ⇒ national savings (private + public) < investment.

## Section: 4.3 Balance of Payments and Exchange Rate (introduction) 🔴

### Core Idea
**BOP** = **systematic record** of all transactions of a country with foreigners over a period (typically one year). Includes trade in goods, services, physical/financial assets, and **transfer payments**. BOP has **two main components**: **Current Account** + **Capital Account**.

---

## Section: 4.3.1 Balance of Payments 🔴

### Key Concepts

#### Two Main Components

| Component | Records |
|---|---|
| **Current Account** | Receipts/payments from international trade in goods and services (incl. factor services) |
| **Capital Account** | Cross-border purchase/sale of real and financial assets |

### Current Account — Composition

| Sub-component | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Balance of trade (merchandise)** | Exports vs imports of **goods** |
| **Balance on invisibles** | Trade in services, factor incomes, transfers |

#### Balance of Trade (Merchandise)
- = Exports of goods − Imports of goods.
- **Trade deficit**: imports > exports.
- **Trade surplus**: exports > imports.

#### Invisibles — Three Sub-Categories

| Sub-category | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade in services** | Cross-border buying/selling of services | India's software exports |
| **Factor incomes** | Investment income (dividends, interest) on foreign-held assets | Indian holds US bond ⇒ interest income (credit); foreigner holds Indian asset ⇒ debit |
| **Unilateral transfers** | Overseas payments **without quid pro quo** | Foreign aid, workers' remittances, gifts, donations |

#### Net Investment Income from Abroad
= Indian residents' earnings on foreign assets − foreigners' earnings on Indian assets.

#### Net Unilateral Transfers
= Transfers **received by Indians from abroad** − transfers **sent by Indians to foreigners**.

> **For many developing countries**, workers' remittances are an important source of foreign exchange.

#### Sign Convention in Current Account

| Transaction type | Entry |
|---|---|
| **Inflow** of foreign exchange (e.g., exports) | **Credit** |
| **Outflow** of foreign exchange (e.g., imports) | **Debit** |

#### Current Account Balance Formula
**CA = Merchandise trade balance + Balance on invisibles**
- Where invisibles balance = services + net investment income + net transfers.

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Receipts > Payments** | Current account **surplus** |
| **Payments > Receipts** | Current account **deficit** |

#### Trade Deficit but CA Surplus (edge case)
- Possible if **net invisibles balance** is positive and large.
- E.g., countries that are net recipients of large unilateral transfers/remittances.

### Real Data (textbook)

| Year | Country | Statistic |
|---|---|---|
| **2021** | **India** | Largest recipient of remittances in **absolute** figure: **$87 billion** |
| **2021** | **Lebanon** | Largest recipient as **% of GDP**: **54%** of GDP |

### Capital Account — Composition

#### Definition
- Records cross-border purchase/sale of **real assets** (land, factories, houses) and **financial assets** (stocks, bonds).
- **Each transaction recorded twice**: once as credit, once as debit (captures both sides).

#### Example
- Domestic resident buys foreign government bond ⇒ **asset import** (credit) + **capital outflow** (debit, payment for asset).

#### Two Classifications

##### (A) By Repayment Obligation

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Debt-creating flows** | Loan creates repayment obligation | Domestic agents borrow from foreign financial institutions |
| **Non-debt-creating flows** | No repayment commitment for recipient | **FDI** (Foreign Direct Investment) |

##### (B) By Duration

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-term** | Withdrawable at short notice | **FII / FPI** (Foreign Portfolio Investment) — equity bought by foreign institutional investors |
| **Long-term** | Not easily withdrawn | **Greenfield FDI** — investment in production sector |

#### Capital Account Balance

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Inflows > Outflows** | Capital account **surplus** |
| **Outflows > Inflows** | Capital account **deficit** |

#### Mirror Relation Current ↔ Capital
- Inflow of forex in CA = credit ⇒ corresponding **debit in capital account**.
- ⇒ **CA surplus mirrored by capital account deficit (and vice versa)**.
- A **CA deficit must be financed** by selling assets or borrowing ⇒ **capital account surplus**.

### Overall BOP Balance

#### Accounting Identity
- Every transaction recorded twice (debit + credit) ⇒ **BOP always balances** in accounting sense.
- In practice imbalances appear due to **time lags / data collection issues** (e.g., exports recorded at customs but payment received 6 months later).
- ⇒ **"Errors and omissions"** entry added to balance the BOP.

#### Overall BOP Surplus/Deficit
**Overall BOP balance = Current Account + Capital Account**

##### BOP Surplus Configurations

| Case | CA | Capital Account |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Surplus | Surplus |
| (ii) | Deficit | Surplus, **larger in magnitude** |
| (iii) | Surplus | Deficit, smaller in magnitude |

##### BOP Deficit Configurations
- Deficit in **both** CA and capital account, OR
- Relatively large deficit in either CA or capital account.

#### Effect on Foreign Exchange Reserves

| BOP outcome | Forex reserves |
|---|---|
| **Surplus** | **Increase** |
| **Deficit** | **Decrease (depleted)** |

### Change in Foreign Exchange Reserves

#### Official Reserve Transactions (ORT)
- Capital account also includes **official reserve transactions** (changes in central bank's forex reserves).
- BOP surplus ⇒ net forex inflow ⇒ ↑forex reserves.
- BOP deficit ⇒ net forex outflow ⇒ ↓forex reserves.

#### Full BOP Identity
**BOP Balance = Current Account + Capital Account (incl. errors & omissions) + Official Reserve Transactions = 0**

#### Worked Example (textbook page 76)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current account | **−$15,000** (deficit) |
| Capital account | **+$12,000** (surplus, smaller) |
| BOP balance | **−$3,000** (deficit) |
| ⇒ Reserves | Decrease by **$3,000** |

#### Sign Convention for Reserves
- **BOP surplus** = **positive** sign.
- Corresponding **increase in reserves** = **negative** sign in the accounts.
- ⇒ Two add to zero.
- ⇒ In BOP accounts, **↑reserves appears as − sign**; **↓reserves appears as + sign**.

### Definitions
- **Balance of Payments (BOP)**: systematic record of all transactions of a country with foreigners during a given period. ⭐
- **Current Account**: BOP component for trade in goods, services, factor incomes, transfers. ⭐
- **Capital Account**: BOP component for cross-border purchase/sale of real and financial assets. ⭐
- **Balance of trade / merchandise trade balance**: exports of goods − imports of goods. ⭐
- **Invisibles**: services, factor incomes, unilateral transfers in current account. ⭐
- **Net investment income from abroad**: domestic factors' foreign earnings − foreign factors' domestic earnings. ⭐
- **Unilateral transfers**: payments without quid pro quo (aid, remittances, gifts). ⭐
- **Workers' remittances**: money sent by overseas residents to families at home. ⭐
- **Trade deficit**: imports > exports. ⭐
- **Current account deficit**: forex payments > receipts in current account.
- **Debt-creating capital flow**: loan to be repaid in future. ⭐
- **Non-debt-creating capital flow**: e.g., FDI; no repayment commitment. ⭐
- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: investment by foreign firms in domestic country; non-debt-creating. ⭐
- **Greenfield FDI**: long-term investment in new production facilities. ⭐
- **Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI / FII)**: short-term equity investment by foreign institutional investors. ⭐
- **Errors and omissions**: balancing entry added because BOP data has time lags. ⭐
- **Official Reserve Transactions (ORT)**: changes in forex reserves held by central bank. ⭐
- **Overall BOP balance**: CA + Capital Account; surplus ⇒ ↑reserves; deficit ⇒ ↓reserves. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Why CA and Capital Accounts Mirror
1. CA inflow of forex (e.g., exports) = credit.
2. Corresponding debit recorded in **capital account** (foreign payment for export).
3. ⇒ CA surplus ⇒ capital account deficit (and vice versa).

#### Why BOP Always Balances Theoretically
1. Double-entry bookkeeping ⇒ each transaction is both debit + credit.
2. Sum of debits = sum of credits ⇒ zero net balance.
3. Errors and omissions entry corrects for data lags.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Trade deficit = current account deficit. → ✅ CA deficit can occur even with trade surplus (if invisibles negative); CA surplus possible with trade deficit (if remittances large).
- ❌ FDI is debt-creating. → ✅ FDI is **non-debt-creating**.
- ❌ FII = FDI. → ✅ FII = short-term portfolio; FDI = long-term direct investment.
- ❌ CA surplus ⇒ capital account surplus. → ✅ **Mirror**: CA surplus ⇒ capital account **deficit**.
- ❌ ↑forex reserves shown as positive in BOP. → ✅ **Negative sign** (so it cancels the BOP surplus to zero).
- ❌ Errors and omissions are accounting fraud. → ✅ Reflect **time lags in data collection**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - BOP = CA + Capital Account.
> - CA = Merchandise + Invisibles (services + factor income + unilateral transfers).
> - **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA**.
> - CA surplus ↔ Capital account deficit (mirror).
> - BOP surplus ⇒ ↑reserves (entered as negative sign).
> - BOP deficit ⇒ ↓reserves (entered as positive sign).
> - India's 2021 remittances = $87bn (largest absolute); Lebanon = 54% of GDP (largest %).

## Section: 3.5.2 (cont.) Lucas SRAS + LRAS, and Fig. 3.9 Demand Shock 🔴

### Core Idea
Page 67 closes Section 3.5.2: under Lucas's hypothesis, **SRAS slopes upward** but **LRAS is vertical**. **LRAS shifts only when production capacity of the economy changes**. Fig. 3.9 then traces a favourable demand shock in AD-AS space.

> **In Simple Terms:** In the short run, firms ramp up output when prices rise (workers willing to supply more labour). In the long run, the economy returns to its capacity ceiling — only more capacity moves that ceiling.

### Key Concepts

#### Lucas's AS Hypothesis (closing sentences, page 67)
- Economic agents respond to **price and wage incentives** ⇒ trade-off between **leisure and work**.
- Actual W > equilibrium W ⇒ households **supply more labour**.
- Actual W < equilibrium W ⇒ households **decrease labour supply**.
- ⇒ **SRAS upward-sloping**.
- **LRAS vertical**; shifts only with production-capacity change.

#### Fig. 3.9: Impact of Demand Shock

| Element | Reading |
|---|---|
| Initial equilibrium E | (Y₀, P₀); AD ∩ upward-sloping AS |
| Favourable demand shock | AD → AD₁ (rightward) |
| New short-run equilibrium F | (Y₁, P₁); both Y and P rise |
| Long-run dynamic | "the increase in output however will be temporary and output will be back at the LRAS level in the long run" |

### Definitions
- **Production capacity (LRAS shifter)**: the only force that shifts the long-run AS; defines the LRAS level. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Demand shock raises Y and P only **temporarily**; long run pulls Y back to LRAS.

### Recap (verbatim trace)
- **IS-LM model** = equilibrium in **real sector** and **monetary sector**.
- **IS curve**: every point ⇒ real sector in equilibrium.
  - **Left of IS** ⇒ excess **demand** for goods.
  - **Right of IS** ⇒ excess **supply** of goods.
- **LM curve**: every point ⇒ monetary sector in equilibrium.
  - **Right of LM** ⇒ excess **demand** for money.
  - **Left of LM** ⇒ excess **supply** of money.
- Off-equilibrium ⇒ economy tends to **move toward** IS ∩ LM.
- **AD curve** derived from IS-LM: traces equilibrium (P, Y) combinations.
- **AS-AD model** ⇒ equilibrium output and prices.

> **Quick Recall (Unit 3):**
> - IS = real sector. LM = monetary sector. Both meet at unique (Y, i).
> - AD comes from IS-LM by varying P. AS comes from production.
> - AD ∩ AS ⇒ macro equilibrium (Y, P).

## Section: 4.2 National Income Identity in an Open Economy 🔴
<!-- Pages 70-73; textbook lists this as "4.1" due to typo -->

### Core Idea
Open economy adds **trade with foreigners** (X, M). NI identity becomes **Y = C + I + G + NX**. Layered with NFIA, this yields the **current-account identity** and ultimately the **twin deficits identity**: (S − I) + (T − G) = CA.

> **In Simple Terms:** A country that spends more than it earns is borrowing from somewhere. The shortfall shows up as a current-account deficit, mirrored in either private over-spending (S < I), public over-spending (T < G), or both.

### Key Concepts

#### Step 1 — Aggregate Expenditure on Domestic Goods (page 70-71)

| Step | Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic expenditure (incl. imports) | C + I + G | Domestic-agent spending on **all** goods |
| On domestic goods only | C + I + G − M | Subtract imports |
| Add foreigner spending on domestic | C + I + G − M + X | Add **exports** ⇒ total final expenditure on domestically produced goods |

#### Step 2 — Open-Economy NI Identity (Eq. 4.1)
**Y = C + I + G + X − M = C + I + G + NX** … (4.1)

Where **NX = X − M** = net exports = balance of trade.

| NX sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **NX > 0** | **Trade surplus** (X > M) |
| **NX < 0** | **Trade deficit** (M > X) |

#### Step 3 — GDP vs GNP (page 71)

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **GDP (Y)** | Aggregate income earned **within geographical boundaries** (residents + foreigners). |
| **GNP** | Aggregate income of **citizens** of the country (within country or abroad). |
| **NFIA** | Income earned **abroad by domestic factors** − income earned by **foreign factors in domestic economy**. |

**GNP = Y + NFIA** … (4.2)

| NFIA sign | Likely cause (from textbook) |
|---|---|
| **NFIA < 0** (GDP > GNP) | Most production units owned by foreign multinationals (large foreign-factor income outflows) **OR** sizeable migrant population employed abroad |
| **NFIA > 0** (GNP > GDP) | (logical mirror) Country's residents earn lots abroad |

> ⚠️ Textbook's wording is slightly tangled at page 71; literally it says GDP > GNP can occur either when foreign MNCs dominate (foreigners' incomes accrue) or when there's a sizeable migrant population. The latter logically belongs to NFIA > 0 — readers should treat each example carefully.

#### Examples (verbatim)
- **German consultant in New Delhi**: salary = part of **German GNP** + **India's GDP**.
- **Indian worker in Singapore**: salary = part of **Singapore's GDP** + **India's GNP**.

#### Step 4 — Current Account Identity (Eq. 4.3)

GNP = C + I + G + NX + NFIA
⇒ GNP = C + I + G + **CA**, where **CA = NX + NFIA**
⇒ **GNP − (C + I + G) = CA** … (4.3)

| CA sign | Implication |
|---|---|
| **CA > 0** (surplus) | GNP > absorption ⇒ **net lender** to RoW |
| **CA < 0** (deficit) | absorption > GNP ⇒ **net debtor / borrower** |

> **Aggregate domestic absorption** = C + I + G.
> A current-account deficit can be financed by **borrowing from RoW** or by **sale of foreign assets held by domestic agents**.

#### Step 5 — Income Side (Eq. 4.4)
**C + S + T = GNP** … (4.4)
(Aggregate income is consumed, saved, or paid in taxes.)

#### Step 6 — Twin Deficits Identity (Eq. 4.5)
Combine (4.3) and (4.4):
- C + S + T = C + I + G + CA
- ⇒ S + T = I + G + CA
- ⇒ **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA** … (4.5) ⭐

| Configuration | CA outcome |
|---|---|
| (S − I) < 0 only | Private investment boom drives CA deficit |
| (T − G) < 0 only | Budget deficit drives CA deficit |
| **Both** < 0 | "Twin deficits" — both private and public sectors over-spending |
| (S − I) > 0 but \|T − G\| > \|S − I\| | Public deficit dominates ⇒ **CA still in deficit** |
| Both > 0 | CA surplus — country is net lender |

#### Step 7 — Closed vs Open Economy (page 72-73)

| Aspect | Closed Economy | Open Economy |
|---|---|---|
| Trade with RoW | None (by definition) | Yes (X, M) |
| CA | **Always = 0** | Can be positive or negative |
| Aggregate expenditure vs income | **Cannot exceed** income | **Can exceed** income (via foreign borrowing) |
| (S − I) < 0 ⇒ requires | **(T − G) > 0** must compensate | Foreign borrowing can fill the gap |
| Funding mechanism | Internal savings only | International capital flows |
| Drawing on others' saving | No | Yes — CA-deficit countries draw on CA-surplus countries' saving |

### Definitions
- **Aggregate domestic absorption**: C + I + G. ⭐
- **Net debtor (open economy)**: country running CA < 0; borrows to bridge income-expenditure gap. ⭐
- **Net lender (open economy)**: country running CA > 0; income exceeds expenditure. ⭐

### CYP 1 (Unit 4) Worked Logic
**Q1: Trade deficit (NX < 0) implies:**
- (a) From Y = C + I + G + NX with NX < 0 ⇒ **C + I + G > Y** ⇒ aggregate expenditure > GDP.
- (b) From (S − I) + (T − G) = CA, assuming CA mirrors NX in sign ⇒ **S + (T − G) < I** ⇒ national savings (private + public) < investment.

**Q2: Two fundamental differences open vs closed**:
- Closed: CA = 0 by definition; aggregate expenditure ≤ aggregate income.
- Open: CA can ≠ 0; can sustain absorption > income via foreign borrowing.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ GDP = GNP. → ✅ Differ by NFIA; can be very large in MNC-heavy or migrant-sending economies.
- ❌ CA deficit always = budget deficit. → ✅ Can come from private (S − I) gap, public (T − G) gap, or both.
- ❌ Closed-economy results carry over without modification. → ✅ Open economies can borrow from RoW ⇒ CA ≠ 0.
- ❌ Trade balance = current account balance. → ✅ CA = NX + NFIA.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Y = C + I + G + NX** (4.1).
> - **GNP = Y + NFIA** (4.2).
> - **GNP − (C + I + G) = CA** (4.3).
> - **C + S + T = GNP** (4.4).
> - **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA** (4.5) — twin deficits.
> - Closed: CA = 0. Open: CA can be ≠ 0.

## Section: 4.3 Balance of Payments and Exchange Rate (introduction) 🔴
<!-- Page 73 -->

### Core Idea
**BOP** = systematic record of **all transactions** of a country with foreigners during a given period (typically **one year**). Includes purchase/sale of **goods, services, physical and financial assets**, and **transfer payments**. Two main components: **current account** + **capital account**.

---

## Section: 4.3.1 Balance of Payments 🔴
<!-- Pages 73-76 -->

### Two Main Components

| Component | Records |
|---|---|
| **Current Account** | Receipts/payments due to international trade in goods and services (incl. factor services) |
| **Capital Account** | Cross-border purchase/sale of real and financial assets |

### Current Account — Composition (page 73-74)

| Sub-component | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Balance of trade (merchandise)** | Exports vs imports of **goods** |
| **Balance on invisibles** | Trade in services + factor incomes + transfers |

> Sign convention: **Inflow** of foreign exchange (e.g., exports) = **credit**; **outflow** (e.g., imports) = **debit**.

#### Balance of Trade (Merchandise)
- = Exports of goods − Imports of goods.
- **Trade deficit**: imports > exports.
- **Trade surplus**: exports > imports.
- May be positive, negative, or zero.

#### Invisibles — Three Sub-Categories

| Sub-category | Definition | Textbook example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade in services** | Cross-border buying/selling of services | India's software exports |
| **Factor incomes** | Investment income (dividends, interest) on foreign-held assets | Indian holds US bond ⇒ interest = credit; foreigner holds Indian asset ⇒ debit |
| **Unilateral transfers** | Overseas payments **without quid pro quo** | Foreign aid, workers' remittances, gifts, donations |

#### Net Investment Income from Abroad
= Indian residents' earnings on foreign assets − foreigners' earnings on Indian assets.

#### Net Unilateral Transfers
= Transfers received by Indians from abroad − transfers sent by Indians to foreigners.

> **For many developing countries**, workers' remittances are an important source of foreign exchange earnings.

#### CA Balance Formula
**CA = Merchandise trade balance + Balance on invisibles**
- where Invisibles balance = services + net investment income + net transfers.

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Receipts > Payments** | Current account **surplus** |
| **Payments > Receipts** | Current account **deficit** |

#### Edge Case: Trade Deficit but CA Surplus
- Possible if **net invisibles balance is positive and very high**.
- E.g., countries that are net recipients of large unilateral transfers / remittances.

### Real Data (page 74-75)

| Year | Country | Statistic |
|---|---|---|
| **2021** | **India** | Largest recipient of remittances in **absolute** figure: **$87 billion** |
| **2021** | **Lebanon** | Largest recipient as **% of GDP**: **54%** of GDP |

### Capital Account — Composition (page 75)

#### Definition
- Records cross-border purchase/sale of **real assets** (land, factories, houses) and **financial assets** (stocks, bonds).
- **Each transaction recorded twice**: once as credit, once as debit (captures both aspects).

#### Worked Illustration
- Domestic resident buys foreign government bond / shares of foreign firm ⇒
  - **Asset import** (credit) + **Capital outflow** (debit, payment for asset).

#### Two Classifications

##### (A) By Repayment Obligation

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Debt-creating flows** | Loan creates repayment obligation at future date | Domestic agents borrow from foreign financial institutions |
| **Non-debt-creating flows** | No repayment commitment for recipient | **FDI** (Foreign Direct Investment) |

##### (B) By Duration

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-term** | Withdrawable at short notice | **FII / FPI** — equity bought by foreign institutional investors; can be sold and withdrawn quickly |
| **Long-term** | Not easily withdrawn | **Greenfield FDI** — investment in production sector |

#### Capital Account Balance

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Inflows > Outflows** | Capital account **surplus** |
| **Outflows > Inflows** | Capital account **deficit** |

#### Mirror Relation Current ↔ Capital
- CA inflow of forex = credit ⇒ corresponding **debit in capital account**.
- ⇒ **CA surplus mirrored by capital account deficit (and vice versa)**.
- A **CA deficit must be financed** — by selling assets or borrowing ⇒ corresponding **capital account surplus**.
- ⇒ CA deficit is financed by **net capital inflows** (private sector or government sector or both).

### Overall BOP Balance (page 75-76)

#### Why BOP "Always Balances"
1. Each transaction recorded **twice** (debit + credit) — double-entry bookkeeping.
2. ⇒ In an accounting sense, BOP is always balanced.
3. In practice, imbalances appear due to **time lags / data-collection issues** (e.g., exports recorded at customs but payment received six months later — not in same year's BOP).
4. ⇒ **"Errors and omissions"** entry added to balance the BOP account.

#### Overall BOP Surplus/Deficit
**Overall BOP balance = Current Account + Capital Account**

##### BOP Surplus Configurations (verbatim list)
| Case | CA | Capital Account |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Surplus | Surplus |
| (ii) | Deficit | Surplus, **larger in magnitude** |
| (iii) | Surplus | Deficit, **smaller in magnitude** |

##### BOP Deficit Configurations (verbatim)
- Deficit in **both** CA and capital account, OR
- **Relatively large deficit in either** CA or capital account.

#### Effect on Foreign Exchange Reserves

| BOP outcome | Forex reserves |
|---|---|
| **Surplus** | **Increase** (net inflow of forex) |
| **Deficit** | **Decrease / depleted** (net outflow of forex) |

### Change in Foreign Exchange Reserves (page 76)

#### Official Reserve Transactions (ORT)
- Capital account also includes **official reserve transactions** — changes in the stock of forex reserves held by the **central bank**.
- Apart from private and government agents' transactions.

#### Full BOP Identity
**BOP Balance = Current Account + Capital Account (incl. errors & omissions) + Official Reserve Transactions = 0**

#### Worked Example (textbook page 76)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Current account | **−$15,000** (deficit) |
| Capital account | **+$12,000** (surplus, smaller in magnitude) |
| BOP balance | **−$3,000** (deficit) |
| ⇒ Reserves | **Decrease by $3,000** |

#### Sign Convention for Reserves (page 76, last paragraph)
- **BOP surplus** = **positive** sign in BOP.
- Corresponding **increase in reserves** = **negative** sign (so the two add to zero).
- ⇒ In BOP accounts:
  - **↑forex reserves appears with a NEGATIVE sign**.
  - **↓forex reserves appears with a POSITIVE sign**.

### Definitions
- **Errors and omissions**: balancing entry in BOP account, included because of time lags in data collection. ⭐
- **Official Reserve Transactions (ORT)**: change in the stock of foreign exchange reserves held by the central bank, recorded in the capital account. ⭐
- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: investment by foreign firms in domestic country; **non-debt-creating** capital inflow. ⭐
- **Greenfield FDI**: long-term FDI in country's production sector; not easily withdrawn. ⭐
- **Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI / FII)**: purchase of domestic firms' shares by foreign institutional investors; short-term capital flow; can be withdrawn at short notice. ⭐
- **Net unilateral transfers**: transfers received from abroad − transfers sent abroad. ⭐
- **Net investment income from abroad**: residents' earnings on foreign assets − foreigners' earnings on domestic assets. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Why CA and Capital Accounts Mirror
1. CA inflow of forex (e.g., export) = credit.
2. Corresponding debit recorded in **capital account** (foreign payment for export creates a foreign claim).
3. ⇒ CA surplus ⇒ capital account deficit (and vice versa).

#### Why BOP Always Balances Theoretically
1. Double-entry bookkeeping ⇒ each transaction is both debit + credit.
2. Sum of debits = sum of credits ⇒ zero net balance.
3. Errors and omissions entry corrects for data lags.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Trade deficit = current-account deficit. → ✅ CA includes invisibles; CA can be in surplus even with trade deficit (large remittances/transfers).
- ❌ FDI is debt-creating. → ✅ FDI is **non-debt-creating** — no repayment commitment.
- ❌ FII = FDI. → ✅ FII = short-term portfolio investment; FDI = long-term direct investment.
- ❌ CA surplus ⇒ capital account surplus. → ✅ **Mirror**: CA surplus ⇒ capital account **deficit**.
- ❌ ↑forex reserves shown as positive sign in BOP. → ✅ **Negative sign** (so it cancels the BOP surplus to zero).
- ❌ Errors and omissions = accounting fraud. → ✅ Reflect **time lags in data collection**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - BOP = CA + Capital Account (+ errors & omissions) + ORT = 0.
> - CA = Merchandise + Invisibles (services + factor income + unilateral transfers).
> - **(S − I) + (T − G) = CA**.
> - CA surplus ↔ Capital account deficit (mirror).
> - BOP surplus ⇒ ↑reserves (entered as **negative** sign).
> - BOP deficit ⇒ ↓reserves (entered as **positive** sign).
> - India 2021 remittances = **$87 bn** (largest absolute); Lebanon = **54% of GDP** (largest %).

## Section: 4.3.2 (cont.) Exchange Rate — Nominal, Real, Regimes 🔴
<!-- Pages 77-79 -->

### Core Idea
The supply of foreign exchange comes primarily from **exports and capital inflows**; demand comes from imports and capital outflows. The **market** value of the exchange rate is set by demand–supply in the forex market, but the **official** rate may differ depending on the regime. The chunk first develops nominal vs real exchange rates, then turns to the three regimes (flexible, managed/dirty float, fixed).

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of foreign currency as a good. Its "price" (the exchange rate) is set by who wants dollars (importers, capital exporters) and who is offering dollars (exporters, capital importers). Some governments let that price float freely; others fix it; most do something in between.

### Key Concepts

#### Forex demand and supply (page 77 — completes from chunk_008)
- **Supply** of foreign exchange: **exports + capital inflows**.
- (Demand: imports + capital outflows — from chunk_008.)
- Market value of exchange rate = determined by demand and supply in the forex market.
- Official rate may differ from market value (preview to regimes discussion).

#### Nominal Exchange Rate (e)
- **e** = number of units of **domestic currency (Rupee)** required to purchase **one unit of foreign currency (dollar)**.
- Example: if Rs. 80 buys $1 ⇒ e = Rs. 80 per dollar.
- Can also be defined vs yen, euro, pound, etc.

| Movement | Reading |
|---|---|
| **e rises** (Rs. 80 → Rs. 82 per $) | Price of dollar in rupees ↑ ⇒ **nominal depreciation of rupee** vis-à-vis dollar (and **nominal appreciation of dollar** vs rupee) |
| **e falls** (Rs. 80 → Rs. 78 per $) | Dollar cheaper in rupees ⇒ **nominal appreciation of rupee** (and **nominal depreciation of dollar**) |

#### Real Exchange Rate (r)
- Captures the rate at which **domestic goods are exchanged for foreign goods**.
- Price of foreign goods in terms of domestic goods.
- **Eq. (4.6): r = (e · p_f) / p_d**
  - e = nominal exchange rate (Rs./$).
  - p_d = domestic price level (in rupees).
  - p_f = foreign price level (in dollars).
  - **e · p_f** = price in rupees of a foreign basket of goods.
  - **p_d** = price in rupees of a comparable domestic basket.

| Movement | Effect | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **r rises** | American goods relatively more expensive vs Indian goods | **Real depreciation of rupee** ⇒ boosts export demand |
| **r falls** | Indian goods relatively more expensive | **Real appreciation of rupee** ⇒ reduces export demand |

> **Real exchange rate** is often used as an index of a country's **international price competitiveness**.

#### Relationship between Nominal and Real Exchange Rates
- If domestic and foreign prices are unchanged (p_f / p_d constant):
  - Nominal depreciation (e ↑) ⇒ corresponding **real depreciation** (r ↑).
  - ⇒ Nominal and real move in **same direction**.
- If prices change with exchange rates:
  - E.g., **nominal appreciation** (e falls) **+ large fall in p_d** ⇒ **real depreciation** (r rises).
  - ⇒ e and r can move in **opposite directions**.

#### Exchange Rate Regimes — Three-way Contrast

| Feature | Flexible / Floating (pure / clean float) | Managed Float (dirty float) | Fixed Exchange Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who sets e? | Market demand-supply, **no government intervention** | Mostly market, **central bank intervenes** within a band | **Government** fixes e |
| Need for forex reserves | **None** | Yes (for intervention) | Yes (**adequate stock** required) |
| BOP balancing mechanism | Via **exchange rate fluctuations** | Mix of e adjustment + intervention | Via reserves; CB defends fixed parity |
| Exchange rate volatility | High — bad for exporters/importers, can hurt production, investment, employment | Moderate — within "target zone" | Pre-announced, low volatility |
| Real-world prevalence | "Pure floats are rarely observed in practice" | **Most countries** | Extreme case |
| Adjustment to BOP surplus | e appreciates (e ↓) ⇒ r ↓ ⇒ X ↓, M ↑, NX ↓ ⇒ BOP balance restored | CB buys/sells forex within band | CB intervention to keep e fixed |

#### Flexible (Floating) Regime — Mechanics
- Exchange rate fully flexible; adjusts continuously to equate forex demand and supply.
- **No role for central bank intervention; no need for reserves.**
- BOP surplus example chain:
  - Supply of foreign currency > demand ⇒ e appreciates (e falls)
  - → given prices, r falls
  - → foreign goods relatively cheaper
  - → X falls, M rises, NX falls (CA deficit widens)
  - → BOP surplus reduced, BOP equilibrium restored.

#### Managed Float ("dirty float")
- Currency floats within a **target zone** (a band).
- **Intervention** = central bank buying or selling foreign exchange to influence exchange rate.
- CB stands ready to intervene whenever exchange rate appears to **breach the band limits**.
- Widely prevalent across countries.

#### Fixed Exchange Rate Regime
- Exchange rate is **not freely floating**; fixed by the government.
- **Central bank intervenes in forex markets** to keep price of forex at the **pre-announced level**.
- Requires adequate stock of forex reserves.
- Fixed parity may be **revised** at times:

| Official intervention move | Term |
|---|---|
| **Decrease in e** (currency strengthens) | **Revaluation** |
| **Increase in e** (currency weakens) | **Devaluation** |

> In a world with few restrictions on international transactions in goods and assets, **maintaining a fixed exchange rate poses an immense challenge** for a country's government.

### Definitions
- **Nominal exchange rate (e)**: number of units of domestic currency required to purchase one unit of foreign currency. ⭐
- **Nominal depreciation**: e ↑; price of foreign currency in domestic units rises. ⭐
- **Nominal appreciation**: e ↓; foreign currency becomes cheaper in domestic units. ⭐
- **Real exchange rate (r)**: r = (e · p_f) / p_d; price of foreign goods in terms of domestic goods. ⭐
- **Real depreciation**: r ↑; foreign goods relatively more expensive ⇒ boosts exports. ⭐
- **Real appreciation**: r ↓; domestic goods relatively more expensive ⇒ hurts exports. ⭐
- **Flexible / floating exchange rate regime**: e set by market demand-supply with no government intervention; "clean / pure float". ⭐
- **Pure float / clean float**: fully flexible e, no CB intervention, no need for reserves. ⭐
- **Managed float (dirty float)**: currency allowed to float within a target zone; CB intervenes at band limits. ⭐
- **Intervention**: central bank buying or selling foreign exchange to influence the exchange rate. ⭐
- **Target zone**: band within which currency is allowed to float under managed float. ⭐
- **Fixed exchange rate system**: e is fixed by the government; CB defends pre-announced parity using reserves. ⭐
- **Revaluation**: official decrease in e under fixed regime (currency officially strengthens). ⭐
- **Devaluation**: official increase in e under fixed regime (currency officially weakens). ⭐
- **International price competitiveness**: relative price of domestic vs foreign goods, indexed by r.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Real depreciation chain (e ↑ with p_f, p_d constant):**
1. e ↑ ⇒ r ↑ (real depreciation)
2. Foreign goods relatively more expensive
3. ⇒ Exports ↑, Imports ↓
4. ⇒ NX ↑ (trade balance improves)

**Flexible regime BOP self-correction (BOP surplus):**
- BOP surplus ⇒ e ↓ ⇒ r ↓ ⇒ X ↓, M ↑ ⇒ NX ↓ ⇒ BOP balance restored.

### Examples
- Rs. 80 → Rs. 82 per dollar = nominal depreciation of rupee.
- Rs. 80 → Rs. 78 per dollar = nominal appreciation of rupee.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Nominal e ↑ always means real e ↑." → ✅ Only if p_f / p_d is constant; price changes can flip direction.
- ❌ "Pure float regimes are common." → ✅ "Pure floats are rarely observed in practice." Most countries run managed floats.
- ❌ "Devaluation = depreciation." → ✅ Devaluation is **official** (by government in fixed regime); depreciation is **market-driven** in flexible regime.
- ❌ "Fixed regime needs no reserves." → ✅ Fixed regimes require **adequate reserves** to defend parity.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Floating exchange rates produce **considerable volatility** that can be **unfavorable for exporters and importers**, with adverse impacts on **domestic production, investment, and employment**.
- Maintaining fixed regime is a major challenge with few restrictions on international goods / asset transactions (foreshadowing impossible-trinity-style policy dilemmas).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **e ↑ = nominal depreciation of rupee** vis-à-vis dollar.
> - **r = (e · p_f) / p_d** … (4.6)
> - **r ↑ = real depreciation** ⇒ X ↑, M ↓, NX ↑ (export competitiveness improves).
> - Three regimes: **flexible (pure/clean float) | managed float (dirty float) | fixed**.
> - Fixed-regime official moves: **revaluation** (e ↓) and **devaluation** (e ↑).

## Section: 5.2.1 The IS Curve (Open Economy) 🔴
<!-- Pages 82-83 -->

### Core Idea
Open-economy aggregate demand adds **net exports (NX)**. NX itself depends on **foreign GDP (Y_f)**, **domestic GDP (Y)**, and **real exchange rate (r)**. The IS curve shows (Y, i) combinations clearing the goods market in the open economy.

> **In Simple Terms:** In a closed economy, AD = C + I + G. In an open economy you add what foreigners buy (X) and subtract what we buy from them (M). Both X and M depend on prices abroad vs at home (r) and on income.

### Aggregate Demand (Eq. 5.1)
**AD = C + I + G + X − M = C + I + G + NX**     … (5.1)

### Component Functional Forms

| Component | Functional form | Determinants |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | C = C(Y_d) | Disposable income Y_d = Y − T |
| **I** | I = I(i) | Inverse function of interest rate i |
| **G** | Exogenous | — |
| **X** | X = X(Y_f, r) | Foreign GDP, real exchange rate |
| **M** | M = M(Y, r) | Domestic GDP, real exchange rate |
| **NX** | NX = (X − M) = NX(Y_f, Y, r) | Y_f, Y, r |

### How NX components respond

| Driver | Effect on X | Effect on M | Effect on NX |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Y_f ↑** (foreign GDP up) | X ↑ | — | **NX ↑** |
| **Y ↑** (domestic GDP up) | — | M ↑ | **NX ↓** |
| **r ↑** (real depreciation) | X ↑ (domestic goods cheaper) | M ↓ (foreign goods costlier) | **NX ↑** |

> Imports = **leakage** from domestic economy (spending on output produced elsewhere).
> Exports = **addition** to total expenditure on domestic goods.

### Marshall–Lerner Condition
- **A real devaluation/depreciation improves the trade balance only if** the sum of price-elasticities of exports and imports is **greater than one**.
- Named after **Alfred Marshall** and **Abba Lerner**.
- **Throughout the discussion, the textbook assumes the Marshall-Lerner condition is satisfied.**

### IS Equation
**Y = AD**
⇒ **Y = C(Y_d) + I(i) + G + NX(Y_f, Y, r)**     … (5.2)

### Two New AD Determinants in Open Economy
Compared to closed economy:
1. **Foreign GDP (Y_f)**.
2. **Real exchange rate (r)**.

| Change | Effect on NX | IS shift |
|---|---|---|
| **Y_f ↑** or **r ↑** | NX ↑ | IS shifts **right** |
| **Y_f ↓** or **r ↓** | NX ↓ | IS shifts **left** |

### Open-economy feedback through M
Any change in AD ⇒ ΔY ⇒ ΔM ⇒ ΔNX:
- ↑G ⇒ ↑Y ⇒ ↑M ⇒ **NX falls** (trade surplus shrinks or trade deficit widens).
- ↑Y_f ⇒ ↑X ⇒ ↑Y ⇒ ↑M, **but** trade balance still improves (assumes ↑X due to ↑Y_f exceeds ↑M induced by ↑Y).

### Price-level note
- IS-LM assumes **given prices** ⇒ with given p_f and p_d, a **nominal depreciation/appreciation (rise/fall in e)** brings about a **real depreciation/appreciation (rise/fall in r)**.

### Definitions
- **Open economy aggregate demand**: AD = C + I + G + NX. ⭐
- **Export function**: X = X(Y_f, r). ⭐
- **Import function**: M = M(Y, r). ⭐
- **Net export function**: NX = NX(Y_f, Y, r). ⭐
- **Marshall–Lerner condition**: depreciation improves trade balance only if sum of price-elasticities of exports and imports > 1. ⭐
- **Open-economy IS curve**: Y = C(Y_d) + I(i) + G + NX(Y_f, Y, r); equilibrium locus for the goods market in an open economy. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Real depreciation impact on AD (Marshall-Lerner satisfied):**
1. r ↑
2. Foreign goods relatively more expensive
3. ⇒ X ↑, M ↓
4. ⇒ NX ↑
5. ⇒ AD ↑ ⇒ IS shifts right.

**Government expansion (open economy):**
1. ↑G
2. ⇒ ↑Y, ↑M
3. ⇒ ↓NX
4. ⇒ Trade surplus shrinks / deficit widens.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Depreciation always improves trade balance." → ✅ Only if Marshall-Lerner holds (sum of elasticities > 1).
- ❌ "X depends only on r." → ✅ X = X(Y_f, r) — also needs foreign income.
- ❌ "Open-economy IS is identical to closed." → ✅ Has two new shifters: Y_f and r.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **AD = C + I + G + NX** (5.1).
> - **NX = NX(Y_f, Y, r)**.
> - **Y_f ↑ or r ↑ ⇒ IS right.**
> - **Marshall-Lerner**: sum of price-elasticities of X and M > 1 ⇒ depreciation improves NX.
> - Given prices: nominal depreciation = real depreciation.

## Section: 5.2.2 The LM Curve (Open Economy) 🔴
<!-- Pages 83-85 -->

### Core Idea
Openness does **not change** the inverse relation between money demand and interest rate ⇒ LM is still **upward-sloping**. But two new open-economy features matter: (a) **capital flows** affect i and money supply via BOP, and (b) under **perfect capital mobility**, **i = i_f** must hold. Foreign reserves ↔ money supply linkage gives rise to **sterilization** as a policy tool.

> **In Simple Terms:** Money demand still rises when interest rates fall. But now, when capital flows in, the central bank's reserves grow, that pumps domestic money into the system, and you might want to cancel that out using bond sales — that's sterilization.

### LM Building Blocks (page 83)
- Money demand still inversely related to interest rate.
- LM curve = locus where **demand for money = supply of money**.
- **Upward-sloping**, as in closed economy.
- **New element**: asset transactions with rest of world ⇒ capital flows (capital account of BOP) ⇒ affect i and money supply.

### Perfect Capital Mobility — Assumption
Definitions and implications (page 83):
- **No restrictions** on international capital inflows/outflows.
- **Zero transaction costs** for cross-border capital movement.
- Captures reality of **large-scale international capital mobility in the era of globalization** with technology cutting cross-border transfer costs.

### Implications for Interest Rate
- Domestic and foreign bonds = **perfect substitutes**.
- ⇒ **i = i_f** must hold.
- **i_f is given** and **not influenced by domestic policy**.

#### Adjustment when i ≠ i_f

| Case | Investor behavior | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **i_f > i** | Foreign bonds preferred (higher returns) ⇒ investors sell domestic bonds, buy dollars, buy foreign bonds | **Capital outflows**; domestic bond prices fall ⇒ **i rises** until i = i_f |
| **i = i_f** | Indifferent between domestic and foreign bonds | Equilibrium |

### Forex Reserves and Money Supply Linkage (page 84)
- Forex reserves = **asset of central bank**.
- When CB buys foreign currency ⇒ assets ↑ ⇒ CB **issues domestic currency** ⇒ matching **liability ↑**.
- ⇒ Change in forex reserves ⇒ change in **monetary base / high-powered money** ⇒ change in money supply via the **money multiplier**.

| BOP outcome | Reserves | Money supply |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital inflows / BOP surplus** | ↑ | ↑ |
| **Capital outflows / BOP deficit** | ↓ | ↓ |

### Sterilization
- **Sterilization** = central-bank policy to **offset the impact of a change in forex reserves on the money supply**.
- Done via **open market operations (OMO)**.

#### Worked example (RBI buys dollars to absorb excess supply):
1. RBI buys dollars against rupees in forex market.
2. ⇒ Forex reserves ↑ ⇒ money supply ↑ (unsterilized).
3. RBI **simultaneously sells bonds** in OMO ⇒ money supply ↓.
4. ⇒ The two effects partially or fully cancel: **sterilized intervention**.

| Type of intervention | Money supply outcome |
|---|---|
| **Unsterilized** | Forex purchase fully passes through to ↑M^s |
| **Fully sterilized** | OMO bond sale completely offsets ⇒ M^s unchanged |
| **Partially sterilized** | OMO partially offsets ⇒ smaller ΔM^s |

### Definitions
- **Perfect capital mobility**: no restrictions on cross-border capital flows + zero transaction costs; domestic and foreign bonds are perfect substitutes. ⭐
- **Interest parity under perfect capital mobility**: i = i_f. ⭐
- **Foreign interest rate (i_f)**: given exogenously; not influenced by domestic policy. ⭐
- **Monetary base / high-powered money**: liabilities of central bank; affected by changes in forex reserves. ⭐
- **Sterilization**: CB policy to offset impact of forex-reserve changes on money supply, using OMO. ⭐
- **Sterilized intervention**: CB intervention in forex market combined with offsetting OMO so that money supply is unchanged (or change is muted).
- **Unsterilized intervention**: forex purchase/sale that passes fully into money supply.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Capital outflow when i_f > i:**
1. Investors prefer foreign bonds.
2. Sell domestic bonds → buy dollars → buy foreign bonds.
3. Domestic bond prices ↓ ⇒ i ↑.
4. Continues until i = i_f.

**Sterilized intervention to absorb dollar excess:**
1. RBI buys dollars against rupees → reserves ↑, M^s ↑.
2. RBI sells bonds in OMO → M^s ↓.
3. Net effect: M^s unchanged (or partially changed).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "i and i_f can differ permanently under perfect capital mobility." → ✅ Any difference triggers infinite capital flows until i = i_f.
- ❌ "Forex intervention is monetarily neutral." → ✅ It changes M^s unless **sterilized**.
- ❌ "Sterilization is automatic." → ✅ CB must actively conduct OMO.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Imperfect capital mobility (mentioned in lead-in to 5.2.3 on page 85) — covered later (chunk 010).
- Assumes i_f is given and not influenced by domestic policy.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - LM still upward-sloping; but now i = i_f under perfect capital mobility.
> - **i_f > i ⇒ capital outflows**; **i_f < i ⇒ capital inflows**.
> - Forex reserves ↑ ⇒ M^s ↑ (via money multiplier).
> - **Sterilization** via OMO offsets the M^s effect of forex intervention.

### Connections
- Builds on: closed-economy LM (Unit 3); BOP capital account (Section 4.3.1).
- Feeds into: 5.2.3 IS-LM equilibrium with BP curve.

## Section: 5.2.3 Equilibrium in Open Economy IS-LM Model 🔴
<!-- Page 85 — continues into chunk 010 -->
<!-- Continues in chunk 010 -->

### Core Idea
As in closed economy, macroeconomic equilibrium = **intersection of IS and LM curves** (Fig. 5.1, point E). At E: **i_0 = i_f** (perfect capital mobility). Equilibrium combines **internal balance** (goods + money market clear) with **external balance** (BOP). The **BP curve** — horizontal under perfect capital mobility — represents BOP equilibrium.

> **In Simple Terms:** In an open economy with totally free capital, equilibrium has to satisfy three things at once: goods market clears (IS), money market clears (LM), and BOP balances (BP). Free capital flows make the BP curve flat at i_f.

### Fig. 5.1 — Macroeconomic Equilibrium in Open-Economy IS-LM Model

| Element | Reading |
|---|---|
| Axes | Y horizontal, i vertical |
| **IS curve** | Downward-sloping (open-economy version) |
| **LM curve** | Upward-sloping; depends on M̄/p |
| **BP curve** | **Horizontal** at i = i_f (under perfect capital mobility) |
| Equilibrium **E** | (Y_0, i_0) where IS ∩ LM, with **i_0 = i_f** |
| Internal balance | Goods + money markets clear |
| External balance | BOP clears (point lies on BP) |

### BP Curve — Definition and Slope

| Capital mobility | BP slope | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Perfect** | **Horizontal** at i_f | Slightest deviation triggers infinite capital flows |
| **Imperfect** | **Upward-sloping** | As Y ↑, M ↑, NX ↓ (CA deficit widens) ⇒ i must ↑ to attract capital inflows for matching capital surplus |

### BP Curve under Perfect Capital Mobility — Disequilibrium Map

| Position vs BP | Reading | Capital flow | BOP outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **i > i_f** (above BP) | Domestic interest higher | Surge in capital inflows | **BOP surplus** (inflows exceed needs) |
| **i < i_f** (below BP) | Domestic interest lower | Unlimited capital outflows | **BOP deficit** |
| **i = i_f** (on BP) | Equilibrium rate | Balanced flows | **BOP balance** |

> **NX represents the current account balance** in this framework.
> "The horizontal line BP that passes through the equilibrium rate of interest represents BOP equilibrium, in the sense that it represents interest rate – income combinations for which the **current account surplus (deficit) is exactly offset by a capital account deficit (surplus)**."

### BP Curve under Imperfect Capital Mobility (introduction, page 85)
- BP curve is **upward sloping**.
- As Y ↑ ⇒ M ↑ ⇒ NX ↓ ⇒ CA deficit widens.
- ⇒ i must ↑ to attract capital inflows ⇒ matching capital account surplus.
- (Detailed implications continue in chunk 010.)

### Definitions
- **BP curve**: locus of (Y, i) combinations consistent with BOP equilibrium. ⭐
- **BP curve under perfect capital mobility**: horizontal at i = i_f. ⭐
- **Internal balance**: simultaneous goods-market and money-market equilibrium. ⭐
- **External balance**: BOP equilibrium (CA surplus/deficit offset by capital account deficit/surplus). ⭐
- **Macroeconomic equilibrium (open economy)**: simultaneous internal + external balance at IS ∩ LM ∩ BP. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Why BP horizontal under perfect capital mobility:**
1. Any i ≠ i_f triggers infinite capital flows.
2. ⇒ Only at i = i_f can BOP be in equilibrium.
3. ⇒ BP is flat at i = i_f.

**Why BP slopes upward under imperfect capital mobility:**
1. Higher Y ⇒ higher M ⇒ lower NX ⇒ wider CA deficit.
2. To match this, capital account surplus must rise.
3. ⇒ Higher i needed to attract capital inflows.
4. ⇒ BP slopes upward in (Y, i) space.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "BP curve always slopes upward." → ✅ Only under **imperfect** capital mobility; **horizontal under perfect** capital mobility.
- ❌ "Above BP = BOP deficit." → ✅ Under perfect capital mobility, **above BP = BOP surplus** (since i > i_f triggers capital inflows).
- ❌ "Internal balance = external balance." → ✅ Two distinct concepts; equilibrium E achieves both simultaneously.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- This section explicitly notes that the **detailed implications of imperfect capital mobility** are deferred — full treatment of the imperfect-mobility BP slope continues into chunk 010.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Equilibrium E**: IS ∩ LM with **i_0 = i_f**.
> - **BP horizontal** at i_f under perfect capital mobility.
> - **Above BP ⇒ BOP surplus**; **below BP ⇒ BOP deficit**.
> - Internal balance + external balance = full open-economy equilibrium.

## Section: 5.3 Monetary and Fiscal Policies under Flexible Exchange Rate 🔴
<!-- Pages 86-87 -->

### Core Idea
Under a **pure float** in a flexible exchange rate regime, **e is determined by demand-supply in the forex market**. There is **no official intervention** and **no role for foreign exchange reserves**. **BOP equilibrium is achieved via exchange rate fluctuations**. With **perfect capital mobility**, this regime sharply differentiates the effectiveness of fiscal vs monetary policy.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine the rupee freely finds its own price every minute. The central bank doesn't touch it, doesn't need a dollar war chest, and any BOP imbalance gets erased by the rupee moving. In this world, government spending fizzles out, but printing money packs a punch.

### Key Concepts
- **Pure float**: no official intervention in currency markets, no role of forex reserves, BOP equilibrium achieved via exchange rate fluctuations.
- All analysis assumes **perfect capital mobility** ⇒ i = i_f at equilibrium.

### Definitions
- **Pure float (flexible regime, in Unit 5)**: regime with no official intervention in currency markets, no role of forex reserves; BOP balance restored via e fluctuations. ⭐

## Section: 5.3.1 Fiscal Policy under Flexible Exchange Rates 🔴
<!-- Pages 86-87 -->

### Core Idea
**Expansionary fiscal policy is INEFFECTIVE under flexible exchange rates with perfect capital mobility.** The IS curve initially shifts right but is then pushed all the way back to its original position by **currency appreciation** that worsens NX. Output returns to Y_0; trade balance **worsens**; fiscal position deteriorates.

> **In Simple Terms:** Government spends more → interest rate inches up → foreigners pile in to buy domestic bonds → rupee appreciates → exports fall, imports rise → net exports collapse and exactly cancel the original spending boost. You end up where you started, but with a weaker trade balance and a larger fiscal deficit.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup (Fig. 5.2)
- Initial equilibrium **E_0**: goods + money markets in equilibrium, BOP equilibrium with **i_0 = i_f**.
- Fiscal expansion: **↑G** or **fall in tax rate t**.

#### Step-by-step adjustment

| Step | Variable change | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **IS₀ → IS₁** | Fiscal expansion raises AD |
| 2 | **i: i_0 → i_1** (i_1 > i_f) | Economy moves to new IS-LM intersection (point E_1') |
| 3 | Domestic bonds more attractive (i_1 > i_f) | **Surge in capital inflows** |
| 4 | **BOP surplus** (excess supply of forex) | Capital inflows exceed CA needs |
| 5 | **e falls (currency appreciation)** | Forex market clears via e adjustment (pure float) |
| 6 | With given prices: **r falls** (real appreciation) | Nominal & real move together when p_f, p_d fixed |
| 7 | **X ↓, M ↑, NX ↓** | Trade balance worsens |
| 8 | **AD ↓ ⇒ IS₁ shifts back leftward** | Eventually all the way back to **IS₀** |
| 9 | New equilibrium **E_0** again | i = i_0 = i_f, output back to **Y_0** |

#### Why IS shifts back fully (not partially)
- At any intermediate position (e.g., **IS₂**), **i₂ > i_f** persists.
- ⇒ e, r, NX would continue to fall ⇒ IS would keep shifting left.
- Process stops only when IS = IS₀ ⇒ i = i_f again.

#### Final outcome — three-fold inefficacy
1. **Output unchanged**: increase from Y_0 to Y_1 is **only temporary**.
2. **Trade balance worsens**: NX is lower because of currency appreciation.
3. **Fiscal position deteriorates**: G has increased (or t has fallen).

### Definitions
- **Expansionary fiscal policy (open econ)**: ↑G or ↓t. ⭐
- **Fiscal ineffectiveness under flexible rates**: result that fiscal expansion fails to raise output in the long-run when capital is perfectly mobile and e floats. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Fiscal expansion under flexible ER chain:**
↑G (or ↓t) → IS right → i↑ above i_f → capital inflows → BOP surplus → **e ↓ (appreciation)** → r ↓ → X↓, M↑, NX ↓ → AD ↓ → IS shifts back to IS₀ → Y back to Y₀, i back to i_f.

### Examples
**Worked example (Fig. 5.2):**
- E_0 = (Y_0, i_0) with i_0 = i_f and IS₀, LM(M̄/p).
- Fiscal expansion shifts IS₀ → IS₁; would-be E_1' has i_1 > i_f.
- Capital inflows + currency appreciation → IS shifts left through IS₂ → all the way to IS₀.
- Final: economy back at E_0, but G is higher and NX is lower.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Fiscal expansion always raises output." → ✅ Under flexible ER + perfect capital mobility, output increase is **only temporary**.
- ❌ "IS shifts back only partway." → ✅ It shifts **all the way back to IS₀** because any i > i_f keeps appreciation going.
- ❌ "Trade balance is unaffected." → ✅ Trade balance **worsens** due to appreciation; NX falls.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- This conclusion depends on **perfect capital mobility** + **flexible (pure-float) exchange rate**. With imperfect mobility, fiscal policy is partly effective.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Flexible ER + perfect capital mobility ⇒ fiscal expansion ineffective.**
> - Channel: **i↑ → capital inflows → e appreciates → NX ↓ → IS reverts.**
> - Final triple: Y unchanged, NX worse, fiscal position worse.

### Connections
- Builds on: 5.2.3 BP horizontal at i_f under perfect mobility (Chunk 009: 4.3.2 (cont.) Exchange Rate — Nominal, Real, Regimes).
- Contrasts with: 5.4.1 (fiscal under fixed ER — extremely effective).

## Section: 5.3.2 Monetary Policy under Flexible Exchange Rates 🔴
<!-- Pages 87-88 -->

### Core Idea
**Expansionary monetary policy is EXTREMELY EFFECTIVE under flexible exchange rates with perfect capital mobility.** ↑M^s shifts LM out → i falls below i_f → capital outflows → currency depreciation → NX rises → IS shifts right until i is restored to i_f. Output rises **all the way to Y_3**; trade balance **improves**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Print more money → interest rate dips → money flees to higher-yielding foreign bonds → rupee weakens → exports become cheaper, imports costlier → net exports surge → output gets a big boost. Everything that hurt fiscal policy here helps monetary policy.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup (Fig. 5.3)
- Initial equilibrium **E_0** at (Y_0, i_0), with i_0 = i_f, IS₀, LM_0(M_0/p).
- Policy: **↑M^s from M_0 to M_1** ⇒ LM_0 → LM_1 (shifts outward).

#### Step-by-step adjustment

| Step | Variable change | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **LM_0 → LM_1** | Nominal money supply rises (M_0 → M_1) |
| 2 | Move to E_1: i falls **i_0 → i_1** (i_1 < i_f) | New IS-LM intersection on IS₀ |
| 3 | Internal balance OK; external imbalance | Goods + money markets clear; BOP disequilibrium |
| 4 | i_1 < i_f ⇒ **capital outflows** | Investors prefer foreign bonds |
| 5 | **BOP deficit** (excess demand for dollars) | Outflows exceed inflows |
| 6 | **e rises (nominal depreciation)** | Forex market clears via e |
| 7 | **r rises (real depreciation)** | Given p_f, p_d ⇒ same direction |
| 8 | **X ↑, M ↓, NX ↑** | Trade balance improves |
| 9 | **IS₀ → IS₃ (shifts right)** | Higher external demand boosts AD |
| 10 | New equilibrium **E_3** at (Y_3, i_f) | Internal + external balance restored |

#### Why IS shifts all the way to IS_3
- At any intermediate IS (e.g., IS_2), **i_2 < i_f** still.
- ⇒ Continued depreciation (e and r still rising) ⇒ NX rising ⇒ IS keeps shifting right.
- Process continues until **i = i_f** again, by then IS = IS_3 and Y = Y_3.

#### Why monetary expansion is "extremely effective"
1. Currency **depreciation** improves trade balance (NX ↑).
2. Output rises from **Y_0 all the way to Y_3** (large boost).
3. Operates in **complete contrast** to fiscal policy under flexible ER.

### Definitions
- **Expansionary monetary policy (open econ)**: ↑ nominal money supply (open market purchase, etc.). ⭐
- **Monetary effectiveness under flexible rates**: result that monetary expansion is extremely effective in raising output when capital is perfectly mobile and e floats. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Monetary expansion under flexible ER chain:**
↑M^s → LM right → i↓ below i_f → capital outflows → BOP deficit → **e ↑ (depreciation)** → r ↑ → X↑, M↓, NX ↑ → AD ↑ → IS shifts right to IS_3 → Y rises to Y_3, i back to i_f.

### Examples
**Worked example (Fig. 5.3):**
- E_0 = (Y_0, i_0) with i_0 = i_f.
- ↑M^s: M_0 → M_1; LM_0 → LM_1; would-be E_1 has i_1 < i_f.
- Capital outflows + currency depreciation → IS shifts right through IS_2 → IS_3.
- Final E_3 = (Y_3, i_f); large output gain + improved NX.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Monetary policy works mainly through investment." → ✅ In open economy with flexible ER, **NX channel** dominates.
- ❌ "Currency depreciation is bad for output." → ✅ It **boosts** output by raising NX (when M-L holds).
- ❌ "i falls and stays below i_f." → ✅ i is restored to i_f; the **output level changes**, not the equilibrium interest rate.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Conclusion depends on **perfect capital mobility** + **pure float** + **Marshall-Lerner condition** (assumed satisfied throughout).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Flexible ER + perfect capital mobility ⇒ monetary expansion VERY effective.**
> - Channel: **i↓ → capital outflows → e depreciates → NX ↑ → IS shifts right.**
> - Output rises from Y_0 to Y_3 (large); trade balance improves.

### Connections
- Builds on: 5.3.1 (mirror-image setup) — flexible ER + perfect capital mobility.
- Contrasts with: 5.4.2 (monetary under fixed ER — totally ineffective).

## Section: 5.4 Monetary and Fiscal Policies under Fixed Exchange Rate 🔴
<!-- Page 88 -->

### Core Idea
Under fixed exchange rates, **e fluctuations are ruled out**. The **central bank holds forex reserves and intervenes in forex markets to maintain the fixed parity**. The CB plays a very important role; this fundamentally changes the monetary mechanism since **money supply becomes endogenous** (passive to forex flows).

> **In Simple Terms:** The exchange rate is bolted in place. Whenever forex demand or supply shifts, the central bank steps in with reserves to keep e at the official parity. That removes the e-channel that mattered under flexible rates and instead pumps reserves and money supply up or down as a side effect.

### Key Concepts
- **Fixed exchange rate regime**: e fluctuations ruled out.
- **CB role**: holds forex reserves, intervenes to maintain fixed parity.
- Implication preview: the e-channel is shut, so the **money-supply channel** becomes endogenous.

### Definitions
- **Fixed exchange rate regime (Unit 5 setup)**: regime in which e fluctuations are ruled out; CB defends fixed parity using reserves. ⭐

### Connections
- Builds on: 5.3 (mirror-image regime).
- Feeds into: 5.4.1 (fiscal) and 5.4.2 (monetary).

## Section: 5.4.1 Fiscal Policy under Fixed Exchange Rates 🔴
<!-- Pages 88-89 -->

### Core Idea
**Fiscal policy is EXTREMELY EFFECTIVE under fixed exchange rates with perfect capital mobility.** ↑G shifts IS right → i rises above i_f → capital inflows → BOP surplus → CB buys dollars to defend parity → forex reserves rise → money supply rises → LM shifts out — until i = i_f again at higher Y. **No crowding out** because monetary expansion accommodates fiscal expansion.

> **In Simple Terms:** Government spends → bond yields rise → foreigners want in → dollars pour in → CB has to buy dollars to keep e fixed → that pumps domestic money into the economy → LM shifts out → interest rate falls back, but at a much higher output. Fiscal policy gets a free monetary boost.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup (Fig. 5.4)
- e = e* (the official parity).
- Initial E_0 = (Y_0, i_0) with i_0 = i_f.
- Fiscal expansion: ↑G or ↓T.

#### Step-by-step adjustment

| Step | Variable change | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **IS_0 → IS_1** | Fiscal expansion |
| 2 | i: i_0 → i_1 (i_1 > i_f) | Move toward new IS-LM intersection |
| 3 | i_1 > i_f ⇒ **capital inflows** | Higher domestic returns |
| 4 | **BOP surplus**, excess supply of dollars | Inflows > needs |
| 5 | **Pressure for currency appreciation** | But CB committed to fixed parity |
| 6 | **CB buys dollars** to mop up excess supply | Defends parity |
| 7 | **Forex reserves ↑ ⇒ M^s ↑** | Money supply expands |
| 8 | **LM_0 → LM_1 (shifts outward)** | Continues as long as i > i_f |
| 9 | New equilibrium **E_1** at (Y_1, i_f) | M_1 > M_0; **no crowding out** |

#### Why fiscal policy is "extremely effective"
- Change in **forex reserves** brings about **monetary expansion** to **accommodate** the fiscal expansion.
- This is in **complete contrast** to flexible ER (where fiscal was ineffective).
- **Output rises from Y_0 to Y_1**.
- **No crowding out** of private investment via interest rates (i restored to i_f).

### Definitions
- **Fiscal effectiveness under fixed rates**: result that fiscal expansion raises output substantially with no crowding out, since money supply endogenously expands via reserve inflows. ⭐
- **No crowding out (in fixed-rate Mundell-Fleming)**: i is held at i_f by capital flows ⇒ private investment unaffected by ↑G. ⭐
- **Accommodative monetary expansion**: passive ↑M^s via forex reserve buildup that supports fiscal expansion under fixed rates. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Fiscal expansion under fixed ER chain:**
↑G (or ↓T) → IS right → i↑ above i_f → capital inflows → BOP surplus → CB buys dollars (defends parity) → reserves ↑ → M^s ↑ → LM right → continues until i back to i_f → final Y_1 > Y_0, no crowding out.

### Examples
**Worked example (Fig. 5.4):**
- E_0 at (Y_0, i_0 = i_f), IS₀, LM(M_0/p).
- ↑G shifts IS₀ → IS_1, i would rise to i_1.
- Capital inflows → CB buys dollars → reserves ↑ → M_0 → M_1.
- LM shifts to LM_1 (M_1/p).
- Final E_1 at (Y_1, i_f) with i = i_f restored.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Fiscal expansion crowds out private I." → ✅ Under fixed ER + perfect mobility, **no crowding out** — i is pinned at i_f.
- ❌ "Money supply doesn't respond." → ✅ M^s **endogenously rises** because CB must buy dollars to keep parity.
- ❌ "Effects same as flexible ER." → ✅ Fiscal is extremely effective under fixed but ineffective under flexible — **complete contrast**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Fixed ER + perfect capital mobility ⇒ fiscal expansion VERY effective; no crowding out.**
> - Channel: **i↑ → capital inflows → CB buys dollars → reserves ↑ → M^s ↑ → LM shifts right → i back to i_f at higher Y.**
> - Money supply is **endogenous** (passive accommodator).

## Section: 5.4.2 Monetary Policy under Fixed Exchange Rates 🔴
<!-- Pages 90-91 -->

### Core Idea
**Monetary policy is INEFFECTIVE under fixed exchange rates with perfect capital mobility.** Open market purchase shifts LM out → i falls below i_f → capital outflows → BOP deficit → CB must sell dollars to defend parity → forex reserves fall → money supply contracts → LM shifts back to its original position. **Output unchanged in the short run.** **Money supply becomes endogenous.** This is the **policy trilemma**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Central bank tries to print money → interest rate dips → money rushes out to foreign bonds → CB has to sell dollars to keep the rupee at parity → that drains domestic money out → printing was undone. The CB literally cannot run an independent monetary policy.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup (Fig. 5.5)
- Initial equilibrium E_0 = (Y_0, i_0) with i_0 = i_f.
- Policy: CB does open market **purchase** of bonds.

#### Step-by-step adjustment

| Step | Variable change | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **LM_0 → LM_1** | M_0 → M_1 via OMO |
| 2 | i: i_0 → i_1 (i_1 < i_f) | Toward new IS-LM intersection |
| 3 | i_1 < i_f ⇒ **capital outflows** | Investors prefer foreign bonds |
| 4 | **BOP deficit, excess demand for dollars** | Outflows > inflows |
| 5 | **Pressure for currency depreciation** | But CB committed to fixed parity |
| 6 | **CB sells dollars against rupees** | Defends parity |
| 7 | **Forex reserves ↓ ⇒ M^s ↓** | Money supply contracts |
| 8 | **LM_1 → LM_0 (shifts left)** | Continues as long as i < i_f |
| 9 | Back to **E_0** at (Y_0, i_f) | M^s back to M_0 |

#### Final outcome
- **Money supply unchanged** (returned to M_0).
- **Output unchanged** at Y_0.
- **Monetary expansion is INEFFECTIVE in the short run** under fixed ER with perfect capital mobility.

#### Endogeneity of Money Supply
- Money supply becomes **endogenous** with fixed exchange rates and perfect capital mobility.
- It **passively adjusts** to changes in forex reserves as CB intervenes to defend the fixed parity.
- Since CB must hold e constant, it must intervene whenever there's pressure for depreciation or appreciation; reserves and hence M^s adjust accordingly.

#### The Policy Trilemma ⭐⭐⭐
**Impossible to simultaneously have all three:**
1. **Perfect capital mobility**.
2. **Independent monetary policy**.
3. **Fixed exchange rates**.

| Combination chosen | What is sacrificed | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect mobility + Fixed ER | Independent monetary policy | Money supply endogenous; CB cannot run independent MP (Section 5.4.2) |
| Perfect mobility + Independent MP | Fixed ER | Flexible ER; MP very effective (Section 5.3.2) |
| Fixed ER + Independent MP | Perfect capital mobility | Capital controls/restrictions; persistent i ≠ i_f |

### Definitions
- **Endogeneity of money supply**: under fixed ER + perfect capital mobility, M^s passively adjusts to forex-reserve changes as CB defends the parity. ⭐
- **Policy trilemma (impossible trinity)**: a country cannot simultaneously have perfect capital mobility, independent monetary policy, and fixed exchange rates — it can have at most two of the three. ⭐
- **Monetary ineffectiveness under fixed rates (perfect mobility)**: result that ↑M^s leaves output unchanged in the short run because reserves drain restores M^s. ⭐
- **Independent monetary policy**: ability of CB to set M^s (or i) on its own without external constraint. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Monetary expansion under fixed ER chain:**
↑M^s → LM right → i↓ below i_f → capital outflows → BOP deficit → CB sells dollars (defends parity) → reserves ↓ → M^s ↓ → LM shifts back left → continues until i back to i_f → final Y unchanged.

**Trilemma derivation:**
1. Perfect capital mobility ⇒ i = i_f at equilibrium.
2. Fixed ER ⇒ CB must defend e using reserves.
3. ⇒ M^s adjusts passively to forex flows.
4. ⇒ CB cannot independently choose M^s ⇒ no independent MP.

### Examples
**Worked example (Fig. 5.5):**
- E_0 at (Y_0, i_0 = i_f), IS₀, LM(M_0/p).
- OMO purchase: M_0 → M_1; LM_0 → LM_1; would-be E_1 has i_1 < i_f.
- Capital outflows → CB sells dollars → reserves ↓ → M_1 → M_0.
- LM shifts back to LM_0.
- Final: back to E_0 = (Y_0, i_f).

**Real-world example of the trilemma (textbook):**
Countries can adopt fixed ER and retain monetary independence **only with restrictions on international capital flows** (giving up perfect mobility). Then i ≠ i_f persists.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "OMO always changes M^s." → ✅ Under fixed ER + perfect mobility, the OMO is **fully reversed** by reserve drain; M^s ends unchanged.
- ❌ "Trilemma means none of the three are possible." → ✅ It means **at most two of the three** simultaneously.
- ❌ "Endogenous M^s is a choice." → ✅ It's a **constraint** imposed by the regime (fixed ER) plus perfect mobility.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- "Money supply becomes ineffective as a policy tool" — this is in the **short run**. In long run analyses, longer-term real adjustments may differ.
- With **capital controls** (no perfect mobility), countries can have fixed ER + independent MP, but interest rate differentials between countries persist owing to mobility restrictions.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Fixed ER + perfect capital mobility ⇒ monetary policy INEFFECTIVE.**
> - Channel: **i↓ → capital outflows → CB sells dollars → reserves ↓ → M^s ↓ → LM shifts back.**
> - **M^s endogenous**; CB cannot run independent MP.
> - **Policy trilemma**: at most 2 of {perfect mobility, independent MP, fixed ER}.

- Connects across: 5.3.1, 5.3.2, 5.4.1.

## Section: 5.5 Let Us Sum Up (Unit 5) 🔴
<!-- Pages 92-93 -->

### Core Idea (verbatim trace)
- Discussed the **open economy IS-LM model**.
- **International trade** affects **aggregate demand** in an economy.
- **Capital flows** play an important role in shaping policy outcomes through their impact on **interest rates, exchange rates, and money supply**.
- With **fixed exchange rates and perfect capital mobility, money supply becomes endogenous** ⇒ countries cannot conduct independent monetary policy.
- **Fiscal policy is effective when exchange rate is fixed.**
- With **flexible exchange rates and perfect capital mobility, fiscal expansion becomes ineffective** — has no effect on output and worsens trade balance.
- The **effectiveness of monetary policy under flexible exchange rates** demonstrates that countries can have **at most any two of the three policy choices** of having (i) independent monetary policy, (ii) fixed exchange rates, and (iii) perfect capital mobility.

### Master Summary Table — Mundell–Fleming Policy Effectiveness ⭐⭐⭐

| Policy | Flexible ER + Perfect Capital Mobility | Fixed ER + Perfect Capital Mobility |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal expansion (↑G or ↓T)** | **INEFFECTIVE.** IS shifts back via e appreciation; Y unchanged; NX worsens; fiscal position deteriorates. | **EXTREMELY EFFECTIVE.** ↑M^s endogenous via reserves; LM shifts out; Y rises; **no crowding out**. |
| **Monetary expansion (↑M^s)** | **EXTREMELY EFFECTIVE.** e depreciates, NX rises, IS shifts right; Y rises substantially; trade balance improves. | **INEFFECTIVE.** Reserves drain reverses ΔM^s; LM shifts back; Y unchanged; M^s endogenous. |
| **Independent MP possible?** | **Yes.** | **No** — M^s endogenous. |

### Three-Choice Trilemma ⭐⭐⭐
Pick **at most two** of:
1. Independent monetary policy.
2. Fixed exchange rates.
3. Perfect capital mobility.
