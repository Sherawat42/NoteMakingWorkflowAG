## Section: Unit 13 Objectives and Introduction 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 13 explains why prices and wages can fail to adjust in the real world and how this rigidity produces persistent unemployment. The central tension is between the Classical/New Classical view of perfectly flexible prices (producing full employment automatically) and the Keynesian/New Keynesian view of price-wage rigidity (producing involuntary unemployment when aggregate demand falls).

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine the economy as a thermostat. Classicals say it adjusts instantly — prices fall and everyone keeps a job. Keynesians say it sticks — when demand drops, the price doesn't fall fast enough, so output and jobs are cut instead.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical adjustment vs Keynesian adjustment
When aggregate demand falls at full employment, the Classical school says wages and prices fall (output and employment stay the same). The Keynesian school says wages/prices are sticky, so the adjustment hits employment and output instead. This single difference — *what gives way when demand changes* — explains why the two schools disagree about persistent unemployment.

| Aspect | Classical / New Classical | Keynesian / New Keynesian |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Wages & prices | Perfectly flexible | Rigid (downward, and even upward) |
| Market structure | Perfect competition | Imperfect competition (price-setters) |
| Adjustment to ↓demand | Prices fall, employment unchanged | Output & employment fall, prices sticky |
| Persistent unemployment | Impossible | Possible / typical |

### Definitions
- **Nominal rigidity**: Nominal prices and wages do not change when conditions call for change. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Real rigidity**: Imperfections in the goods, labour, or credit markets that prevent **relative** prices and **real** wages from adjusting as a Walrasian competitive model would predict. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Classical = flexibility = full employment.
> - Keynesian = rigidity = involuntary unemployment.
> - In practice, the issue is the *rate* of adjustment, not all-or-nothing.

## Section: New Classical School versus New Keynesian School 🔴

### Core Idea
The New Classical School (1970s) revived perfect competition + wage-price flexibility under rational expectations and real business cycles, denying short-run Keynesian unemployment. The New Keynesian counter-revolution (1980s) accepted the New Classical *tools* — optimisation, rational expectations — but built models with **imperfect competition** that show why rational firms keep prices fixed even when fundamentals would suggest a change. The result: theoretically rigorous Keynesian conclusions.

> **In Simple Terms:** New Classicals said "every firm is rational, so prices must move with money supply." New Keynesians replied: "even rational firms in monopolistic markets will sit on prices because the gain from changing isn't worth the cost — and we'll prove it using your own math."

### Key Concepts

#### How New Keynesian differs from (and resembles) New Classical
**Different — Imperfect Competition**: New Classicals assume perfect competition (price-takers). New Keynesians work with markets where firms have monopoly power (price-setters). This is the empirically realistic foundation.

**Same — Analytical Tools**: Both schools use optimising rational individuals and rational expectations. There is nothing intrinsic to rational expectations that ties it to either school — New Keynesian models are equally rigorous.

| Feature | New Classical | New Keynesian |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| Market structure | Perfect competition | Imperfect competition (price-setters) |
| Optimising agents | Yes | Yes |
| Rational expectations | Yes | Yes |
| Wage/price flexibility | Yes | No (sticky) |
| Predicts booms & busts | No | Yes |

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Keynes (1930s) → Classical orthodoxy challenged → Keynesianism becomes orthodox
2. Neo-Classical synthesis (IS-LM, then AS-AD) absorbs Keynes — Keynesian short run, Classical long run
3. New Classical revolution (1970s, rational expectations, RBC) → denies Keynesian unemployment
4. New Keynesian counter-revolution (1980s) → uses NC tools + imperfect competition to rebuild Keynesian results

### Connections
- Builds on: Rational Expectations (Unit 7) and Real Business Cycles (Unit 12)
- Continues into: Wage rigidity theories (Chunk 002)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - New Keynesians = imperfect competition + same optimisation tools as New Classicals
> - New Keynesians explain *upward* price rigidity even when money supply rises

## Section: Nominal Rigidities versus Real Rigidities 🔴

### Core Idea
Nominal rigidity is about the **money price/wage** failing to move. Real rigidity is about the **real wage** or **relative price** sitting at a non-market-clearing level for *voluntary, rational* reasons — e.g., firms paying above-market wages to keep workers efficient. Both produce unemployment, but through different routes.

> **In Simple Terms:** Nominal rigidity is "the price tag won't change." Real rigidity is "even if prices were free to move, firms would still choose to pay above-market wages because it pays off."

### Key Concepts

#### The crucial distinction
Nominal rigidity = sticky $-prices and $-wages (firms *can't* or won't change them quickly). Real rigidity = real wages above market-clearing because firms *deliberately* choose this — perhaps to raise efficiency, or because of collective bargaining, or because of imperfections in goods/credit markets.

| Aspect | Nominal Rigidity | Real Rigidity |
|--------|------------------|---------------|
| What's stuck? | Money prices, money wages | Real wages, relative prices |
| Where it lives | Goods markets (menu costs) | Goods, labour, credit markets |
| Why it happens | Costs of price change | Voluntary firm/worker behaviour, market imperfections |
| Example | Restaurant doesn't reprint menu | Firm pays efficiency wage above market |

### Definitions
- **Nominal rigidity**: nominal prices/wages do not change when conditions call for change (leads to Keynesian unemployment). ⭐
- **Real rigidity**: imperfections in goods, labour, or credit markets that prevent real wages and relative prices from adjusting to clear those markets. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - New Keynesians invoke **both** nominal and real rigidities.
> - Real wage > market-clearing wage → unemployment of willing workers.

## Section: Nominal Rigidities and Menu Costs 🔴

### Core Idea
Firms with monopoly power face small but non-trivial costs of changing prices ("menu costs"). Even when the macroeconomy calls for a price change (e.g., rise in money supply), the firm's *private* incremental profit from changing the price can be smaller than these menu costs — so the firm rationally leaves the price alone. The price level stays fixed; output and employment do the adjusting instead.

> **In Simple Terms:** A restaurant doesn't reprint its menu every time inflation ticks up by 1%. The cost of reprinting and annoying customers is bigger than the lost profit from holding prices. Multiply this across the economy and you get the Keynesian conclusion that money supply changes hit *output*, not prices.

### Key Concepts

#### Menu costs — the broad concept
Originally the literal cost of reprinting restaurant menus, but the term covers any cost of changing prices: reprogramming vending machines, recalibrating coin-operated devices, plus the "irritability" cost of losing customers fed up with constant small price changes. In a modern economy with automated dispensers and pay-points, these costs are widespread.

#### Mankiw Model of Nominal Rigidities (formal logic)
The model relies on monopolistic firms facing **downward-sloping demand** (less than infinitely elastic). A firm setting a price *off* the optimal profit-maximising price doesn't lose huge demand — only a sliver of profit. So the *incremental* gain from re-pricing is small. If menu costs exceed this small private gain, the firm holds price.

Crucially: in **perfect competition**, demand is horizontal (infinitely elastic), and even a small price deviation collapses demand — so there a competitive firm *must* re-price even with menu costs. Menu-cost rigidity is a **monopolistic phenomenon**.

#### The private vs social benefit gap
The firm reckons only its *private* incremental profit (small, often < menu cost). But the social cost of *not* changing the price is high — the resulting price rigidity causes economy-wide unemployment. So the private benefit from a price change is much smaller than the social benefit. This is the core New Keynesian insight: small private frictions → large social losses.

### Definitions
- **Menu costs**: costs incurred by price-setting firms in changing the prices they charge — including printing/reprogramming costs and customer-irritation/lost-clientele costs. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
Mankiw mechanism:
1. Money supply rises → optimal price for firm rises
2. Firm faces menu cost C of changing price
3. Firm's private profit gain from re-pricing = small (because monopolistic demand is inelastic and deviation from optimum is small)
4. If C > private gain → firm leaves price unchanged
5. All similar firms behave the same way → aggregate price level stays fixed
6. Real money supply ↑ → interest rate ↓ (or real-balance effect) → output ↑, employment ↑
7. Result: monetary expansion has *real* effects, not nominal — the Keynesian conclusion

### Examples
**Example: Restaurants** — Reprinting menu cards is cheap, but losing diners who dislike constantly changing prices is the bigger menu cost. The restaurant prefers to wait and re-price in larger, less-frequent jumps.

**Example: Vending machines / pay phones** — Adjusting coin-operated dispensers each time prices change is mechanically costly. Suppliers prefer to leave prices alone.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Menu costs are just the cost of reprinting menus." → ✅ Correct: They include all costs of changing prices — physical (reprogramming machines), and indirect (customer irritation, lost clientele).
- ❌ Mistake: "Menu costs apply equally in all market structures." → ✅ Correct: They generate rigidity *only* in monopolistic markets; perfectly competitive firms must re-price because horizontal demand makes off-optimum pricing catastrophic.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The Mankiw model justifies rigidity *especially when* (a) elasticity of demand is **low** (high monopoly power) and (b) the deviation from the optimum price is **small**.
- Not everyone agrees menu costs alone are quantitatively large enough to explain real-world rigidity (see Romer 2001 critique in next section).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Menu costs > private gain from re-pricing → price stays fixed → ↑M flows into output, not prices.
> - Works *only* under monopolistic competition (downward-sloping demand).
> - References: Mankiw (1985 QJE), Akerlof & Yellen (1985 QJE).

### Connections
- Builds on: Imperfect competition (microeconomics)
- Contrasts with: New Classical assumption of price flexibility
- Is foundation for: Real rigidities (next section), which extend the rigidity story when menu costs alone are too small

## Section: Real Rigidities 🔴

### Core Idea
A sceptical view (Romer 2001 and others) holds that **menu costs alone are too small** to produce realistic levels of nominal rigidity at the macro level. To make rigidity stick, the economy must also have **real rigidities** in the goods, labour, or credit markets — features that make demand shifts translate into *quantity* changes (output, employment) more than into *price* changes (mark-ups, real wages).

> **In Simple Terms:** Menu costs are the small lock on the door. Real rigidities are the chain and bolt that actually keep prices stuck. Without real rigidities, firms would happily eat the menu cost to grab the gain from re-pricing.

### Key Concepts

#### Why menu costs alone aren't enough
Romer (2001) shows that for realistic elasticity values — e.g., demand elasticity = 5, labour-supply elasticity = 0.1 — a 3% fall in output gives the firm a profit gain from re-pricing equal to about **one-fourth of revenue**. Firms would gladly pay menu costs up to ¼ of revenue to re-price. Since real menu costs are nowhere near that large, firms *would* re-price if menu costs were the only friction.

#### The signature of real rigidity
In real-world goods and labour markets, demand shifts produce **smaller variation in prices and larger variation in quantities** than perfect competition would predict. This rigidity in real prices (relative to quantities) is what "real rigidity" names.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Demand shock under real rigidity: ↑aggregate demand → mark-ups stay or fall → prices stay → output ↑ → employment ↑ (rather than: prices ↑, quantities unchanged)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Romer's critique: menu costs alone are too small given realistic elasticities.
> - Real rigidities = built-in features of markets that make quantities flex more than prices.
> - Three loci: goods market, labour market, credit market.

## Section: Real Rigidities in the Goods Market 🔴

### Core Idea
In imperfect competition, prices are set as a **mark-up over cost**, not at marginal cost. New Keynesians argue this mark-up is **countercyclical** — it falls in booms and rises in busts. Countercyclical mark-ups absorb demand shocks, keeping prices relatively stable while output flexes.

> **In Simple Terms:** When the economy heats up, sellers reluctantly trim their margins instead of jacking up prices. So prices look sticky and the boom shows up as more units sold, more workers hired.

### Key Concepts

#### Three reasons mark-ups are countercyclical
1. **"Thick-market" effects**: In booms, higher activity reduces information acquisition/dissemination costs. Markets become more competitive → mark-ups compressed.
2. **Collusion breakdown in oligopolies**: Booms increase the temptation for individual oligopolists to defect from collusive arrangements (because there's more to gain by undercutting). This pressure pushes mark-ups down.
3. **Pro-cyclical marginal costs**: Overtime wages — which are highly pro-cyclical and expensive — push marginal costs up in booms. If prices are rigid, the gap between price and marginal cost (the mark-up) is squeezed. (Note: this argument is partly circular — it presumes price rigidity rather than explaining it.)

### Definitions
- **Mark-up**: the amount by which price exceeds marginal cost in imperfect competition; covers fixed costs and profits. ⭐
- **Countercyclical mark-up**: mark-up falls in booms, rises in recessions. ⭐
- **Thick-market effects**: higher economic activity in booms lowers information costs, making markets more competitive. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Pro-cyclical marginal costs *cause* price rigidity." → ✅ Correct: They presume price rigidity. The first two arguments (thick-market, collusion breakdown) are independent reasons; the marginal-cost argument is more of a description of the squeeze given that prices are already rigid.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Imperfect competition → price = MC + mark-up
> - Mark-up countercyclical → price stable, quantities adjust
> - Three drivers: thick markets, oligopoly collusion break-up, pro-cyclical marginal costs

### Connections
- Builds on: Imperfect competition framework from Section 13.4
- Contrasts with: Walrasian model where price = marginal cost

## Section: Real Rigidities in the Credit Market 🔴

### Core Idea
Credit markets suffer from **asymmetric information** (borrowers know more than lenders about project quality and risk). This produces equilibrium credit rationing and gives the **credit channel** of monetary policy a much larger role than the textbook money-supply channel — explaining how small disturbances can become amplified financial collapses.

> **In Simple Terms:** Banks can't tell which borrowers are safe and which are risky, so when reserves fall, they don't just charge higher rates — they cut lending entirely. The economy shrinks because credit *vanishes*, not just because borrowing got more expensive.

### Key Concepts

#### Asymmetric information consequences
- Microeconomic: credit rationing in equilibrium, demand for financial intermediation, justification for government intervention.
- Macroeconomic: monetary policy works mainly through (i) the credit channel, not the money supply channel; (ii) credit rationing, not interest-rate increases.

#### Why banks are special
Banks act as **financial intermediaries** that overcome information asymmetries through screening, monitoring, and relationship lending. Other lenders cannot easily replace banks. So when restrictive monetary policy ↓bank reserves, the bank-credit shortfall is **not** filled by other lenders → aggregate demand falls.

#### Magnification of shocks
Credit imperfections **propagate and amplify** real disturbances. A shock that would only mildly hurt output under Walrasian credit can — under information asymmetries — trigger financial collapse, because shrinking output redistributes wealth from borrowers to lenders, worsening credit conditions, which further depresses output. The spiral is the magnification effect.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Restrictive monetary policy → ↓bank reserves → ↓bank lending capacity → credit rationing (not just higher rates) → ↓available loans to firms → ↓aggregate demand → ↓output, ↑unemployment

Magnification spiral: shock → ↓output and wealth redistribution borrowers→lenders → credit markets less efficient → further ↓output → financial collapse risk

### Definitions
- **Asymmetric information (credit market)**: borrowers know more about their project quality and probability of success than lenders do. ⭐
- **Credit channel**: the monetary-transmission mechanism that operates through the *availability* of bank loans rather than through the interest rate. ⭐
- **Credit rationing**: lenders restrict the *quantity* of credit (not just price) at the prevailing interest rate due to information asymmetries. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Credit market imperfection = information asymmetry between lenders and borrowers.
> - Credit channel > money-supply channel for monetary policy transmission.
> - Credit rationing > interest-rate hikes for restrictive policy.
> - Magnification: real shocks become bigger via credit distortions.

### Connections
- Parallels: Goods-market real rigidity (imperfections produce price rigidity; credit imperfections produce loan-quantity rigidity)

## Section: Real Rigidities in the Labour Market 🟡

### Core Idea
New Keynesian labour theories explain why **changes in aggregate demand cause large employment changes and only small real-wage changes** — and why involuntary unemployment persists. The leading mechanism: firms voluntarily pay above-market real wages to (a) raise efficiency or (b) deter shirking when worker effort can't be perfectly monitored. The full theory comes in the efficiency-wage model (Chunk 002).

> **In Simple Terms:** Firms choose to overpay workers because cheap labour is lazy labour. The overpayment is real-wage rigidity, and the leftover unemployed workers are involuntarily out of work even though they would happily work at the going wage.

### Key Concepts

#### Two facts to explain
1. Persistent involuntary unemployment exists.
2. Aggregate demand shifts produce large employment changes and small real-wage changes (real wages are only mildly pro-cyclical).

Both facts contradict the Walrasian labour market.
