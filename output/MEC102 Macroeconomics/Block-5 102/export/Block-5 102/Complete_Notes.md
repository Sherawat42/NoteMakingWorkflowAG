## Section: Unit 13 Objectives and Introduction 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 13 explains why prices and wages can fail to adjust in the real world and how this rigidity produces persistent unemployment. The central tension is between the Classical/New Classical view of perfectly flexible prices (producing full employment automatically) and the Keynesian/New Keynesian view of price-wage rigidity (producing involuntary unemployment when aggregate demand falls).

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine the economy as a thermostat. Classicals say it adjusts instantly — prices fall and everyone keeps a job. Keynesians say it sticks — when demand drops, the price doesn't fall fast enough, so output and jobs are cut instead.

### Key Concepts

#### Classical adjustment vs Keynesian adjustment
When aggregate demand falls at full employment, the Classical school says wages and prices fall (output and employment stay the same). The Keynesian school says wages/prices are sticky, so the adjustment hits employment and output instead. This single difference — *what gives way when demand changes* — explains why the two schools disagree about persistent unemployment.

| Aspect | Classical / New Classical | Keynesian / New Keynesian |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Wages & prices | Perfectly flexible | Rigid (downward, and even upward) |
| Market structure | Perfect competition | Imperfect competition (price-setters) |
| Adjustment to ↓demand | Prices fall, employment unchanged | Output & employment fall, prices sticky |
| Persistent unemployment | Impossible | Possible / typical |

### Definitions
- **Nominal rigidity**: Nominal prices and wages do not change when conditions call for change. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Real rigidity**: Imperfections in the goods, labour, or credit markets that prevent **relative** prices and **real** wages from adjusting as a Walrasian competitive model would predict. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Classical = flexibility = full employment.
> - Keynesian = rigidity = involuntary unemployment.
> - In practice, the issue is the *rate* of adjustment, not all-or-nothing.

## Section: New Classical School versus New Keynesian School 🔴

### Core Idea
The New Classical School (1970s) revived perfect competition + wage-price flexibility under rational expectations and real business cycles, denying short-run Keynesian unemployment. The New Keynesian counter-revolution (1980s) accepted the New Classical *tools* — optimisation, rational expectations — but built models with **imperfect competition** that show why rational firms keep prices fixed even when fundamentals would suggest a change. The result: theoretically rigorous Keynesian conclusions.

> **In Simple Terms:** New Classicals said "every firm is rational, so prices must move with money supply." New Keynesians replied: "even rational firms in monopolistic markets will sit on prices because the gain from changing isn't worth the cost — and we'll prove it using your own math."

### Key Concepts

#### How New Keynesian differs from (and resembles) New Classical
**Different — Imperfect Competition**: New Classicals assume perfect competition (price-takers). New Keynesians work with markets where firms have monopoly power (price-setters). This is the empirically realistic foundation.

**Same — Analytical Tools**: Both schools use optimising rational individuals and rational expectations. There is nothing intrinsic to rational expectations that ties it to either school — New Keynesian models are equally rigorous.

| Feature | New Classical | New Keynesian |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| Market structure | Perfect competition | Imperfect competition (price-setters) |
| Optimising agents | Yes | Yes |
| Rational expectations | Yes | Yes |
| Wage/price flexibility | Yes | No (sticky) |
| Predicts booms & busts | No | Yes |

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Keynes (1930s) → Classical orthodoxy challenged → Keynesianism becomes orthodox
2. Neo-Classical synthesis (IS-LM, then AS-AD) absorbs Keynes — Keynesian short run, Classical long run
3. New Classical revolution (1970s, rational expectations, RBC) → denies Keynesian unemployment
4. New Keynesian counter-revolution (1980s) → uses NC tools + imperfect competition to rebuild Keynesian results

### Connections
- Builds on: Rational Expectations (Unit 7) and Real Business Cycles (Unit 12)
- Continues into: Wage rigidity theories (Chunk 002: New-Keynesian Theories of Wage Rigidity)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - New Keynesians = imperfect competition + same optimisation tools as New Classicals
> - New Keynesians explain *upward* price rigidity even when money supply rises

## Section: Nominal Rigidities versus Real Rigidities 🔴

### Core Idea
Nominal rigidity is about the **money price/wage** failing to move. Real rigidity is about the **real wage** or **relative price** sitting at a non-market-clearing level for *voluntary, rational* reasons — e.g., firms paying above-market wages to keep workers efficient. Both produce unemployment, but through different routes.

> **In Simple Terms:** Nominal rigidity is "the price tag won't change." Real rigidity is "even if prices were free to move, firms would still choose to pay above-market wages because it pays off."

### Key Concepts

#### The crucial distinction
Nominal rigidity = sticky $-prices and $-wages (firms *can't* or won't change them quickly). Real rigidity = real wages above market-clearing because firms *deliberately* choose this — perhaps to raise efficiency, or because of collective bargaining, or because of imperfections in goods/credit markets.

| Aspect | Nominal Rigidity | Real Rigidity |
|--------|------------------|---------------|
| What's stuck? | Money prices, money wages | Real wages, relative prices |
| Where it lives | Goods markets (menu costs) | Goods, labour, credit markets |
| Why it happens | Costs of price change | Voluntary firm/worker behaviour, market imperfections |
| Example | Restaurant doesn't reprint menu | Firm pays efficiency wage above market |

### Definitions
- **Nominal rigidity**: nominal prices/wages do not change when conditions call for change (leads to Keynesian unemployment). ⭐
- **Real rigidity**: imperfections in goods, labour, or credit markets that prevent real wages and relative prices from adjusting to clear those markets. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - New Keynesians invoke **both** nominal and real rigidities.
> - Real wage > market-clearing wage → unemployment of willing workers.

## Section: Nominal Rigidities and Menu Costs 🔴

### Core Idea
Firms with monopoly power face small but non-trivial costs of changing prices ("menu costs"). Even when the macroeconomy calls for a price change (e.g., rise in money supply), the firm's *private* incremental profit from changing the price can be smaller than these menu costs — so the firm rationally leaves the price alone. The price level stays fixed; output and employment do the adjusting instead.

> **In Simple Terms:** A restaurant doesn't reprint its menu every time inflation ticks up by 1%. The cost of reprinting and annoying customers is bigger than the lost profit from holding prices. Multiply this across the economy and you get the Keynesian conclusion that money supply changes hit *output*, not prices.

### Key Concepts

#### Menu costs — the broad concept
Originally the literal cost of reprinting restaurant menus, but the term covers any cost of changing prices: reprogramming vending machines, recalibrating coin-operated devices, plus the "irritability" cost of losing customers fed up with constant small price changes. In a modern economy with automated dispensers and pay-points, these costs are widespread.

#### Mankiw Model of Nominal Rigidities (formal logic)
The model relies on monopolistic firms facing **downward-sloping demand** (less than infinitely elastic). A firm setting a price *off* the optimal profit-maximising price doesn't lose huge demand — only a sliver of profit. So the *incremental* gain from re-pricing is small. If menu costs exceed this small private gain, the firm holds price.

Crucially: in **perfect competition**, demand is horizontal (infinitely elastic), and even a small price deviation collapses demand — so there a competitive firm *must* re-price even with menu costs. Menu-cost rigidity is a **monopolistic phenomenon**.

#### The private vs social benefit gap
The firm reckons only its *private* incremental profit (small, often < menu cost). But the social cost of *not* changing the price is high — the resulting price rigidity causes economy-wide unemployment. So the private benefit from a price change is much smaller than the social benefit. This is the core New Keynesian insight: small private frictions → large social losses.

### Definitions
- **Menu costs**: costs incurred by price-setting firms in changing the prices they charge — including printing/reprogramming costs and customer-irritation/lost-clientele costs. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
Mankiw mechanism:
1. Money supply rises → optimal price for firm rises
2. Firm faces menu cost C of changing price
3. Firm's private profit gain from re-pricing = small (because monopolistic demand is inelastic and deviation from optimum is small)
4. If C > private gain → firm leaves price unchanged
5. All similar firms behave the same way → aggregate price level stays fixed
6. Real money supply ↑ → interest rate ↓ (or real-balance effect) → output ↑, employment ↑
7. Result: monetary expansion has *real* effects, not nominal — the Keynesian conclusion

### Examples
**Example: Restaurants** — Reprinting menu cards is cheap, but losing diners who dislike constantly changing prices is the bigger menu cost. The restaurant prefers to wait and re-price in larger, less-frequent jumps.

**Example: Vending machines / pay phones** — Adjusting coin-operated dispensers each time prices change is mechanically costly. Suppliers prefer to leave prices alone.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Menu costs are just the cost of reprinting menus." → ✅ Correct: They include all costs of changing prices — physical (reprogramming machines), and indirect (customer irritation, lost clientele).
- ❌ Mistake: "Menu costs apply equally in all market structures." → ✅ Correct: They generate rigidity *only* in monopolistic markets; perfectly competitive firms must re-price because horizontal demand makes off-optimum pricing catastrophic.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The Mankiw model justifies rigidity *especially when* (a) elasticity of demand is **low** (high monopoly power) and (b) the deviation from the optimum price is **small**.
- Not everyone agrees menu costs alone are quantitatively large enough to explain real-world rigidity (see Romer 2001 critique in next section).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Menu costs > private gain from re-pricing → price stays fixed → ↑M flows into output, not prices.
> - Works *only* under monopolistic competition (downward-sloping demand).
> - References: Mankiw (1985 QJE), Akerlof & Yellen (1985 QJE).

### Connections
- Builds on: Imperfect competition (microeconomics)
- Contrasts with: New Classical assumption of price flexibility
- Is foundation for: Real rigidities (next section), which extend the rigidity story when menu costs alone are too small

## Section: Real Rigidities 🔴

### Core Idea
A sceptical view (Romer 2001 and others) holds that **menu costs alone are too small** to produce realistic levels of nominal rigidity at the macro level. To make rigidity stick, the economy must also have **real rigidities** in the goods, labour, or credit markets — features that make demand shifts translate into *quantity* changes (output, employment) more than into *price* changes (mark-ups, real wages).

> **In Simple Terms:** Menu costs are the small lock on the door. Real rigidities are the chain and bolt that actually keep prices stuck. Without real rigidities, firms would happily eat the menu cost to grab the gain from re-pricing.

### Key Concepts

#### Why menu costs alone aren't enough
Romer (2001) shows that for realistic elasticity values — e.g., demand elasticity = 5, labour-supply elasticity = 0.1 — a 3% fall in output gives the firm a profit gain from re-pricing equal to about **one-fourth of revenue**. Firms would gladly pay menu costs up to ¼ of revenue to re-price. Since real menu costs are nowhere near that large, firms *would* re-price if menu costs were the only friction.

#### The signature of real rigidity
In real-world goods and labour markets, demand shifts produce **smaller variation in prices and larger variation in quantities** than perfect competition would predict. This rigidity in real prices (relative to quantities) is what "real rigidity" names.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Demand shock under real rigidity: ↑aggregate demand → mark-ups stay or fall → prices stay → output ↑ → employment ↑ (rather than: prices ↑, quantities unchanged)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Romer's critique: menu costs alone are too small given realistic elasticities.
> - Real rigidities = built-in features of markets that make quantities flex more than prices.
> - Three loci: goods market, labour market, credit market.

## Section: Real Rigidities in the Goods Market 🔴

### Core Idea
In imperfect competition, prices are set as a **mark-up over cost**, not at marginal cost. New Keynesians argue this mark-up is **countercyclical** — it falls in booms and rises in busts. Countercyclical mark-ups absorb demand shocks, keeping prices relatively stable while output flexes.

> **In Simple Terms:** When the economy heats up, sellers reluctantly trim their margins instead of jacking up prices. So prices look sticky and the boom shows up as more units sold, more workers hired.

### Key Concepts

#### Three reasons mark-ups are countercyclical
1. **"Thick-market" effects**: In booms, higher activity reduces information acquisition/dissemination costs. Markets become more competitive → mark-ups compressed.
2. **Collusion breakdown in oligopolies**: Booms increase the temptation for individual oligopolists to defect from collusive arrangements (because there's more to gain by undercutting). This pressure pushes mark-ups down.
3. **Pro-cyclical marginal costs**: Overtime wages — which are highly pro-cyclical and expensive — push marginal costs up in booms. If prices are rigid, the gap between price and marginal cost (the mark-up) is squeezed. (Note: this argument is partly circular — it presumes price rigidity rather than explaining it.)

### Definitions
- **Mark-up**: the amount by which price exceeds marginal cost in imperfect competition; covers fixed costs and profits. ⭐
- **Countercyclical mark-up**: mark-up falls in booms, rises in recessions. ⭐
- **Thick-market effects**: higher economic activity in booms lowers information costs, making markets more competitive. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Pro-cyclical marginal costs *cause* price rigidity." → ✅ Correct: They presume price rigidity. The first two arguments (thick-market, collusion breakdown) are independent reasons; the marginal-cost argument is more of a description of the squeeze given that prices are already rigid.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Imperfect competition → price = MC + mark-up
> - Mark-up countercyclical → price stable, quantities adjust
> - Three drivers: thick markets, oligopoly collusion break-up, pro-cyclical marginal costs

### Connections
- Builds on: Imperfect competition framework from Section 13.4
- Contrasts with: Walrasian model where price = marginal cost

## Section: Real Rigidities in the Credit Market 🔴

### Core Idea
Credit markets suffer from **asymmetric information** (borrowers know more than lenders about project quality and risk). This produces equilibrium credit rationing and gives the **credit channel** of monetary policy a much larger role than the textbook money-supply channel — explaining how small disturbances can become amplified financial collapses.

> **In Simple Terms:** Banks can't tell which borrowers are safe and which are risky, so when reserves fall, they don't just charge higher rates — they cut lending entirely. The economy shrinks because credit *vanishes*, not just because borrowing got more expensive.

### Key Concepts

#### Asymmetric information consequences
- Microeconomic: credit rationing in equilibrium, demand for financial intermediation, justification for government intervention.
- Macroeconomic: monetary policy works mainly through (i) the credit channel, not the money supply channel; (ii) credit rationing, not interest-rate increases.

#### Why banks are special
Banks act as **financial intermediaries** that overcome information asymmetries through screening, monitoring, and relationship lending. Other lenders cannot easily replace banks. So when restrictive monetary policy ↓bank reserves, the bank-credit shortfall is **not** filled by other lenders → aggregate demand falls.

#### Magnification of shocks
Credit imperfections **propagate and amplify** real disturbances. A shock that would only mildly hurt output under Walrasian credit can — under information asymmetries — trigger financial collapse, because shrinking output redistributes wealth from borrowers to lenders, worsening credit conditions, which further depresses output. The spiral is the magnification effect.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Restrictive monetary policy → ↓bank reserves → ↓bank lending capacity → credit rationing (not just higher rates) → ↓available loans to firms → ↓aggregate demand → ↓output, ↑unemployment

Magnification spiral: shock → ↓output and wealth redistribution borrowers→lenders → credit markets less efficient → further ↓output → financial collapse risk

### Definitions
- **Asymmetric information (credit market)**: borrowers know more about their project quality and probability of success than lenders do. ⭐
- **Credit channel**: the monetary-transmission mechanism that operates through the *availability* of bank loans rather than through the interest rate. ⭐
- **Credit rationing**: lenders restrict the *quantity* of credit (not just price) at the prevailing interest rate due to information asymmetries. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Credit market imperfection = information asymmetry between lenders and borrowers.
> - Credit channel > money-supply channel for monetary policy transmission.
> - Credit rationing > interest-rate hikes for restrictive policy.
> - Magnification: real shocks become bigger via credit distortions.

### Connections
- Parallels: Goods-market real rigidity (imperfections produce price rigidity; credit imperfections produce loan-quantity rigidity)

## Section: Real Rigidities in the Labour Market 🟡

### Core Idea
New Keynesian labour theories explain why **changes in aggregate demand cause large employment changes and only small real-wage changes** — and why involuntary unemployment persists. The leading mechanism: firms voluntarily pay above-market real wages to (a) raise efficiency or (b) deter shirking when worker effort can't be perfectly monitored. The full theory comes in the efficiency-wage model (Chunk 002: New-Keynesian Theories of Wage Rigidity).

> **In Simple Terms:** Firms choose to overpay workers because cheap labour is lazy labour. The overpayment is real-wage rigidity, and the leftover unemployed workers are involuntarily out of work even though they would happily work at the going wage.

### Key Concepts

#### Two facts to explain
1. Persistent involuntary unemployment exists.
2. Aggregate demand shifts produce large employment changes and small real-wage changes (real wages are only mildly pro-cyclical).

Both facts contradict the Walrasian labour market.

## Section: New-Keynesian Theories of Wage Rigidity 🔴

### Core Idea
The labour market has two stylised facts that any theory must explain: (1) **persistent unemployment**, and (2) **moderately pro-cyclical real wages** — when demand rises, employment goes up a lot and real wages go up a little. The original Keynesian model (constant nominal wage W) wrongly predicts **counter-cyclical** real wages; New Keynesian theories correct this by predicting mildly pro-cyclical real wages, matching the data.

> **In Simple Terms:** Old Keynesianism said "fix nominal wages and you're done." But that wrongly implies real wages fall in booms (because P rises). The data say real wages mildly *rise* in booms. New Keynesian theories had to do better — and they do.

### Key Concepts

#### Why old Keynesian fails on wage cyclicality
With nominal wage W fixed, real wage W/P moves *opposite* to P. In a boom, P rises → W/P falls → counter-cyclical real wage. In contraction, P falls → W/P rises. Empirically wrong: real wages in fact rise mildly in booms and fall in recessions (mildly pro-cyclical).

| Model | Predicted real-wage cyclicality | Matches data? |
|-------|--------------------------------|---------------|
| Classical | Real wage flexes with employment, equals MPL | Partial |
| Old Keynesian (W fixed) | Counter-cyclical (W/P falls in booms) | ❌ |
| New Keynesian | Mildly pro-cyclical | ✅ |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Two facts to explain: persistent unemployment + mildly pro-cyclical real wages.
> - Old Keynesian: gets unemployment right, gets wage cyclicality wrong.
> - New Keynesian: gets both right — that's the advance.

## Section: Efficiency-Wage Theories 🔴

### Core Idea
Efficiency-wage theories are **non-Walrasian**: they postulate that firms voluntarily pay real wages **above** market-clearing because doing so raises worker efficiency more than it raises wage costs. The unemployment that results is the natural by-product — there are simply more workers willing to work at the high wage than firms are willing to hire.

> **In Simple Terms:** Pay your workers a premium and they work harder, attract better candidates, stay loyal, and don't slack off. The premium more than pays for itself — but it leaves some willing workers without jobs.

### Key Concepts

#### Four reasons firms pay above-market wages
1. **Higher consumption → higher productivity (nutrition argument)**: Especially in low-income economies, higher wages let workers eat better, stay healthier, and work harder.
2. **Selection on reservation wage**: A higher wage attracts workers with higher reservation wages, who tend to have higher (unobservable) abilities — so the firm gets a better-quality applicant pool.
3. **Loyalty / morale / fair-wage**: A premium wage builds loyalty and effort. The flip side: under-paying produces anger, sabotage, revenge.
4. **Anti-shirking (Shapiro–Stiglitz logic)**: When effort cannot be monitored perfectly, paying above-market wages gives workers something to lose if caught shirking — the alternative job pays only the lower market-clearing wage.

### Definitions
- **Efficiency wage**: a real wage paid above market-clearing because higher wages raise effort/efficiency by more than the wage cost. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Reservation wage**: the minimum wage at which a worker is willing to supply labour. ⭐
- **Non-Walrasian theory**: any theory in which markets do not clear; here, the labour market with persistent unemployment. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Efficiency wage = chosen, voluntary, above-market real wage.
> - Four motives: nutrition, selection, loyalty, anti-shirking.
> - Result: persistent involuntary unemployment.

- Is foundation for: Efficiency Wage Model (next section)

## Section: Efficiency Wage Model — An Example (Romer 2001) 🔴

### Core Idea
A formal one-firm model with effort as a function of the real wage. Profit-maximisation gives a clean condition: **the optimal wage is set where the elasticity of effort with respect to the wage equals one**. At that point, the firm minimises the per-unit cost of *effective* labour. This single condition explains efficiency-wage rigidity, persistent unemployment, and price stickiness — all together.

> **In Simple Terms:** The firm doesn't ask "how cheap can I get a worker?" but "how cheap can I get a unit of effort?" That's a different number, and it's set higher than the market-clearing wage.

### Key Concepts

#### 13.8.1 Specification
- M identical firms, N identical workers.
- Single-input Neo-Classical production function: **Y = F(e·L)**, where L = physical workers hired, e = effort per worker, e·L = effective (efficiency) labour.
- Effort function: **e = e(w)** — effort is an increasing function of the real wage.
- F'(·) > 0, F''(·) < 0 (positive but diminishing marginal product of efficiency labour).
- Each of N workers supplies one unit of labour inelastically — higher wage raises effort, *not* hours.

#### 13.8.2 Solution
Profit: π = Y − w·L = F(e(w)·L) − w·L. Choose w and L to maximise.

The first-order condition with respect to L (after manipulation) gives the **Solow condition**:

**w · e'(w) / e(w) = 1**

i.e., the **elasticity of effort with respect to the wage equals 1** at the optimum.

**Economic interpretation**: w/e(w) is the cost per *efficiency unit* of labour. The firm picks w to **minimise w/e(w)** — getting the most effective labour per rupee. At the optimum:
- For w below optimum: ↑w yields more-than-proportional ↑e (still gaining)
- For w above optimum: ↑w yields less-than-proportional ↑e (now losing)
- At optimum: marginal product of effective labour = its cost

#### 13.8.3 Implications
Let **w*** and **L*** be the optimum. Total demand for labour = M·L*.

**(i) Unemployment can persist**: If N > M·L*, there are more workers than jobs at wage w*. Workers willing to work for less can't push the wage down — w* is set by effort considerations, not market-clearing. (If N < M·L*, wage is bid up above w* and there's no unemployment.)

**(ii) No real-wage response to demand shocks**: An increase in aggregate demand does *not* raise w* — the efficiency wage depends only on the effort function, not on demand conditions. So a demand shock raises **employment**, not real wages. This rationalises the empirical fact that real wages are only mildly pro-cyclical.

**(iii) Price rigidity is implied**: Since real wage and effort don't change in upswings, **labour costs don't change**, so price-setting firms have no incentive to adjust prices. Efficiency-wage rigidity → cost rigidity → price rigidity.

#### 13.8.4 Extensions of the model
The simple e = e(w) model has a flaw: it predicts *no* trend in the real wage in the long run, contradicting the empirical secular rise in real wages. Fix: extend the effort function to **e = e(w, w', u)**, where:
- w' = real wage available in alternative firms
- u = unemployment rate
- e is increasing in w, increasing in u, decreasing in w'

**Logic**: The threat of getting caught shirking only bites if (a) other firms pay less (w' low) and (b) you can't quickly find another job (u high). If w' is high and u is low, getting fired is no punishment, so high wages don't deter shirking.

In equilibrium with identical firms, w = w'. Unemployment can still arise if firms' total demand for L is below total supply of L at the common efficiency wage. The extended model produces the right short-run/long-run pattern: short-run demand shocks → larger employment effect; long-run growth → trend rise in real wages.

### Definitions
- **Effort function e(w)**: per-worker effort as an increasing function of the real wage. ⭐
- **Solow condition / first-order condition**: w·e'(w)/e(w) = 1 — elasticity of effort with respect to wage equals one at the optimum. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Per-unit cost of effective labour**: w/e(w); the firm chooses w to minimise this. ⭐
- **Extended effort function**: e(w, w', u) — effort depends on own wage, alternative wage, and unemployment rate. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Firm specifies effort function e(w)
2. Maximises π = F(e(w)·L) − w·L over (w, L)
3. FOC w.r.t. L gives w·e'(w)/e(w) = 1
4. This determines w* independently of demand
5. L* determined by F'(e·L) = w/e (marginal product of effective labour = its cost)
6. Aggregate demand for labour = M·L*
7. If N > M·L* → involuntary unemployment at w*

### Examples
**Worked example: interpreting the FOC**
- Suppose at w = ₹100, e(w) = 50, e'(w) = 0.5. Elasticity = 100·0.5/50 = 1. ✓ At optimum.
- Cost per efficiency unit = w/e(w) = 100/50 = 2.
- If w were ₹80 with e = 30, e' = 0.5: elasticity = 80·0.5/30 = 1.33 > 1. Below optimum — raising w further yields more effort gain than cost.
- If w were ₹120 with e = 60, e' = 0.4: elasticity = 120·0.4/60 = 0.8 < 1. Above optimum — raising w further wastes money.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "The efficiency wage equals the marginal product of labour." → ✅ Correct: It is set by minimising w/e(w); the FOC is about the elasticity of effort, not the value of MPL.
- ❌ Mistake: "Higher demand for output raises efficiency wage." → ✅ Correct: w* depends only on the effort function — demand changes affect L*, not w*.
- ❌ Mistake: "The simple model captures both short and long run." → ✅ Correct: It uniformly predicts employment effects only; the extended model with e(w, w', u) is needed for long-run real-wage trend.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- If M·L* > N at w*, the wage is bid above w* and unemployment vanishes — the model only explains unemployment in the "excess supply" case.
- The simple model is silent on the long-run secular rise in real wages. The extension repairs this.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Production: Y = F(e·L)
> - Effort: e = e(w), e increasing in w
> - FOC: **w·e'(w)/e(w) = 1** (elasticity of effort w.r.t. wage = 1)
> - Optimum: minimises w/e(w) (cost per efficiency unit)
> - Extension: e = e(w, w', u) — needed for long-run dynamics
> - Three implications: unemployment persists, no real-wage response to demand, price rigidity implied

- Reinforces: Price rigidity from Mankiw model (Chunk 001: Unit 13 Objectives and Introduction) — efficiency wages keep marginal costs constant, so prices stay constant
- Contrasts with: Walrasian labour market (always clears via wage adjustment)

## Section: Contracting and Insider-Outsider Models 🔴

### Core Idea
Wage rigidity can arise not from efficiency considerations but from **long-term relationships** between firms and workers. Workers stay in jobs for many years (US average ≈ 10 years) because of firm-specific skills and training costs. So wages are set by long-term contracts, not by spot-market clearing. Two contract types: **fixed-wage** (inefficient) and **implicit** (efficient). The **insider-outsider** extension shows why wage bargaining favours the employed at the expense of the unemployed.

> **In Simple Terms:** Workers and firms are stuck with each other for years, so they don't haggle wages every month — they sign contracts. The unemployed have no seat at the table, so wages stay too high and unemployment lingers.

### Key Concepts

#### Why long-term relationships matter
- Many jobs require **firm-specific skills** worth less outside the firm.
- Training new workers each period is **costly**.
- Workers stay if their *long-term* expected earnings exceed outside opportunities, even if current earnings are low.
- US average tenure ≈ 10 years.
- Hence wages don't adjust period-to-period to clear the labour market — labour market is **non-Walrasian**.

#### Two types of contracts

| Aspect | Fixed-wage contract | Implicit contract |
|--------|---------------------|-------------------|
| What's specified | Pre-determined wage; firm chooses employment | Wage AND employment for each state of economy |
| Stated explicitly? | Yes | No (implicit / understood) |
| Pareto efficient? | ❌ No — MPL ≠ marginal disutility | ✅ Yes |
| Wage rigidity implied? | Yes | Yes |
| Unemployment implied? | Yes | Yes |

**Fixed-wage contract**: Wage W is fixed; firm freely chooses employment L given the state of the economy. Workers supply whatever firm demands. When labour demand falls, firms reduce L at fixed W → unemployment. **Inefficient** because at the contract wage, marginal product of labour ≠ marginal disutility of work — a different deal could make both parties better off.

**Implicit contract**: Real-world contracts don't explicitly tie wage and employment to the state of the economy, but behave *as if* they do. The firm specifies (w, L) for each possible state. **Pareto efficient**, but still implies real wage rigidity and the same kind of unemployment consequences.

#### Insider-Outsider Models
Three categories of agents:
- **Firms**
- **Insiders**: currently-employed workers (have bargaining power via unions)
- **Outsiders**: unemployed workers (no bargaining power)

**Key insight**: The unemployed (outsiders) would *like* contracts that lower wages and raise employment. But they aren't at the bargaining table. The deal is struck between firms and insiders, who prefer high wages over high employment. So wages stay rigid and outsiders stay unemployed — a non-Walrasian feature explaining persistent unemployment.

### Definitions
- **Fixed-wage contract**: pre-specified wage; firm chooses employment freely; **inefficient** (MPL ≠ marginal disutility). ⭐
- **Implicit contract**: contract between firm and workers specifying both wage and employment for each state of the economy; not explicitly written but behavioural; **Pareto efficient**. ⭐
- **Pareto efficiency**: a contract is Pareto efficient if it is impossible to make one party better off without making the other worse off. ⭐
- **Insiders**: currently-employed workers, with bargaining power. ⭐
- **Outsiders**: unemployed workers, without bargaining power. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Implicit contracts are inefficient because they're vague." → ✅ Correct: They are *Pareto efficient*; the term "implicit" refers to their unwritten nature, not their efficiency.
- ❌ Mistake: "Fixed-wage contracts give the firm bad outcomes." → ✅ Correct: They are inefficient *for both parties together* — there's a different contract that would make both better off. The inefficiency is joint, not unilateral.
- ❌ Mistake: "Insider-outsider models are about wage discrimination." → ✅ Correct: They're about who has bargaining power — the *unemployed* would lobby for lower wages and more jobs, but they have no voice at the bargaining table.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Contracting models depart from efficiency-wage models: efficiency-wage rigidity comes from worker effort; contract rigidity comes from long-term relationships. Both produce real wage rigidity.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Fixed-wage contract = inefficient (MPL ≠ marginal disutility).
> - Implicit contract = efficient, but *also* implies real wage rigidity.
> - Insider-outsider: insiders + firms bargain; outsiders excluded → wages too high, unemployment persists.

### Connections
- Parallels: Efficiency wage model — both produce non-Walrasian labour market with real-wage rigidity, but for different reasons (effort vs. long-term relations).

## Section: Unit 14 Objectives and Introduction 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 14 introduces **search theory**: a Walrasian-compatible explanation of unemployment. Where New Keynesian theories (Unit 13) explained unemployment via market imperfections and rigidities, search theory shows that **unemployment can exist as an equilibrium phenomenon even within a perfectly competitive Walrasian framework** — because the labour market is heterogeneous and matching workers to jobs takes time and resources.

> **In Simple Terms:** Some workers are without jobs and some jobs are without workers — at the same time. Why? Because finding the right match isn't free. Search theory makes that friction the source of unemployment, without needing rigidity.

### Key Concepts

#### Why search theory matters
- The labour market suffers from a stylised fact: workers without jobs *and* jobs without workers coexist.
- Two structural reasons: (i) **labour is heterogeneous**, (ii) **recruitment costs** in time and money.
- Neoclassical theory only delivers voluntary unemployment, contradicting the persistent involuntary unemployment we see.
- Keynesian theory delivers involuntary unemployment but lacks microfoundations for wage rigidity.
- **Search theory bridges this gap**: equilibrium unemployment, microfounded, in a competitive setting.

| Theory | Type of unemployment | Microfoundations | Walrasian? |
|--------|---------------------|------------------|------------|
| Classical / Neoclassical | Voluntary only | Strong | Yes |
| Keynesian | Involuntary | Weak | No (rigidity) |
| New Keynesian | Involuntary | Strong | No (imperfect competition) |
| **Search & Matching** | **Involuntary, equilibrium** | **Strong** | **Yes** |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Search theory = unemployment in equilibrium *within* the Walrasian framework.
> - The friction is heterogeneous labour + costly matching, not market imperfection.

## Section: Search Theory and Theories of Unemployment — A Four-Way Classification 🔴

### Core Idea
Theories of unemployment can be classified by how a **firm responds when an unemployed worker offers to work for slightly less than the current wage**. The four possible responses correspond to four schools of thought: Neoclassical (accepts), Search & Matching (questions identity of worker), Contracting (constrained by collective agreements), and Efficiency Wage (refuses for efficiency reasons).

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine an unemployed person showing up at your door asking for a job at slightly lower pay. The four ways you can answer reveal four different theories of why unemployment exists.

### Key Concepts

#### The four responses

| Response | School | Mechanism | Type of unemployment |
|----------|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| (i) "Yes, you're hired at the lower wage" | Neoclassical / Walrasian | Wages clear the market | Voluntary only |
| (ii) "You're not the same as my workers — labour is heterogeneous" | **Search & Matching** | Heterogeneity + matching costs | Equilibrium frictional |
| (iii) "I'd like to but I'm bound by collective agreement" | Contracting models | Institutional wage-setting | Wage rigidity from contracts |
| (iv) "I won't lower wages — high wages give me efficiency" | Efficiency-Wage theories | Higher wage → higher productivity | Voluntary high wage |

Note: The course covers (iii) and (iv) under the New Keynesian banner — but their intellectual ancestry is captured by this four-way map.

### Definitions
- **Walrasian labour market**: a perfectly competitive labour market where wages adjust until demand equals supply; involuntary unemployment is impossible. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Voluntary unemployment**: unemployment of those unwilling to work at the going wage rate. ⭐
- **Involuntary unemployment**: unemployment of those willing to work at the going wage rate but unable to find work. ⭐
- **Search and matching model**: model in which heterogeneous workers and heterogeneous jobs are paired through a costly search-and-match process, generating equilibrium unemployment. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Search theory is a kind of New Keynesian theory." → ✅ Correct: Search theory is **Walrasian** — it doesn't require imperfect competition or wage rigidity. It belongs to a different lineage.
- ❌ Mistake: "All four responses produce the same unemployment." → ✅ Correct: Only the Neoclassical response (i) is consistent with voluntary-only unemployment. Responses (ii)-(iv) all generate involuntary unemployment, but for different reasons.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Four responses → four theories.
> - (i) Neoclassical = voluntary only.
> - (ii) Search = heterogeneity + matching costs.
> - (iii) Contracting = institutional rigidity (Unit 13).
> - (iv) Efficiency wage = chosen-high-wage rigidity (Unit 13).

## Section: Search Theories — A Brief Historical Overview 🟡

### Core Idea
Search theory has roots in **A.C. Pigou** (inter-war period), who used the idea of frictional unemployment from workers shifting jobs. It was formalised in the 1970s by Edmund Phelps and others, then developed into rigorous general-equilibrium models in the 1980s by **Peter Diamond (1981)** and **Christopher Pissarides (1985)**, and into business-cycle search models like **Howitt (1988)**.

> **In Simple Terms:** The seed was Pigou nearly a century ago: "people are unemployed because they're between jobs." The 1970s and 1980s built that seed into rigorous general-equilibrium models, eventually winning Nobel prizes for Diamond, Mortensen, and Pissarides.

### Key Concepts

#### Pigou's contribution
- A.C. Pigou (inter-war) introduced **frictional unemployment**: workers between jobs, moving to higher-productivity positions.
- Pigou himself observed jobs weren't shifting much in the 1920s — so he ultimately attributed unemployment more to "workers pricing themselves out of the market via trade unions" than to frictions.
- Search and matching unemployment is a *form* of frictional unemployment.

#### The 1970s formalisation
- **Edmund Phelps (ed., 1970)**: "Microeconomic Foundations of Employment and Inflation Theory" (Norton, NY) — first formal models using search theory to explain equilibrium unemployment.
- **Lucas & Prescott (1974)**: general equilibrium model with stochastic sectoral shocks; one-period lag in worker movement creates unemployment.

#### The 1980s — continuous-time GE models
- **Peter Diamond (1981)**: "Mobility Costs, Frictional Unemployment, and Efficiency" (*Journal of Political Economy*).
- **Christopher Pissarides (1985)**: "Short-Run Equilibrium Dynamics of Unemployment, Vacancies, and Real Wages" (*American Economic Review*).
- These integrated search into the real-business-cycle tradition.

### Definitions
- **Frictional unemployment**: unemployment of workers moving between jobs because the job-matching process takes time. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Connections
- Builds on: Pigou's classical insight
- Continues into: Howitt model in next section

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Pigou: frictional unemployment in the inter-war period.
> - Phelps (1970): first formal search models.
> - Diamond (1981) and Pissarides (1985): canonical equilibrium models.
> - Howitt (1988): basis for the model in this Unit.

## Section: A Search and Matching Model — Howitt (1988) 🔴

### Core Idea
Howitt's model embeds search frictions in a real-business-cycle framework. **Firms hire by equating the marginal cost of hiring (which depends on aggregate unemployment) to the marginal benefit (the present value of the worker's future marginal product, scaled by the firm's bargaining share).** The equilibrium produces a non-zero unemployment rate — the *natural rate* — that depends on the separation rate and the difficulty of matching.

> **In Simple Terms:** Hiring isn't free — firms must search for the right worker. They keep hiring until the cost of one more hire (which is high when unemployment is low because workers are scarce) equals the value of having that worker. The leftover unemployed workers are the natural rate.

### Key Concepts

#### 14.4.1 Model Specification (5 building blocks)

1. **Setup**: F competitive firms, N identical workers. Each period, fraction **δ** of employed workers is laid off (the **separation rate**) and joins the unemployment pool. Firms hire from this pool.

2. **Marginal cost of hiring**:
   - Increasing in the firm's own hiring rate (high hiring forces higher search intensity or worse matches).
   - **Decreasing in aggregate unemployment** (high u → cheap and easy to find willing, competent workers).

3. **Marginal value of a hired worker (q_t)**:
   - Equals expected present value of the worker's marginal product over their tenure.
   - Infinite sum, with two discount factors per term: time discounting + probability the worker has separated by time t+i.
   - Firm assumed risk-neutral.

4. **Wage bargaining**: No spot labour market — wage is set by bilateral bargaining over the surplus from the match.
   - Worker share of surplus = **ξ** (bargaining power)
   - Firm share = **(1 − ξ)**
   - Worker's outside option = 0 (no costs/benefits from unemployment).
   - **Marginal benefit to firm: λ_t = (1 − ξ) · q_t**

5. **Firm's hiring decision**: Choose hiring rate **h_t** such that marginal benefit (λ_t) = marginal cost.

#### 14.4.2 Solution
- Employment dynamics: **n_t = (1 − δ)·n_{t−1} + h_t**
- Unemployment rate: **u_t = 1 − (F·n_t)/N**
- Combining the four equations (λ_t, h_t, n_t, u_t) yields the law of motion:

**u_t = δ + {1 − δ − (F/G)·λ_t} · u_{t−1}**

where **G** is a parameter in the cost-of-hiring function — larger G means harder to locate workers.

In steady state (constant productivity, λ_t = constant·k), the **natural rate of unemployment**:

**u\* = δ / {δ + (F/G)·k}**

**Comparative statics**:
- ↑δ (higher separation rate) → ↑u*
- ↑G (harder matching) → ↑u*

#### 14.4.3 Optimality of u*
The equilibrium u* is **not socially optimal** for two opposing reasons:

| Effect | Direction | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| (1) Hiring externality | u* **too low** (over-hiring) | A firm's hiring lowers aggregate unemployment, raising the marginal cost of hiring for *other* firms — an externality the hiring firm ignores |
| (2) Bargaining wedge | u* **too high** (under-hiring) | Private benefit (1−ξ)·q_t < social benefit q_t. The wedge ξ > 0 means firms hire less than socially optimal |

The two effects pull in opposite directions, so the **net deviation** from social optimum is **ambiguous**.

#### 14.4.4 Productivity-shock dynamics
The model is in the RBC tradition. Productivity shocks drive employment and wages.

- **Adverse temporary productivity shock**:
  - ↓marginal productivity → ↓λ → ↓hiring → ↑u (immediate)
  - When shock fades: u returns to normal *slowly* via gradual hiring.
  - **State-dependent magnification**: shocks have a *bigger* effect on u when u is *high* (because hiring is cheaper there) — non-linearity is built into u*.

- **Real wage cyclicality**:
  - With ξ constant and small, real wages move **with productivity**.
  - High hiring (booms) → high real wages (pro-cyclical), but **less than employment fluctuates** if ξ is small.
  - Matches the empirical fact: real wages mildly pro-cyclical, employment strongly pro-cyclical.

### Definitions
- **Separation rate (δ)**: fraction of employed workers laid off each period. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Marginal value of a worker (q_t)**: expected present value of marginal products over the worker's tenure with the firm. ⭐
- **Worker's bargaining share (ξ)**: fraction of the match surplus going to the worker. ⭐
- **Natural rate of unemployment (u*)**: equilibrium unemployment when productivity is constant; **u* = δ / {δ + (F/G)·k}**. ⭐
- **G (matching difficulty)**: parameter in cost-of-hiring function; higher G means harder to locate workers. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Hiring decision per firm**:
1. Compute marginal value of hire: q_t = expected PV of marginal products
2. Multiply by firm's share: λ_t = (1 − ξ)·q_t
3. Compute marginal cost of hiring (rises with own hiring; falls with aggregate u)
4. Hire until MC = λ_t

**Unemployment dynamics**:
δ separates → u_t determined by previous u, separation, and aggregate hiring → u* in steady state

**Productivity-shock transmission**:
↓productivity → ↓q_t → ↓λ_t → ↓hiring → ↑u → slow recovery as λ_t recovers

### Examples
**Worked illustration of u\* sensitivity**:
- Suppose δ = 0.05, (F/G)·k = 0.95 → u* = 0.05/(0.05+0.95) = 5%
- Now ↑δ to 0.10 → u* = 0.10/(0.10+0.95) ≈ 9.5% — separation rate has powerful effect.
- Now ↑G (matching harder, so (F/G)·k = 0.45): u* = 0.05/(0.05+0.45) = 10% — matching difficulty also raises u*.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "The hiring externality means u* is too high." → ✅ Correct: The hiring externality alone makes u* too **low** (over-hiring). It's the *bargaining wedge* that pulls u* too **high**. The two effects oppose each other.
- ❌ Mistake: "Search models can only deliver voluntary unemployment." → ✅ Correct: They deliver **involuntary equilibrium unemployment** — workers willing to work at the going wage but unable to find a match.
- ❌ Mistake: "Real wages don't move at all in this model." → ✅ Correct: They **do** move pro-cyclically (with productivity), but by less than employment moves — capturing the empirical fact.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The model takes the bargaining share ξ as **exogenous** — Pissarides (1985) endogenises it (see Section 14.5).
- The non-linearity in u* means productivity shocks have asymmetric effects depending on the level of u.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **u\* = δ / {δ + (F/G)·k}**
> - ↑δ → ↑u*; ↑G → ↑u*
> - λ_t = (1 − ξ)·q_t (firm's share of the match surplus)
> - Two opposing distortions: hiring externality (u* too low) vs bargaining wedge (u* too high)
> - Productivity shocks drive employment a lot, real wages a little

## Section: Some Alternative Search Models — Pissarides (1985) and Lilien (1982) 🟡

### Core Idea
The Howitt model is one of several search models. **Pissarides (1985)** improves on Howitt by specifying the search technology and endogenising the worker's bargaining share ξ. **Lilien (1982)** introduces *sectoral shocks* — labour cannot relocate between sectors instantaneously, so a shift in demand between sectors generates aggregate unemployment even in a frictionless aggregate world.

> **In Simple Terms:** Pissarides asks "what *determines* the worker's bargaining power?" Lilien asks "what if labour can't switch sectors fast enough?" Both refine the basic search story.

### Key Concepts

#### Pissarides (1985) extensions over Howitt
1. **Specifies search technology and matching process** explicitly — Howitt just postulates them.
2. **Endogenises ξ**: the worker's bargaining share depends on the worker's outside option of turning down the match and waiting for another. Howitt took ξ as fixed.

| Feature | Howitt (1988) | Pissarides (1985) |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| Search technology | Postulated | Explicitly specified |
| Matching process | Implicit | Explicit |
| Worker share ξ | Exogenous, fixed | Endogenous, depends on outside option |
| Equilibrium unemployment | Yes | Yes |

#### Lilien (1982): Sectoral shocks model
- "Sectoral Shifts and Cyclical Unemployment" (*Journal of Political Economy*).
- **Setup**: Two-sector economy. Labour is **immobile within periods** but **fully mobile across periods**.
- Workers supply 1 unit of labour inelastically if wage > reservation wage.
- Initially labour is fully employed across both sectors.
- **Demand shifts from sector 1 to sector 2** within a period:
  - Wage rises in sector 2, falls in sector 1.
  - Labour can't shift instantly → employment in sector 2 *cannot* rise.
  - Employment in sector 1 *falls* (lower demand at fixed labour).
  - **Net effect**: aggregate unemployment **rises** in the current period.
- Next period: labour reallocates → aggregate employment returns to normal.

### Definitions
- **Sectoral shock**: a change in relative productivity or relative demand across sectors (as opposed to an aggregate shock). ⭐
- **Inter-period mobility / intra-period immobility**: Lilien's assumption — labour can move between sectors across periods but not within a period. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes
Lilien's mechanism:
1. Demand shift sector 1 → sector 2 within period
2. Wage rises in sector 2, falls in sector 1
3. Sector 2 employment cannot rise (no labour available)
4. Sector 1 employment falls (lower demand)
5. **Aggregate unemployment ↑** for the current period
6. Next period: labour reallocates → unemployment returns to normal

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "In a frictionless world, sectoral shifts cause unemployment." → ✅ Correct: In a frictionless world, the decline in one sector's employment is exactly offset by the rise in the other sector's. Sectoral-shift unemployment requires *frictions* — the inability to relocate instantly.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Pissarides extends Howitt by specifying search technology and endogenising ξ.
> - Lilien: sectoral demand shifts + intra-period labour immobility → aggregate unemployment.
> - Key insight: it's the **friction**, not the shock itself, that creates unemployment.

## Section: Significance of the Concept and Theory of Search Unemployment 🔴

### Core Idea
Search unemployment is not just a theoretical curiosity — it has **real policy significance**. Higher unemployment benefits raise the duration and incidence of search unemployment because workers wait longer for a better job. The 1990s US restructuring of unemployment benefits and the persistent EU-vs-US unemployment differential are both partly explained by this mechanism.

> **In Simple Terms:** If you're paid generously while unemployed, you can afford to be picky about which job to take. So generous benefits → more search unemployment. The US tightened benefits in the 1990s; the EU didn't, and EU unemployment stayed higher.

### Key Concepts

#### Why benefits matter
- The willingness to keep searching for a *better* job depends on income while unemployed.
- **Search unemployment**: arises when (a) someone quits to look for a better job, or (b) someone refuses an offer hoping for a better one.
- If all jobs were identical, no one would search — they'd take the first offer.
- Heterogeneity of jobs → search has option value → benefits raise that option value.

#### Higher benefits → higher search unemployment
- ↑unemployment benefits → ↑willingness to keep searching → ↑duration of unemployment.
- ↑benefits → ↑willingness to *quit* current job to search.
- Both effects raise the equilibrium unemployment rate.

#### US (1990s) vs European Union
- **1990s US**: Social security/benefits system restructured — benefits made harder to obtain.
- **Effect on US**: Lower duration of search unemployment.
- **EU**: Retained more generous benefits.
- **Result**: EU unemployment rates persistently higher than US in recent years — *partly* explained by benefits differential. (Not the whole story, but a major piece.)

### Definitions
- **Search unemployment**: unemployment that arises when (a) a worker quits to find a better job, or (b) an unemployed worker delays accepting a job in hopes of a better one. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Worker faces dispersion in job quality
2. Generous benefits raise the option value of continued search
3. Worker either quits current job or rejects current offer to keep searching
4. Aggregate search-unemployment rate rises

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Higher unemployment benefits unambiguously increase welfare." → ✅ Correct: They cushion individual hardship but raise the equilibrium unemployment rate via longer search durations. There's a real trade-off.
- ❌ Mistake: "All EU-US unemployment differential is due to benefits." → ✅ Correct: Benefits are a *partial* explanation; many other factors (labour-market regulation, hiring/firing costs, taxes) also matter.

### Examples
**Example: US benefits restructuring (1990s)**
- Before: relatively long-lasting benefits.
- Restructured: harder to obtain, shorter duration.
- Effect: workers more likely to take an available job rather than continue searching.
- Outcome: US unemployment rates fell relative to EU through the 1990s and 2000s.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Generous unemployment benefits → ↑search unemployment.
> - US 1990s reform tightened benefits → reduced search unemployment.
> - EU-US unemployment gap is partly explained by this — but only partly.

## Section: Unit 14 Summary 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 14 introduced **search and matching theory** as a Walrasian-compatible explanation for unemployment. The neoclassical model is theoretically elegant but doesn't accord with the observed reality of prolonged unemployment.
