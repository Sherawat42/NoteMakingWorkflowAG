### Nominal Rigidities versus Real Rigidities 🔴

**The crucial distinction**
| Aspect | Nominal Rigidity | Real Rigidity |
|--------|------------------|---------------|
| What's stuck? | Money prices, money wages | Real wages, relative prices |
| Where it lives | Goods markets (menu costs) | Goods, labour, credit markets |
| Why it happens | Costs of price change | Voluntary firm/worker behaviour, market imperfections |
| Example | Restaurant doesn't reprint menu | Firm pays efficiency wage above market |
- **Nominal rigidity**: nominal prices/wages do not change when conditions call for change (leads to Keynesian unemployment). ⭐
- **Real rigidity**: imperfections in goods, labour, or credit markets that prevent real wages and relative prices from adjusting to clear those markets. ⭐

**Quick Recall:**
- New Keynesians invoke **both** nominal and real rigidities.
- Real wage > market-clearing wage → unemployment of willing workers.

### Nominal Rigidities and Menu Costs 🔴

**Menu costs — the broad concept**

**Mankiw Model of Nominal Rigidities (formal logic)**

**The private vs social benefit gap**
- **Menu costs**: costs incurred by price-setting firms in changing the prices they charge — including printing/reprogramming costs and customer-irritation/lost-clientele costs. ⭐ (exam-important)
1. Money supply rises → optimal price for firm rises
2. Firm faces menu cost C of changing price
3. Firm's private profit gain from re-pricing = small (because monopolistic demand is inelastic and deviation from optimum is small)
4. If C > private gain → firm leaves price unchanged
5. All similar firms behave the same way → aggregate price level stays fixed
6. Real money supply ↑ → interest rate ↓ (or real-balance effect) → output ↑, employment ↑
7. Result: monetary expansion has *real* effects, not nominal — the Keynesian conclusion

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Menu costs are just the cost of reprinting menus." → ✅ Correct: They include all costs of changing prices — physical (reprogramming machines), and indirect (customer irritation, lost clientele).
- ❌ Mistake: "Menu costs apply equally in all market structures." → ✅ Correct: They generate rigidity *only* in monopolistic markets; perfectly competitive firms must re-price because horizontal demand makes off-optimum pricing catastrophic.
- The Mankiw model justifies rigidity *especially when* (a) elasticity of demand is **low** (high monopoly power) and (b) the deviation from the optimum price is **small**.
- Not everyone agrees menu costs alone are quantitatively large enough to explain real-world rigidity (see Romer 2001 critique in next section).

**Quick Recall:**
- Menu costs > private gain from re-pricing → price stays fixed → ↑M flows into output, not prices.
- Works *only* under monopolistic competition (downward-sloping demand).
- References: Mankiw (1985 QJE), Akerlof & Yellen (1985 QJE).
- Builds on: Imperfect competition (microeconomics)
- Contrasts with: New Classical assumption of price flexibility
- Is foundation for: Real rigidities (next section), which extend the rigidity story when menu costs alone are too small

### Real Rigidities 🔴

**Why menu costs alone aren't enough**

**The signature of real rigidity**

**Quick Recall:**
- Romer's critique: menu costs alone are too small given realistic elasticities.
- Real rigidities = built-in features of markets that make quantities flex more than prices.
- Three loci: goods market, labour market, credit market.

### Real Rigidities in the Goods Market 🔴

**Three reasons mark-ups are countercyclical**
1. **"Thick-market" effects**: In booms, higher activity reduces information acquisition/dissemination costs. Markets become more competitive → mark-ups compressed.
2. **Collusion breakdown in oligopolies**: Booms increase the temptation for individual oligopolists to defect from collusive arrangements (because there's more to gain by undercutting). This pressure pushes mark-ups down.
3. **Pro-cyclical marginal costs**: Overtime wages — which are highly pro-cyclical and expensive — push marginal costs up in booms. If prices are rigid, the gap between price and marginal cost (the mark-up) is squeezed. (Note: this argument is partly circular — it presumes price rigidity rather than explaining it.)
- **Mark-up**: the amount by which price exceeds marginal cost in imperfect competition; covers fixed costs and profits. ⭐
- **Countercyclical mark-up**: mark-up falls in booms, rises in recessions. ⭐
- **Thick-market effects**: higher economic activity in booms lowers information costs, making markets more competitive. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Pro-cyclical marginal costs *cause* price rigidity." → ✅ Correct: They presume price rigidity. The first two arguments (thick-market, collusion breakdown) are independent reasons; the marginal-cost argument is more of a description of the squeeze given that prices are already rigid.

**Quick Recall:**
- Imperfect competition → price = MC + mark-up
- Mark-up countercyclical → price stable, quantities adjust
- Three drivers: thick markets, oligopoly collusion break-up, pro-cyclical marginal costs
- Builds on: Imperfect competition framework from Section 13.4
- Contrasts with: Walrasian model where price = marginal cost

### Real Rigidities in the Credit Market 🔴

**Asymmetric information consequences**
- Microeconomic: credit rationing in equilibrium, demand for financial intermediation, justification for government intervention.
- Macroeconomic: monetary policy works mainly through (i) the credit channel, not the money supply channel; (ii) credit rationing, not interest-rate increases.

**Why banks are special**

**Magnification of shocks**
- **Asymmetric information (credit market)**: borrowers know more about their project quality and probability of success than lenders do. ⭐
- **Credit channel**: the monetary-transmission mechanism that operates through the *availability* of bank loans rather than through the interest rate. ⭐
- **Credit rationing**: lenders restrict the *quantity* of credit (not just price) at the prevailing interest rate due to information asymmetries. ⭐

**Quick Recall:**
- Credit market imperfection = information asymmetry between lenders and borrowers.
- Credit channel > money-supply channel for monetary policy transmission.
- Credit rationing > interest-rate hikes for restrictive policy.
- Magnification: real shocks become bigger via credit distortions.
- Parallels: Goods-market real rigidity (imperfections produce price rigidity; credit imperfections produce loan-quantity rigidity)

**Quick Recall:**
- Real rigidity in labour = real wage stuck above market-clearing wage.
- Reason: firms voluntarily overpay to raise efficiency / prevent shirking.
- Effect: involuntary unemployment + small real-wage cyclicality.

### Efficiency-Wage Theories 🔴

**Four reasons firms pay above-market wages**
1. **Higher consumption → higher productivity (nutrition argument)**: Especially in low-income economies, higher wages let workers eat better, stay healthier, and work harder.
2. **Selection on reservation wage**: A higher wage attracts workers with higher reservation wages, who tend to have higher (unobservable) abilities — so the firm gets a better-quality applicant pool.
3. **Loyalty / morale / fair-wage**: A premium wage builds loyalty and effort. The flip side: under-paying produces anger, sabotage, revenge.
4. **Anti-shirking (Shapiro–Stiglitz logic)**: When effort cannot be monitored perfectly, paying above-market wages gives workers something to lose if caught shirking — the alternative job pays only the lower market-clearing wage.
- **Efficiency wage**: a real wage paid above market-clearing because higher wages raise effort/efficiency by more than the wage cost. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Reservation wage**: the minimum wage at which a worker is willing to supply labour. ⭐
- **Non-Walrasian theory**: any theory in which markets do not clear; here, the labour market with persistent unemployment. ⭐

**Quick Recall:**
- Efficiency wage = chosen, voluntary, above-market real wage.
- Four motives: nutrition, selection, loyalty, anti-shirking.
- Result: persistent involuntary unemployment.
- Builds on: Real wage rigidity (Section 13.5.3, Chunk 001)
- Is foundation for: Efficiency Wage Model (next section)

### Efficiency Wage Model — An Example (Romer 2001) 🔴

**13.8.1 Specification**
- M identical firms, N identical workers.
- Single-input Neo-Classical production function: **Y = F(e·L)**, where L = physical workers hired, e = effort per worker, e·L = effective (efficiency) labour.
- Effort function: **e = e(w)** — effort is an increasing function of the real wage.
- F'(·) > 0, F''(·) < 0 (positive but diminishing marginal product of efficiency labour).
- Each of N workers supplies one unit of labour inelastically — higher wage raises effort, *not* hours.

**13.8.2 Solution**
- For w below optimum: ↑w yields more-than-proportional ↑e (still gaining)
- For w above optimum: ↑w yields less-than-proportional ↑e (now losing)
- At optimum: marginal product of effective labour = its cost

**13.8.3 Implications**

**13.8.4 Extensions of the model**
- w' = real wage available in alternative firms
- u = unemployment rate
- e is increasing in w, increasing in u, decreasing in w'
- **Effort function e(w)**: per-worker effort as an increasing function of the real wage. ⭐
- **Solow condition / first-order condition**: w·e'(w)/e(w) = 1 — elasticity of effort with respect to wage equals one at the optimum. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Per-unit cost of effective labour**: w/e(w); the firm chooses w to minimise this. ⭐
- **Extended effort function**: e(w, w', u) — effort depends on own wage, alternative wage, and unemployment rate. ⭐
1. Firm specifies effort function e(w)
2. Maximises π = F(e(w)·L) − w·L over (w, L)
3. FOC w.r.t. L gives w·e'(w)/e(w) = 1
4. This determines w* independently of demand
5. L* determined by F'(e·L) = w/e (marginal product of effective labour = its cost)
6. Aggregate demand for labour = M·L*
7. If N > M·L* → involuntary unemployment at w*
- Suppose at w = ₹100, e(w) = 50, e'(w) = 0.5. Elasticity = 100·0.5/50 = 1. ✓ At optimum.
- Cost per efficiency unit = w/e(w) = 100/50 = 2.
- If w were ₹80 with e = 30, e' = 0.5: elasticity = 80·0.5/30 = 1.33 > 1. Below optimum — raising w further yields more effort gain than cost.
- If w were ₹120 with e = 60, e' = 0.4: elasticity = 120·0.4/60 = 0.8 < 1. Above optimum — raising w further wastes money.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "The efficiency wage equals the marginal product of labour." → ✅ Correct: It is set by minimising w/e(w); the FOC is about the elasticity of effort, not the value of MPL.
- ❌ Mistake: "Higher demand for output raises efficiency wage." → ✅ Correct: w* depends only on the effort function — demand changes affect L*, not w*.
- ❌ Mistake: "The simple model captures both short and long run." → ✅ Correct: It uniformly predicts employment effects only; the extended model with e(w, w', u) is needed for long-run real-wage trend.
- If M·L* > N at w*, the wage is bid above w* and unemployment vanishes — the model only explains unemployment in the "excess supply" case.
- The simple model is silent on the long-run secular rise in real wages. The extension repairs this.

**Quick Recall:**
- Production: Y = F(e·L)
- Effort: e = e(w), e increasing in w
- FOC: **w·e'(w)/e(w) = 1** (elasticity of effort w.r.t. wage = 1)
- Optimum: minimises w/e(w) (cost per efficiency unit)
- Extension: e = e(w, w', u) — needed for long-run dynamics
- Three implications: unemployment persists, no real-wage response to demand, price rigidity implied
- Builds on: Efficiency-Wage Theories (previous section)
- Reinforces: Price rigidity from Mankiw model (Chunk 001) — efficiency wages keep marginal costs constant, so prices stay constant
- Contrasts with: Walrasian labour market (always clears via wage adjustment)

### Contracting and Insider-Outsider Models 🔴

**Why long-term relationships matter**
- Many jobs require **firm-specific skills** worth less outside the firm.
- Training new workers each period is **costly**.
- Workers stay if their *long-term* expected earnings exceed outside opportunities, even if current earnings are low.
- US average tenure ≈ 10 years.
- Hence wages don't adjust period-to-period to clear the labour market — labour market is **non-Walrasian**.

**Two types of contracts**
| Aspect | Fixed-wage contract | Implicit contract |
|--------|---------------------|-------------------|
| What's specified | Pre-determined wage; firm chooses employment | Wage AND employment for each state of economy |
| Stated explicitly? | Yes | No (implicit / understood) |
| Pareto efficient? | ❌ No — MPL ≠ marginal disutility | ✅ Yes |
| Wage rigidity implied? | Yes | Yes |
| Unemployment implied? | Yes | Yes |

**Insider-Outsider Models**
- **Firms**
- **Insiders**: currently-employed workers (have bargaining power via unions)
- **Outsiders**: unemployed workers (no bargaining power)
- **Fixed-wage contract**: pre-specified wage; firm chooses employment freely; **inefficient** (MPL ≠ marginal disutility). ⭐
- **Implicit contract**: contract between firm and workers specifying both wage and employment for each state of the economy; not explicitly written but behavioural; **Pareto efficient**. ⭐
- **Pareto efficiency**: a contract is Pareto efficient if it is impossible to make one party better off without making the other worse off. ⭐
- **Insiders**: currently-employed workers, with bargaining power. ⭐
- **Outsiders**: unemployed workers, without bargaining power. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Implicit contracts are inefficient because they're vague." → ✅ Correct: They are *Pareto efficient*; the term "implicit" refers to their unwritten nature, not their efficiency.
- ❌ Mistake: "Fixed-wage contracts give the firm bad outcomes." → ✅ Correct: They are inefficient *for both parties together* — there's a different contract that would make both better off. The inefficiency is joint, not unilateral.
- ❌ Mistake: "Insider-outsider models are about wage discrimination." → ✅ Correct: They're about who has bargaining power — the *unemployed* would lobby for lower wages and more jobs, but they have no voice at the bargaining table.
- Contracting models depart from efficiency-wage models: efficiency-wage rigidity comes from worker effort; contract rigidity comes from long-term relationships. Both produce real wage rigidity.

**Quick Recall:**
- Fixed-wage contract = inefficient (MPL ≠ marginal disutility).
- Implicit contract = efficient, but *also* implies real wage rigidity.
- Insider-outsider: insiders + firms bargain; outsiders excluded → wages too high, unemployment persists.
- Parallels: Efficiency wage model — both produce non-Walrasian labour market with real-wage rigidity, but for different reasons (effort vs. long-term relations).

**Quick Recall:**
- Two pillars of New Keynesian unemployment: nominal rigidity (Mankiw / menu costs) + real rigidity (goods / credit / labour).
- Three labour-market mechanisms: efficiency wages, contracts, insider-outsider.
| Theory | Type of unemployment | Microfoundations | Walrasian? |
|--------|---------------------|------------------|------------|
| Classical / Neoclassical | Voluntary only | Strong | Yes |
| Keynesian | Involuntary | Weak | No (rigidity) |
| New Keynesian | Involuntary | Strong | No (imperfect competition) |
| **Search & Matching** | **Involuntary, equilibrium** | **Strong** | **Yes** |

**Quick Recall:**
- Search theory = unemployment in equilibrium *within* the Walrasian framework.
- The friction is heterogeneous labour + costly matching, not market imperfection.

### Search Theory and Theories of Unemployment — A Four-Way Classification 🔴

**The four responses**
| Response | School | Mechanism | Type of unemployment |
|----------|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| (i) "Yes, you're hired at the lower wage" | Neoclassical / Walrasian | Wages clear the market | Voluntary only |
| (ii) "You're not the same as my workers — labour is heterogeneous" | **Search & Matching** | Heterogeneity + matching costs | Equilibrium frictional |
| (iii) "I'd like to but I'm bound by collective agreement" | Contracting models | Institutional wage-setting | Wage rigidity from contracts |
| (iv) "I won't lower wages — high wages give me efficiency" | Efficiency-Wage theories | Higher wage → higher productivity | Voluntary high wage |
- **Walrasian labour market**: a perfectly competitive labour market where wages adjust until demand equals supply; involuntary unemployment is impossible. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Voluntary unemployment**: unemployment of those unwilling to work at the going wage rate. ⭐
- **Involuntary unemployment**: unemployment of those willing to work at the going wage rate but unable to find work. ⭐
- **Search and matching model**: model in which heterogeneous workers and heterogeneous jobs are paired through a costly search-and-match process, generating equilibrium unemployment. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Search theory is a kind of New Keynesian theory." → ✅ Correct: Search theory is **Walrasian** — it doesn't require imperfect competition or wage rigidity. It belongs to a different lineage.
- ❌ Mistake: "All four responses produce the same unemployment." → ✅ Correct: Only the Neoclassical response (i) is consistent with voluntary-only unemployment. Responses (ii)-(iv) all generate involuntary unemployment, but for different reasons.

**Quick Recall:**
- Four responses → four theories.
- (i) Neoclassical = voluntary only.
- (ii) Search = heterogeneity + matching costs.
- (iii) Contracting = institutional rigidity (Unit 13).
- (iv) Efficiency wage = chosen-high-wage rigidity (Unit 13).
- Builds on: Walrasian general equilibrium framework
- Cross-references: Contracting and Efficiency-Wage theories (Chunk 002)
- **Frictional unemployment**: unemployment of workers moving between jobs because the job-matching process takes time. ⭐ (exam-important)

**Quick Recall:**
- Pigou: frictional unemployment in the inter-war period.
- Phelps (1970): first formal search models.
- Diamond (1981) and Pissarides (1985): canonical equilibrium models.
- Howitt (1988): basis for the model in this Unit.

### A Search and Matching Model — Howitt (1988) 🔴

**14.4.1 Model Specification (5 building blocks)**
1. **Setup**: F competitive firms, N identical workers. Each period, fraction **δ** of employed workers is laid off (the **separation rate**) and joins the unemployment pool. Firms hire from this pool.
2. **Marginal cost of hiring**:
   - Increasing in the firm's own hiring rate (high hiring forces higher search intensity or worse matches).
   - **Decreasing in aggregate unemployment** (high u → cheap and easy to find willing, competent workers).
3. **Marginal value of a hired worker (q_t)**:
   - Equals expected present value of the worker's marginal product over their tenure.
   - Infinite sum, with two discount factors per term: time discounting + probability the worker has separated by time t+i.
   - Firm assumed risk-neutral.
4. **Wage bargaining**: No spot labour market — wage is set by bilateral bargaining over the surplus from the match.
   - Worker share of surplus = **ξ** (bargaining power)
   - Firm share = **(1 − ξ)**
   - Worker's outside option = 0 (no costs/benefits from unemployment).
   - **Marginal benefit to firm: λ_t = (1 − ξ) · q_t**
5. **Firm's hiring decision**: Choose hiring rate **h_t** such that marginal benefit (λ_t) = marginal cost.

**14.4.2 Solution**
- Employment dynamics: **n_t = (1 − δ)·n_{t−1} + h_t**
- Unemployment rate: **u_t = 1 − (F·n_t)/N**
- Combining the four equations (λ_t, h_t, n_t, u_t) yields the law of motion:
- ↑δ (higher separation rate) → ↑u*
- ↑G (harder matching) → ↑u*

**14.4.3 Optimality of u***
| Effect | Direction | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| (1) Hiring externality | u* **too low** (over-hiring) | A firm's hiring lowers aggregate unemployment, raising the marginal cost of hiring for *other* firms — an externality the hiring firm ignores |
| (2) Bargaining wedge | u* **too high** (under-hiring) | Private benefit (1−ξ)·q_t < social benefit q_t. The wedge ξ > 0 means firms hire less than socially optimal |

**14.4.4 Productivity-shock dynamics**
- **Adverse temporary productivity shock**:
  - ↓marginal productivity → ↓λ → ↓hiring → ↑u (immediate)
  - When shock fades: u returns to normal *slowly* via gradual hiring.
  - **State-dependent magnification**: shocks have a *bigger* effect on u when u is *high* (because hiring is cheaper there) — non-linearity is built into u*.
- **Real wage cyclicality**:
  - With ξ constant and small, real wages move **with productivity**.
  - High hiring (booms) → high real wages (pro-cyclical), but **less than employment fluctuates** if ξ is small.
  - Matches the empirical fact: real wages mildly pro-cyclical, employment strongly pro-cyclical.
- **Separation rate (δ)**: fraction of employed workers laid off each period. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Marginal value of a worker (q_t)**: expected present value of marginal products over the worker's tenure with the firm. ⭐
- **Worker's bargaining share (ξ)**: fraction of the match surplus going to the worker. ⭐
- **Natural rate of unemployment (u*)**: equilibrium unemployment when productivity is constant; **u* = δ / {δ + (F/G)·k}**. ⭐
- **G (matching difficulty)**: parameter in cost-of-hiring function; higher G means harder to locate workers. ⭐
1. Compute marginal value of hire: q_t = expected PV of marginal products
2. Multiply by firm's share: λ_t = (1 − ξ)·q_t
3. Compute marginal cost of hiring (rises with own hiring; falls with aggregate u)
4. Hire until MC = λ_t
- Suppose δ = 0.05, (F/G)·k = 0.95 → u* = 0.05/(0.05+0.95) = 5%
- Now ↑δ to 0.10 → u* = 0.10/(0.10+0.95) ≈ 9.5% — separation rate has powerful effect.
- Now ↑G (matching harder, so (F/G)·k = 0.45): u* = 0.05/(0.05+0.45) = 10% — matching difficulty also raises u*.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "The hiring externality means u* is too high." → ✅ Correct: The hiring externality alone makes u* too **low** (over-hiring). It's the *bargaining wedge* that pulls u* too **high**. The two effects oppose each other.
- ❌ Mistake: "Search models can only deliver voluntary unemployment." → ✅ Correct: They deliver **involuntary equilibrium unemployment** — workers willing to work at the going wage but unable to find a match.
- ❌ Mistake: "Real wages don't move at all in this model." → ✅ Correct: They **do** move pro-cyclically (with productivity), but by less than employment moves — capturing the empirical fact.
- The model takes the bargaining share ξ as **exogenous** — Pissarides (1985) endogenises it (see Section 14.5).
- The non-linearity in u* means productivity shocks have asymmetric effects depending on the level of u.

**Quick Recall:**
- **u\* = δ / {δ + (F/G)·k}**
- ↑δ → ↑u*; ↑G → ↑u*
- λ_t = (1 − ξ)·q_t (firm's share of the match surplus)
- Two opposing distortions: hiring externality (u* too low) vs bargaining wedge (u* too high)
- Productivity shocks drive employment a lot, real wages a little
- Builds on: Real Business Cycle theory (Unit 12)
- Continues into: Alternative search models (next section)

|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| Search technology | Postulated | Explicitly specified |
| Matching process | Implicit | Explicit |
| Worker share ξ | Exogenous, fixed | Endogenous, depends on outside option |

- **Sectoral shock**: a change in relative productivity or relative demand across sectors (as opposed to an aggregate shock). ⭐
- **Inter-period mobility / intra-period immobility**: Lilien's assumption — labour can move between sectors across periods but not within a period. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "In a frictionless world, sectoral shifts cause unemployment." → ✅ Correct: In a frictionless world, the decline in one sector's employment is exactly offset by the rise in the other sector's. Sectoral-shift unemployment requires *frictions* — the inability to relocate instantly.

**Quick Recall:**
- Pissarides extends Howitt by specifying search technology and endogenising ξ.
- Lilien: sectoral demand shifts + intra-period labour immobility → aggregate unemployment.
- Key insight: it's the **friction**, not the shock itself, that creates unemployment.

### Significance of the Concept and Theory of Search Unemployment 🔴

**Why benefits matter**
- The willingness to keep searching for a *better* job depends on income while unemployed.
- **Search unemployment**: arises when (a) someone quits to look for a better job, or (b) someone refuses an offer hoping for a better one.
- If all jobs were identical, no one would search — they'd take the first offer.
- Heterogeneity of jobs → search has option value → benefits raise that option value.

**Higher benefits → higher search unemployment**
- ↑unemployment benefits → ↑willingness to keep searching → ↑duration of unemployment.
- ↑benefits → ↑willingness to *quit* current job to search.
- Both effects raise the equilibrium unemployment rate.

**US (1990s) vs European Union**
- **1990s US**: Social security/benefits system restructured — benefits made harder to obtain.
- **Effect on US**: Lower duration of search unemployment.
- **EU**: Retained more generous benefits.
- **Result**: EU unemployment rates persistently higher than US in recent years — *partly* explained by benefits differential. (Not the whole story, but a major piece.)
- **Search unemployment**: unemployment that arises when (a) a worker quits to find a better job, or (b) an unemployed worker delays accepting a job in hopes of a better one. ⭐ (exam-important)
1. Worker faces dispersion in job quality
2. Generous benefits raise the option value of continued search
3. Worker either quits current job or rejects current offer to keep searching
4. Aggregate search-unemployment rate rises

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Higher unemployment benefits unambiguously increase welfare." → ✅ Correct: They cushion individual hardship but raise the equilibrium unemployment rate via longer search durations. There's a real trade-off.
- ❌ Mistake: "All EU-US unemployment differential is due to benefits." → ✅ Correct: Benefits are a *partial* explanation; many other factors (labour-market regulation, hiring/firing costs, taxes) also matter.
- Before: relatively long-lasting benefits.
- Restructured: harder to obtain, shorter duration.
- Effect: workers more likely to take an available job rather than continue searching.
- Outcome: US unemployment rates fell relative to EU through the 1990s and 2000s.

**Quick Recall:**

**Quick Recall:**
- Search theory = equilibrium frictional unemployment in a Walrasian world.
- Heterogeneity + matching costs do the work that price rigidity does in New Keynesian theory.
- Equilibrium ≠ socially optimal: opposite-sign distortions from externalities and bargaining.
- Sectoral shocks + intra-period labour immobility → unemployment (Lilien).
- Higher unemployment benefits → more search unemployment.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Search theory and New Keynesian theory contradict each other." → ✅ Correct: They explain *different aspects* of unemployment. Real economies likely contain both — rigidity *and* matching frictions.

**Quick Recall:**
- Two routes to involuntary unemployment: rigidity (New Keynesian) and frictions (search).
- Both microfounded; both empirically reasonable.
- The difference is whether the labour market is Walrasian — a deep methodological choice.
