11.4.3 Political Business Cycles 
The term political business cycle is used mainly to describe the stimulation of the 
economy just prior to an election in order to improve prospects of the incumbent 
government getting re-elected. Expansionary monetary and fiscal policies have 
politically popular consequences in the short run, such as falling 
unemployment, economic growth, and benefits from government spending on 
public services. However, the same policies, especially if pursued to excess, are 
found to have unpleasant consequences in the long term, such as accelerating 
inflation and damaging the foreign trade balance. Thus, they can harm the long-
term growth potential of the economy. Governments prefer to pursue popular 
expansionary monetary and fiscal policies for a short horizon immediately before 
an election. However, being aware of its adverse effects, they do not continue 
with those measures after they get elected. Thus, after the election is over, 
government often reverses earlier decisions, which may include cutting spending, 
slowing the growth of money supply, and allowing interest rates to rise. As a 
result, the regular holding of elections will produce cyclical fluctuation of 
economic activity because of recurring patterns of government stimulus and 
restraint in order to induce an artificial boom in the election time.

US, the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) has a dedicated research 
program for identifying the dates of business cycle turning points. Similarly the 
Centre for Economic Policy Research (CEPR) Euro Area Business Cycle Dating 
Committee establishes the chronology of recessions and expansions of Euro Area 
member countries. In India there have been some attempts to determine the 
chronology of business cycles. 
The NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee maintains a chronology of the 
US business cycle. The chronology comprises alternating dates of peaks and 
troughs in economic activity. A recession is a period between a peak and a 
trough, and an expansion is a period between a trough and a peak. The NBER 
does not define a recession in terms of two consecutive quarters of decline in real 
GDP. Rather, a recession is a significant decline in economic activity spread 
across the economy, lasting more than a few months, normally visible in real 
GDP, real income, employment, industrial production, and wholesale-retail sales. 
Similarly, during an expansion, economic activity rises substantially, spreads 
across the economy, and usually lasts for several years. Thus, the NBER 
approach identified cycles as recurrent sequences of alternating phases of 
expansion and contraction in the levels of a large number of economic time 
series. This working definition, in substantially its present form, has been in use 
at the NBER for over fifty years and is currently employed by the NBER to 
identify and date the US business cycle. These dates are widely accepted by 
government, academic, and business analysts. The definition served as the basis 
for the NBER's pre-World War II business cycle chronologies for England, 
France, and Germany as well as the US.  
In both recessions and expansions, brief reversals in economic activity may 
occur-a recession may include a short period of expansion followed by further 
decline; an expansion may include a short period of contraction followed by 
further growth. The Committee applies its judgment based on the above 
definitions of recessions and expansions and has no fixed rule to determine these 
upturns and downturns. 
The Committee does not have a fixed definition of economic activity. It examines 
and compares the behaviour of various measures of broad activity: real GDP 
measured on the product and income sides, economy-wide employment, and real 
income. The Committee also may consider indicators that do not cover the entire 
economy, such as real sales and the Federal Reserve's index of industrial 
production (IIP).

The objectives of macroeconomic policy have long included the avoidance of 
protracted recessions in which resources go underutilized, and of periods of 
unsustainable growth that jeopardize reasonable price stability. An important part 
of the job of the central bank is therefore to gather information on the current 
state of the economy and if possible, forecast future economic conditions. The

such as GDP, sales, investment, stock prices, is rather simple though its practical 
application is difficult. Usually, the time pattern of these fluctuating economic 
series is diverse. While some economic series are expanding at a given point in 
time, others have already reached their upper turning point and still others are on 
the downswing; a few economic activities might even be at a lower turning point. 
Thus the issue arises is how to measure the aggregate state of the economy using 
these various individual economic series as they have diverse timing patterns.  
Economic indicators were conceived at the NBER originally by Mitchell and 
Burns. This approach requires monitoring of economic variables that tend to be 
sensitive to cyclical changes no matter what their cause. Burn and Mitchell 
studied a group of about 487 economic variables to see if turning points in the 
variables persistently led, coincided with, or lagged turning points in the US 
business cycle. Seventy one series were chosen and ordered according to the 
average lead or lag with regard to the reference revivals. For example, six time 
series had no average lead or lag. On the average, the leading series were from 
one to ten months ahead of the reference revivals. The lagging series were on the 
average from one to twelve months behind. The idea of leading, lagging, and 
coinciding (reference) series was developed even further by G.H. Moore in 1950. 
Out of 801 series, he chose 21 series which passed a test for conformity and 
timing; conformity meaning the consistency with which the series have 
conformed to business cycles and timing referring to the consistency with which 
a time series' turning point has led, lagged or roughly coincided with the 
reference data. Those series were selected which were similar in timing at peaks 
and troughs and were classified into three groups: leading, roughly coincident, 
and lagging. 
11.6.1 Leading Indicators 
Leading economic indicators help us in assessing where the economy is heading. 
They indicate what is coming, such as moving from a peak phase into a 
contraction, before it actually happens. 
One of the most significant leading indicators is the stock market itself, gauged 
by an index such as the S&P 500. It will begin to rise before economic conditions 
seems favourable, and it will be begin to decline before economic conditions 
seem to warrant it. Another important leading indicator is interest rates. Low 
interests stimulate borrowing and buying, which favours the economy. An 
increase in interest rates shows the economy is doing well, but eventually rising 
interest rates lead to a slow down because less people borrow money to start new 
projects.

Unlike leading indicators, lagging indicators shift after the economy changes. 
Although they do not typically tell us where the economy is headed, they indicate 
how the economy changes over time and can help to identify long-term trends. 
Lagging economic indicators reveal past information about the economy. 
The GDP is how much a country is producing. There is significant lag time 
between when the data is compiled and when it is released, yet it is still an 
important indicator. Many consider a recession to be underway if two quarters 
see back-to-back declining GDP. Other indicators, such as the Consumer Price 
Index (CPI), are also sometimes considered lagging indicators, since they reveal 
information that is already known to most consumers. 
11.6.3 Coincident Indicators 
Coincident indicators (more or less) in parallel with the general economic 
conditions and therefore reflect the current status of the economy. They give 
consumers, business leaders, and policy makers an idea about where 
the economy is currently, right now. When the economy rises today, then 
coincident indicators are also rising right now. And when the economy declines 
today, then coincident indicators are also declining right now. Typical examples 
of coincident indicators are industrial production or turnover.

Business Cycles 11.7 EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS 
Researchers have faced a many of challenges while studying business cycles. 
One of the major challenges is the problem of representing complex features of 
economic data. Kuznets, Mitchell, Burns and many others employed various 
techniques (i.e., moving averages, piecewise trends, etc.) to study the fluctuations 
of variables around the long term path. As you know, there are various 
techniques of data analysis. However, there is no single correct way to represent 
these components.  
During the nineteenth century researchers focused on real variables such as 
agricultural output to explain business cycle. The classical economists assumed 
perfectly competitive markets and flexibility in prices and wage rate; thus ruled 
out the possibility of business cycle. According to them, money was neutral in its 
effects. Later on, during the twentieth century, economists ascribed monetary 
variables such as money supply, interest rates and prices important variables 
causing business cycles.  
D. K. Backus and P. J. Kehoe in their study titled, “International Evidence on the 
Historical Properties of Business Cycles” in 1922 took a group of macro 
variables, in addition to the GDP, for analysis of business cycle. They examined 
the cyclical behaviour of private consumption expenditure, fixed investment, 
government purchases and net exports in ten countries, namely, Australia, 
Canada, Denmark, Ger many, Italy, Japan, Norway, Sweden, the United 
Kingdom, and the US. They find a great deal of regularity in the cyclical 
behaviour of real quantities. The patterns of variation in all the variables, 
however, are not similar. The magnitude of output fluctuations varies across 
countries and over time. Further, the relationships among macroeconomic 
variables are remarkably stable. For example, investment is about 2 to 4 times 
more volatile than output while the variation in consumption is almost the same 
as the variation in output. Both investment and consumption are strongly pro-
cyclical. Trade balance is generally countercyclical, exhibiting larger deficits 
during booms than during recessions. The exception to this regularity in 
quantities is government purchases, which exhibit no systematic cyclical 
tendency. 
I. Konstantakopoulou and E. G. Tsionas in their work on “Half a century of 
empirical evidence of Business Cycles in OECD countries” in 2014 examine the 
existence of common business cycles in OECD countries using H-P filter over 
the period from 1960 to 2010. The results suggests that there are two distinct 
cycles in the OECD countries: the Euro area cycle, which includes the business 
cycles of Germany, France, Italy, Netherlands, Australia and Belgium and the 
world cycle, which consist of the business cycles of the US, Canada and the U.K. 
For a long time, the prevailing view was that we need different theories to 
explain the different features of the observed data – growth theory to explain the 
long run trend in a series and something else to explain the cyclical movements

11.8 LET US SUM UP 
In this unit we focused on three issues, viz., basic features of business cycles , 
traditional theories of business cycles and measurement of business cycles. 
Business cycle should be thought of as apparent deviations from a trend. Certain 
economic variables move together with aggregate output. An examination of the 
time path of output for any modern industrialized economy reveals that output 
tends to fluctuate about a long term growth path. These fluctuations about trend

are what we most often think of as the business cycle. The fluctuations are 
typically irregularly spaced and of varying amplitude and duration. Nevertheless, 
the one very regular feature of these fluctuations is the way variables move 
together. Business cycle is characterised by four phases, viz., expansion, 
recession, depression and recovery. Some of the important mathematical theories 
which explain these feature of business cycles are: a) Keynesian theory which 
showed that changes in the level of aggregate effective demand bring about 
fluctuations in the level of income, output and employment; b) Samuelson model 
of business cycle which convincingly proved that it is the interaction between the 
multiplier and accelerator that gives rise to cyclical fluctuations in economic 
activity, and c) Political theory of business cycle which suggests that business 
cycles are an outcome of the periodicity of elections in a democracy that prompts 
the governments to increase public investment before elections and cut down on 
it afterwards. NBER uses the working definition of business cycle formulated by 
Burns and Mitchell in the 1940s to identify and date the US business cycle. These 
dates are widely accepted by government, academic and business analysts. The 
main problem encountered in the research of business cycle is the lack of a single 
sufficiently long and consistent measure of aggregate economic activity. The 
NBER, therefore, relies on the evidence from a large number of comprehensive 
indicators rather than any single one to date a business cycle. The variables 
include quantities and prices, stocks and flows, outputs and inputs, real monetary 
and financial variables. Timing and amplitude of these variables are used to 
group them into leading, lagging and coincident indicators. 
To conclude, a business cycle is not just a movement along a curve in a textbook. 
It implies new jobs for people, or the loss of them. It is new income, or the loss of 
it. It is the funds to build new infrastructure such as schools, hospitals and roads. 
The story of the business cycle is the story of progress and plenty of failure and 
sacrifice. The effects of recessions extend beyond the purely economic realm and 
influence the social fabric of society as well. Misery of households, poverty, 
unemployment, property related crimes, etc. tend to rise during recessions.
