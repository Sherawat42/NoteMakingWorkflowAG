## Section: Introduction to Business Cycles 🟢

### Core Idea
Economic growth in a country is not smooth — GDP and other economic aggregates (income, employment, prices) move through periodical ups and downs around their long-term trend. These alternating phases of upswings and downswings are called **business cycles**. Formal study began in the early 20th century with Wesley Mitchell, Simon Kuznets, and Frederick Mills.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine a long road that gradually climbs uphill — the economy walks along it but keeps swaying side to side as it goes. Those sideways swings around the upward path are business cycles.

### Key Concepts

#### Historical context of business cycle research
Mitchell documented co-movement of variables across cycles; Mills documented co-movement of prices and quantities over expansions and contractions; Kuznets studied the patterns of both growth and fluctuations. The 1930s was a very active period for business cycle research. Interest declined after Keynes' *General Theory* (which redirected attention to short-run macroeconomic management), and revived in the 1970s when the prevailing economic crisis could not be explained by the Keynesian model.

### Definitions
- **Business cycle**: Recurrent alternating phases of expansion and contraction in a number of economic variables including employment, production, real income, and real sales. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: Features of Business Cycles 🔴
<!-- Reason: Check Your Progress Q2 explicitly asks "List the important features of a business cycle"; unit objective "explain features" -->

### Core Idea
Business cycles are recurrent, multidimensional, and asymmetric fluctuations in which quantities, prices, stocks, flows, real, monetary and financial variables tend to move together. Expansions typically exceed contractions in size and duration, and cycles are larger, longer, and more widely diffused than other fluctuations.

> **In Simple Terms:** A business cycle isn't just GDP swinging — it's the whole economy (jobs, prices, profits, inventories) breathing in and out together, with the breathe-in (expansion) usually deeper and longer than the breathe-out (contraction).

### Key Concepts

#### The eight major features

1. **Distinct phases, irregular regularity** — Cycles do not show the same regularity but always have four phases: expansion, recession, depression, recovery. Duration ranges from 2 to 12 years.

2. **Synchronic** — Depression/contraction occurs simultaneously across most industries. Recession spreads industry-by-industry through a chain reaction; prosperity spreads through input-output linkages and demand relations.

3. **Co-movement of aggregates** — Output, employment, investment, consumption, interest rates, and price level all fluctuate together.

4. **Durable goods and investment swing the most** — Investment is especially unstable because, as Keynes stressed, it depends on private entrepreneurs' profit expectations. Amplitude of fluctuation in durable household goods is *higher* than that of GDP.

5. **Non-durables and services are stable** — Households maintain great stability in consumption of non-durables. Their amplitude of fluctuation is *lower* than that of GDP.

6. **Inventory effect is immediate** — In recession, inventories accumulate beyond desired level → production cuts. In recovery, demand picks up, inventories fall below desired level → more orders → production and investment rise.

7. **Profits fluctuate the most among incomes** — Cycles create forecasting uncertainty for businessmen; in depression profits turn negative and bankruptcies rise.

8. **International in character** — Once started in one country, cycles spread via *contagion effect*: financial markets are linked through capital flows; a recession in (say) the US reduces US imports, hurting major US-exporting countries.

| Variable type | Amplitude vs GDP | Why |
|---------------|------------------|-----|
| Investment, consumer durables | Higher | Sensitive to profit/income expectations; postponable |
| Non-durables and services | Lower | Households smooth consumption of necessities |
| Profits | Highest among incomes | Residual claimants absorb cyclical shocks |

### Definitions
- **Synchronic cycles**: Cycles in which contraction (or expansion) occurs simultaneously across most industries / sectors. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Contagion effect**: Mechanism by which a cycle spreads from one country to others through financial-market linkages and trade (export–import) channels.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: All categories of consumption fluctuate equally with GDP. → ✅ Correct: Non-durables and services are *more stable* than GDP; durables and investment are *more volatile* than GDP.
- ❌ Mistake: Business cycles repeat with fixed periodicity. → ✅ Correct: They have distinct phases but no fixed duration — anywhere from 2 to 12 years.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 phases: expansion → recession → depression → recovery
> - Duration: 2–12 years
> - Investment amplitude > GDP amplitude > non-durables amplitude
> - Synchronic across sectors; international via contagion
> - Profits fluctuate most; non-durables fluctuate least

## Section: Phases of a Business Cycle 🔴
<!-- Reason: Check Your Progress Q3 explicitly asks "Describe the different phases"; unit objective "identify the various phases" -->

### Core Idea
A business cycle has four phases — **expansion, recession, depression, and recovery** — measured against a long-run upward-sloping steady-state growth path. The two turning points are the **peak** (end of expansion / start of recession) and the **trough** (end of depression / start of recovery).

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of a roller coaster on an upward-tilting track. The high point is the *peak* (recession starts here); the low point is the *trough* (recovery starts here). Climb up = expansion, fall down = recession deepening into depression.

### Key Concepts

#### Expansion phase
Production, employment, output, wages, profits, demand, supply, and sales all rise. Begins from forces such as banks' willingness to lend and businesses' willingness to borrow. Optimism prevails. Eventually the economy *overheats*: wages and prices rise faster than output → production cost ↑ → profits ↓. The central bank pursues restrictive monetary policy to curb inflation. Growth slows and reaches the **peak**.

#### Peak
Variables (production, profit, sales, employment) are high but no longer accelerating. Demand for inputs gradually decreases due to high input prices → product prices rise → real income lags → consumers cut back, especially on luxuries and durables. When the demand decline becomes rapid and steady, recession begins.

#### Recession phase
All variables (production, prices, saving, investment) start decreasing. Initially producers do not see the demand fall → keep producing → supply exceeds demand → inventories accumulate. Producers then realise rising costs and falling profits → cut new investment → demand for factors falls → input prices fall → unemployment rises. A chain reaction sets in: lower income → lower demand → lower output → lower employment → lower income… Social effects also appear: unrest and crime rise.

During recession, actual output may fall below potential output (*positive output gap*). If recession deepens, growth becomes negative (absolute GDP declines). Firms struggle to repay debt, demand for credit falls, banks tighten lending due to default risk.

#### Depression phase
Economic growth is below the long-run trend; can even be negative. (E.g., in 2019, Venezuela, Argentina, and Turkey had negative growth.) The difference between recession and depression is one of *degree and extent*.

#### Recovery phase
Optimism rebuilds in some sectors → reversal of recession. Consumer spending and demand for consumer goods rise → firms increase production and undertake new investments. Replacement and maintenance of obsolete machinery also drives investment. **Price mechanism is central**: during recession, input prices fell more than product prices → cost of production falls → profits recover. As recovery gains momentum, the economy enters expansion again.

| Phase | Direction | Key driver | Output vs trend |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Expansion | ↑ | Lending, borrowing, optimism | Below to above trend |
| Peak | flat | Constraints, cost pressure | At/above trend |
| Recession | ↓ | Inventory glut, falling profits | Falling toward trend |
| Depression | ↓↓ | Negative growth | Below trend |
| Recovery | ↑ | Cost reduction, replacement investment | Below to at trend |

### Definitions
- **Peak**: The point at which an expansion ends and a recession begins. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Trough**: The point at which a depression ends and recovery begins. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Output gap (positive)**: When actual output is below potential output (typical of recession).

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Recession chain reaction:**
Demand falls → inventories ↑ → producers cut output → factor demand ↓ → input prices ↓ → unemployment ↑ → income ↓ → demand ↓ (loop)

**Recovery price mechanism:**
Input prices fell more than product prices in recession → production cost ↓ → profit margins ↑ → replacement / maintenance investment ↑ → employment ↑ → expansion

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Recession and depression are different phenomena. → ✅ Correct: They differ only in *degree and extent* — depression is a deep, prolonged recession.
- ❌ Mistake: Peak is the highest GDP level ever. → ✅ Correct: Peak is the *turning point* between expansion and recession; absolute level can vary.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Peak = expansion → recession turning point
> - Trough = depression → recovery turning point
> - Recession vs depression: degree, not kind
> - Recovery driver: input prices fall more than product prices → profits recover

## Section: Keynes' Theory of Business Cycle 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "appreciate the Keynesian view"; named theory; Check Your Progress 2 Q1 -->

### Core Idea
In *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, Keynes argued that **fluctuations in aggregate effective demand** cause fluctuations in income. Since the propensity to consume is stable in the short run, fluctuations in aggregate demand depend mostly on **investment demand**, and the **multiplier** magnifies any change in investment into a larger change in income. However, Keynesian theory alone fails to explain the *cumulative* character of cycles — that is what Samuelson's accelerator addresses.

> **In Simple Terms:** Keynes says: when business confidence dips, firms invest less; the multiplier turns that small drop into a much bigger drop in national income. But why doesn't the economy just settle at a new lower level instead of cycling? Keynes alone can't say — that's the gap Samuelson fills.

### Key Concepts

#### Effective demand → income via multiplier
Aggregate demand = consumption demand + investment demand. Consumption is stable in the short run, so investment swings drive aggregate demand swings. The multiplier ensures that a given investment change produces a magnified income change: ΔY = ΔI × (1 / (1 − MPC)).

#### The gap Keynes leaves
Example: If investment rises by ₹100 and the multiplier = 4, income rises by ₹400 and reaches a new stable equilibrium. But in real life, the rise in income has *further repercussions* — feedback into investment — which Keynes alone does not explain. This is captured by the **accelerator principle**, and Samuelson combined the two.

### Definitions
- **Aggregate effective demand**: Total demand for consumption goods plus demand for investment goods at the prevailing price level. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Multiplier (k)**: 1 / (1 − MPC). The factor by which a change in autonomous spending changes equilibrium income.

### Mechanisms / Processes
ΔI (autonomous) → multiplier effect → ΔY = ΔI × 1/(1−MPC) → (Keynes stops here) → in reality, ΔY further induces ΔI via accelerator → cycle

### Connections
- Builds on: Keynesian theory of effective demand (prerequisite from earlier blocks).
- Is prerequisite for: Samuelson's Multiplier-Accelerator Model (next section).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - ΔY = ΔI / (1 − MPC) under pure multiplier
> - Keynes: investment is unstable because it depends on profit expectations
> - Limitation: cannot explain *cumulative*, cyclical character — just one-shot magnification

## Section: Samuelson's Multiplier-Accelerator Model 🔴
<!-- Reason: Named model; unit objective "explain the multiplier-accelerator model"; full algebraic derivation in source; Check Your Progress 2 Q2 -->
<!-- Continues in chunk 002 -->

### Core Idea
Samuelson combined the Keynesian **multiplier** with the **accelerator** principle and showed that their interaction — without any external shock — can generate cyclical fluctuations in economic activity. An autonomous rise in investment increases income via the multiplier; the rise in income then induces further investment via the accelerator; the magnitude and pattern of the resulting fluctuations depend on the values of MPC (c) and capital-output ratio (v).

> **In Simple Terms:** Multiplier and accelerator are two amplifiers wired in a feedback loop — any push to investment travels around the loop, and depending on how strong each amplifier is, the loop can settle, oscillate gently, oscillate violently, or explode.

### Key Concepts

#### Mutual interaction: investment ↔ income
ΔIₐ (autonomous investment) → ΔY = ΔIₐ × (1/(1−MPC)) [multiplier] → ΔI_d = v × ΔY [accelerator-induced investment] → aggregate demand and income increase by an even larger amount.

#### Notation
- ΔIₐ: increase in autonomous investment
- ΔY: increase in income
- 1 / (1 − MPC): size of multiplier; MPC = marginal propensity to consume
- ΔI_d: increase in induced investment
- v: size of accelerator (capital-output ratio)

#### The model equations
The interaction between multiplier and accelerator is captured by:

| Eq. | Form | Meaning |
|-----|------|---------|
| 11.1 | Yₜ = Cₜ + Iₜ | Income identity |
| 11.2 | Cₜ = Cₐ + c·Y_(t−1) | Consumption depends on previous period's income (1-period lag) |
| 11.3 | Iₜ = Iₐ + v·(Y_(t−1) − Y_(t−2)) | Induced investment depends on *change* in previous-period income (2-period gap) |
| 11.4 | Yₜ = Cₐ + c·Y_(t−1) + Iₐ + v·(Y_(t−1) − Y_(t−2)) | Substitute 11.2 & 11.3 into 11.1 |
| 11.5 | Y = Cₐ + cY + I (static eqm) | When Yₜ = Y_(t−1) = Y_(t−2): accelerator term = 0; multiplier alone applies |

Where: Cₐ = autonomous consumption, Iₐ = autonomous investment, c = MPC, v = capital-output ratio (accelerator).

#### Why the lags matter
Equation 11.4 is a *dynamic* relation. In static equilibrium, Yₜ = Y_(t−1) = Y_(t−2) so the accelerator term is zero — only the multiplier operates. In a dynamic state, when autonomous investment changes, equation 11.4 describes the path the disequilibrium system follows. Whether the economy converges to a new equilibrium or diverges depends on the values of c (MPC) and v (capital-output ratio).

#### Five paths from (c, v) combinations
Samuelson plotted combinations of c and v on a chart and identified five regions A, B, C, D, E with distinct dynamic behaviour:

| Region | Dynamic behaviour | Cyclical? |
|--------|-------------------|-----------|
| A | Income moves up/down at decreasing rate, settles at new equilibrium (Fig. 11.4a) | No |
| B | Damped cycles whose amplitudes decline until cycles disappear (Fig. 11.4b) | Yes |
| C | Explosive cycles — fluctuations of successively greater amplitude (Fig. 11.4c) | Yes |
| D | Income moves up/down at increasing rate (no cycles, monotonic divergence) | No |
| E | Constant-amplitude fluctuations (Fig. 11.4e) | Yes |

**Key takeaway:** Only regions **B, C, and E** produce business cycles. Regions A and D give monotonic (non-cyclical) paths.

### Definitions
- **Accelerator (v)**: Capital-output ratio; the ratio by which a change in income/output induces a change in investment. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Induced investment (I_d)**: Investment generated by changes in income, equal to v × (Y_(t−1) − Y_(t−2)). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Autonomous investment (Iₐ)**: Investment independent of current income.
- **Multiplier**: 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / (1 − c).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: All combinations of c and v give business cycles. → ✅ Correct: Only regions B, C, and E (out of A–E) give cyclical behaviour.
- ❌ Mistake: Accelerator depends on the *level* of income. → ✅ Correct: Accelerator-induced investment depends on the *change* in income (Y_(t−1) − Y_(t−2)).
- ❌ Mistake: In static equilibrium both multiplier and accelerator operate. → ✅ Correct: In static equilibrium ΔY = 0, so the accelerator term vanishes.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Yₜ = Cₐ + cY_(t−1) + Iₐ + v·(Y_(t−1) − Y_(t−2))
> - Multiplier = 1/(1−c); Accelerator = v
> - Region A: damped non-cyclical; B: damped cycles; C: explosive cycles; D: monotonic divergent; E: constant-amplitude cycles
> - Cycles ONLY in regions B, C, E
> - Static eqm collapses the accelerator term to zero

## Section: Political Business Cycles 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "examine the political business cycle theory"; named theory -->

### Core Idea
The **political business cycle** describes the deliberate stimulation of the economy *just before* an election to improve the incumbent government's re-election prospects. Expansionary monetary and fiscal policies are popular short-term (lower unemployment, growth, public-spending benefits) but harmful long-term (accelerating inflation, worsened trade balance). Governments push these stimuli pre-election and reverse them post-election, producing recurrent cycles tied to the electoral calendar.

> **In Simple Terms:** Politicians fire up the economic engine right before voters head to the polls — cheap money, big spending — and quietly slam on the brakes after winning. Repeat every election, and you get a cycle baked into the political calendar.

### Key Concepts

#### Pre-election stimulus / post-election reversal
- **Before election**: expansionary monetary and fiscal policy → falling unemployment, growth, public service spending.
- **After election**: government reverses earlier decisions — cuts spending, slows money-supply growth, allows interest rates to rise.
- The *regular holding of elections* produces cyclical fluctuation through this recurring stimulus-and-restraint pattern.

#### Why pursue policies known to harm long-term growth
Governments are aware of the adverse long-term effects (accelerating inflation, foreign trade balance damage). They restrict the expansion to a *short horizon immediately before* the election to capture political benefit, then reverse course.

### Definitions
- **Political business cycle**: Stimulation of the economy just prior to an election to improve the incumbent government's re-election prospects, followed by post-election restraint. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
Pre-election expansionary policy → growth ↑ + unemployment ↓ → re-election achieved → policy reversal: spending cut + tighter money + interest rates ↑ → contraction → next election cycle

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Political business cycles are caused by uninformed governments. → ✅ Correct: Governments are aware of the long-term costs but accept them in exchange for short-term political gain.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Stimulus before election, restraint after
> - Driven by election periodicity in democracies
> - Long-term cost: accelerating inflation, trade balance damage

### Connections
- Contrasts with: Keynes' Theory and Samuelson's Model (Chunk 001: Introduction to Business Cycles) — those derive cycles from economic mechanisms, this one from political incentives.

## Section: Dating of Business Cycles (NBER Methodology) 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "appreciate the methodology used by NBER to date the business cycle" -->

### Core Idea
Identifying the chronology of business-cycle turning points is essential for designing macroeconomic stabilisation. The **National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER)** in the US, the CEPR Euro Area Business Cycle Dating Committee in Europe, and similar bodies in India maintain official chronologies. NBER's working definition — formulated by Burns & Mitchell in the 1940s and used for over 50 years — does *not* equate recession with "two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP".

> **In Simple Terms:** NBER doesn't use the popular "two negative-GDP quarters = recession" shortcut. They look at a basket of indicators (GDP, employment, real income, industrial production, sales) and use *judgment* to call peaks and troughs — like umpires of the economy.

### Key Concepts

#### NBER's working definition of recession
A **recession** is "a significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months, normally visible in real GDP, real income, employment, industrial production, and wholesale-retail sales." An **expansion** is the period from a trough to a peak; a recession is the period from a peak to a trough.

#### Why NBER avoids the "two-quarters of declining real GDP" rule
NBER focuses on *spread, depth and duration* — the decline must be widespread across the economy and visible in multiple indicators, not just GDP. This handles cases where:
- A recession contains a brief expansion followed by further decline.
- An expansion contains a brief contraction followed by further growth.

#### What NBER examines
The Committee compares behaviour of multiple measures of broad activity:
- Real GDP (product side and income side)
- Economy-wide employment
- Real income
- Real sales (sub-economy)
- Federal Reserve's Index of Industrial Production (IIP)

The Committee has *no fixed rule*; it applies judgment based on the working definition. There is no fixed definition of "economic activity" either.

#### Other dating bodies
- **CEPR Euro Area Business Cycle Dating Committee** — establishes chronology of recessions/expansions for Euro-area member countries.
- **India** — some attempts have been made to determine the chronology of business cycles.

### Definitions
- **Recession (NBER working definition)**: A significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months, normally visible in real GDP, real income, employment, industrial production, and wholesale-retail sales. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Expansion (NBER working definition)**: A period of substantial rise in economic activity that spreads across the economy and usually lasts for several years.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: NBER calls a recession when GDP falls for two consecutive quarters. → ✅ Correct: NBER explicitly rejects that rule. Recession is defined by depth, breadth, and duration across multiple indicators.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - NBER definition is *judgmental*, not mechanical
> - Recession ≠ "two negative GDP quarters" (NBER explicitly rejects this)
> - Multi-indicator: GDP, employment, real income, industrial production, sales
> - Definition originated under Burns & Mitchell, in use 50+ years
> - CEPR plays the same role for the Euro area

## Section: Economic Indicators 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "distinguish between leading, lagging and coincident indicators"; Check Your Progress 2 Q3 & Q4 -->

### Core Idea
Because individual economic series have *diverse timing patterns*, no single series captures the whole economy's state. Following Mitchell and Burns at NBER, economists classify cyclical indicators by the **timing** of their turning points relative to the reference cycle: **leading**, **coincident**, and **lagging**. G.H. Moore (1950) refined the procedure further, screening 801 series down to 21 that passed tests for *conformity* and *timing*.

> **In Simple Terms:** Some economic signals turn before the economy turns (early warning), some turn at the same time (current dashboard), some turn after (confirmation). Sorting them into these three baskets is how forecasters read the cycle.

### Key Concepts

#### How indicators were derived
- Burns & Mitchell studied ~487 economic variables to test whether their turning points led, coincided, or lagged the US business cycle. They selected **71 series**, ordered by average lead/lag against reference revivals.
- On average, leading series were 1–10 months ahead; lagging series were 1–12 months behind; six series had no average lead/lag.
- **G.H. Moore (1950)** improved the method: from 801 series, he chose **21** that passed two tests:
  - **Conformity** — consistency with which the series has conformed to business cycles.
  - **Timing** — consistency with which the series' turning points have led, lagged, or coincided with the reference data.

#### Three categories

##### Leading Indicators
Help assess where the economy is *heading*; signal upcoming turns *before* they happen. Examples:
- **Stock market** (e.g., S&P 500) — rises before favourable economic conditions appear; falls before economic conditions seem to warrant.
- **Interest rates** — low rates stimulate borrowing and buying (favouring economy); rising rates eventually slow it as fewer people borrow.

##### Lagging Indicators
Shift *after* the economy changes. Don't tell us where the economy is headed but show how it has changed and help identify long-term trends. Examples:
- **GDP** — long lag between data compilation and release; many call a recession underway after two consecutive declining quarters of GDP (note: NBER rejects this rule, but the public uses it).
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** — reveals information already known to consumers.

##### Coincident Indicators
Move (more or less) in parallel with general economic conditions; reflect the current status. Examples:
- **Industrial production**
- **Turnover**

(Source: US indicators published in *Business Conditions Digest*, Bureau of Economic Analysis, US Department of Commerce.)

### Definitions
- **Conformity** (of a series to the cycle): The consistency with which the series has conformed to business cycles. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Timing** (of a series): The consistency with which a time series' turning point has led, lagged, or roughly coincided with the reference data. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Leading indicator**: A series whose turning points consistently *precede* the cyclical turning points of the reference cycle (1–10 months on average).
- **Lagging indicator**: A series whose turning points consistently *follow* cyclical turning points (1–12 months on average). Useful for *confirming* a pattern.
- **Coincident indicator**: A series whose turning points roughly coincide with the cyclical turning points of the reference cycle.
- **Importance of lagging indicators**: Their importance lies in their ability to *confirm* that a pattern is occurring (e.g., unemployment confirms a poorly-performing economy). ⭐ (exam-important)

### Examples
**Example: Stock market as a leading indicator.** The S&P 500 begins to rise *before* economic conditions seem favourable, and begins to decline *before* conditions seem to warrant. Forecasters use this signal to anticipate turns.

**Example: Unemployment as a lagging indicator.** A rising unemployment rate confirms that the economy has been doing poorly (after the fact), but does not predict the next turn.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: GDP is a leading indicator. → ✅ Correct: GDP is a lagging indicator due to publication delay.
- ❌ Mistake: Lagging indicators are useless because they're late. → ✅ Correct: Their value is *confirmatory* — they verify a pattern is real.
- ❌ Mistake: Stock prices are coincident with GDP. → ✅ Correct: Stock prices are a *leading* indicator.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Leading: stock prices, interest rates, building permits, consumer sentiment, money supply changes
> - Coincident: GNP, industrial production, employment, personal income
> - Lagging: unemployment duration, CPI, unit labour costs, short-term interest rates, business expenditures
> - Two-screen test (G.H. Moore): conformity + timing
> - Lagging indicators *confirm*; leading indicators *predict*

## Section: Empirical Analysis 🟡

### Core Idea
Researchers face challenges in representing the cyclical component of complex economic data. Various detrending techniques (moving averages, piecewise trends, **H-P filter**) are used; there is no single correct approach. Modern business cycle theory holds that **a single theory should explain both growth and cyclical features**, in contrast to the older view that growth and cycles required separate theories.

### Key Concepts

#### Evolution of empirical focus
- **19th century**: Researchers focused on *real variables* (e.g., agricultural output) to explain cycles. Classical economists assumed perfectly competitive markets and flexible prices and wages → ruled out the possibility of business cycles. They treated money as *neutral*.
- **20th century**: Economists ascribed *monetary variables* (money supply, interest rates, prices) as important causes of cycles.

#### Backus & Kehoe (1992) — "International Evidence on the Historical Properties of Business Cycles"
Studied a group of macro variables (private consumption, fixed investment, government purchases, net exports) in 10 countries: Australia, Canada, Denmark, Germany, Italy, Japan, Norway, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the US.

**Findings (great regularity, with exceptions):**
- Magnitude of output fluctuations varies across countries and over time.
- Macro relationships are remarkably stable.
- **Investment is 2–4 times more volatile than output.**
- **Consumption volatility is roughly the same as output volatility.**
- Both investment and consumption are **strongly pro-cyclical**.
- **Trade balance is generally counter-cyclical** — larger deficits during booms, smaller during recessions.
- **Government purchases show no systematic cyclical tendency** — exception to the general regularity.

#### Konstantakopoulou & Tsionas (2014) — "Half a century of empirical evidence of Business Cycles in OECD countries"
Used the H-P filter on 1960–2010 OECD data. Found **two distinct common cycles**:
1. **Euro area cycle** — Germany, France, Italy, Netherlands, Australia, Belgium.
2. **World cycle** — US, Canada, UK.

#### Detrending techniques
- **Stochastic trend approach** — first-difference the (logs of) data to remove the trend.
- **Hodrick-Prescott filter (H-P filter)** — most widely used; separates growth and business-cycle components.

#### Old vs new view
| View | Approach | Theories needed |
|------|----------|-----------------|
| Old (prevailing for a long time) | Different theories for growth and for cycles | Two |
| Modern business cycle theory | Same theory should explain both | One |

### Definitions
- **Hodrick-Prescott filter (H-P filter)**: A statistical detrending technique used to separate the cyclical component from the long-term trend in time-series data.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Government purchases are pro-cyclical. → ✅ Correct: Backus & Kehoe found *no systematic cyclical tendency* for government purchases.
- ❌ Mistake: Trade balance is pro-cyclical. → ✅ Correct: Trade balance is *counter-cyclical* (larger deficits in booms).

## Section: New-Classical View on Business Cycle 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "describe salient features of new-classical economics", "outline assumptions and methodology"; Check Your Progress 1 Q1 & Q2 -->

### Core Idea
**New-classical economics**, led by Robert Lucas and Thomas Sargent, revived classical ideas with new analytical tools. It holds that markets *always* clear, agents have *rational expectations*, and policy-induced changes in output happen only when policy is *unexpected*. Business cycles arise from (i) inter-temporal substitution between labour and leisure and (ii) the Lucas supply function (signal-extraction error).

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine an economy of clairvoyant households and firms with perfectly flexible prices. Government policy that anyone can predict has no effect — only *surprises* move output. Booms and busts are people reacting rationally to surprises, not failures of the market.

### Key Concepts

#### Background: from Friedman to New-Classical
During the 1950s–60s, Keynesian economics dominated. Milton Friedman challenged it, arguing changes in aggregate income are explained mostly by **monetary policy**. Had the government redeemed bonds via open-market operations during the Great Depression, money supply would have risen and the impact would have been less severe. Friedman also disagreed with the inflation–unemployment trade-off of the Phillips Curve, introduced expectations, and showed the Phillips curve is **vertical in the long run**: trade-off only exists in the short run; in the long run unemployment cannot fall below the natural rate. The 1970s **stagflation** further consolidated the dissent on Keynesian economics, leading to the new-classical revolution under Lucas and Sargent.

#### Three foundational assumptions
1. **Rational expectations hypothesis** — people correctly anticipate the effect of government actions and take counter-measures to neutralise the impact of policy. Only *unexpected* policy shocks lead to temporary deviations of output and employment from natural levels.
2. **Continuous market clearing** — markets clear at every point in time. Demand always equals supply; the economy is always in equilibrium. Wages and prices are *flexible*; adjustments are *instantaneous*.
3. **Aggregate supply hypothesis** — the supply of labour by workers and of output by firms depends on *relative* prices. This is the **Lucas supply function** (discussed in Unit 7).

#### A debatable consequence: "voluntary unemployment"
Under continuous labour-market clearing, anyone willing to work at the going wage gets employed → unemployment is treated as a *voluntary* phenomenon. (The source flags this as a debatable assumption.)

#### Three prominent features of new-classical economics
1. **Microfoundation** — macroeconomic phenomena are explained through the behaviour of microeconomic agents (households via utility functions; firms via production functions).
2. **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE)** —
   - **General equilibrium**: pertains to the whole economy.
   - **Dynamic**: inter-temporal models; agents optimise over time (dynamic optimisation, see MEC-203 Block 7).
   - **Stochastic**: shocks (demand, supply, policy) are random/exogenous.
   - Simple DSGE models have three blocks: (i) demand block, (ii) supply block, (iii) monetary policy equation (e.g., a policy rule).
3. **Quantitative calibration** — instead of econometric *estimation*, researchers use **calibration**: choose parameters from past studies and use numerical iteration to replicate the empirical economy. Calibration ≠ estimation.

#### Why business cycles occur if markets always clear
Two basic reasons:

(i) **Inter-temporal substitution between labour and leisure**:
- If actual real wage > expected real wage → household substitutes leisure with labour → works more hours → labour supply ↑ → output ↑ above potential → boom.
- If actual real wage < expected real wage → household enjoys more leisure, supplies less labour → output ↓ → recession.
- Labour-supply changes are *voluntary* responses to rational expectations of real wages.

(ii) **Lucas supply function (signal-extraction problem)**:
Firms cannot distinguish a *general* price rise from a *relative* price rise for their own product. When there is a general price rise, the cost of production also rises, so there is no real relative gain — but firms see the price of their output rise and *increase production*. This causes a (positive) deviation of employment and output from natural levels → boom. The reverse generates recession.

#### A bold inference
Anticipated government policy is **ineffective** — it doesn't change real output or employment. Only *unexpected* policy shocks matter, and only temporarily.

### Definitions
- **Rational expectations hypothesis**: Economic agents form expectations using all available information, including a correct understanding of how the economy works, so they correctly anticipate systematic government actions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Continuous market clearing**: Demand equals supply at every point in time; prices and wages are perfectly flexible and adjust instantaneously. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Lucas supply function**: Output supply depends on the difference between actual and expected price levels (relative-price perception); firms suffer signal-extraction problems. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Microfoundation**: Macroeconomic phenomena explained via the optimising behaviour of micro agents (households, firms). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **DSGE model**: Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium model — inter-temporal, whole-economy, with random shocks. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Calibration**: Choosing model parameter values (often from past studies) so that model output best replicates empirical data; uses numerical iteration. Distinct from econometric estimation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Inter-temporal substitution between labour and leisure**: Household decisions to shift work effort between time periods in response to relative wage and interest-rate movements. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Inter-temporal labour substitution chain (boom case):**
Actual real wage rises above expected real wage → household substitutes leisure with labour → labour supply ↑ → output ↑ above potential → boom

**Lucas supply chain (boom case):**
General price rise → firm misperceives as relative price rise → firm raises production → output and employment deviate above natural levels → boom

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: New-classical models assume government policy *can* stabilise output. → ✅ Correct: Anticipated policy is *ineffective*; only unexpected policy temporarily moves output.
- ❌ Mistake: Calibration is the same as econometric estimation. → ✅ Correct: Calibration picks parameter values to *replicate* data; estimation derives them statistically. They are different procedures.
- ❌ Mistake: Under new-classical, unemployment is structural. → ✅ Correct: It is treated as *voluntary* — anyone willing to work at the prevailing wage is employed.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three assumptions: rational expectations, continuous market clearing, Lucas supply (relative prices)
> - Three features: microfoundation, DSGE, calibration
> - Two reasons for cycles: inter-temporal labour-leisure substitution + Lucas signal-extraction error
> - Anticipated policy is *ineffective*; only unexpected shocks move output temporarily
> - Phillips curve is vertical in the long run (Friedman)

- Is prerequisite for: Real Business Cycle Theory (next section) — RBC inherits new-classical assumptions and tools.
- Contrasts with: Keynesian and Samuelson theories (Chunk 001: Introduction to Business Cycles) — those rely on demand-side mechanisms and rigidities; new-classical denies both.

## Section: Real Factors vs. Monetary Factors 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "bring out important features of real business cycle theory"; named theory; Check Your Progress 2 Q1 -->

### Core Idea
**Real Business Cycle (RBC) theory**, originating with Kydland & Prescott's 1982 paper "Time to Build and Aggregate Fluctuations," argues that business cycles are caused by **real shocks** (especially productivity / supply shocks) — *not* by fluctuations in monetary variables. RBC is a distinct branch of new-classical macroeconomics: it inherits the assumptions and tools (DSGE, microfoundation, rational expectations) but emphasises real disturbances rather than monetary ones.

> **In Simple Terms:** RBC says: forget money supply. Cycles happen because the *real* engine of the economy — its productivity — gets jolted (oil shocks, new tech, droughts, regulation changes), and rational households and firms respond optimally. The optimal response itself looks like a boom or a recession.

### Key Concepts

#### Two types of shocks
| Type | Source | Curve affected |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **Nominal shocks** | Money supply or money demand | LM curve |
| **Real shocks** | Production function, real government spending, savings/consumption decisions | IS curve (goods and labour markets) |

RBC models attribute cycles primarily to **real shocks** affecting the production function — *supply shocks* or *productivity shocks*.

#### Sources of supply shocks
1. Development of new products or production methods (production technology)
2. Changes in the quality of labour or capital
3. Changes in the availability of raw materials (relative prices, oil crisis)
4. Unusually good or bad weather (drought, flood, earthquake)
5. Changes in government regulations affecting production (tax rate, subsidies, etc.)

#### Effect of a temporary adverse supply shock (Fig. 12.1)
Negative productivity shock → marginal productivity of labour ↓ → demand for labour ↓ → real wage ↓ → households see lower wage → labour supply ↓ → output ↓ → **recession**.

A *beneficial* productivity shock works the opposite way: temporary productivity hike → investment and work effort ↑ in current period → real output and consumption ↑ → boom. Once the shock dies down, the economy returns to natural output and employment.

#### Two foundational principles of RBC
1. **Money has little role to play** in business cycles.
2. Business cycles occur as the **rational response of optimising agents** (firms and households) to real shocks. The economy is *continuously at equilibrium*; supply of labour and output reflect rational expectations.

#### Three differences between RBC and the broader new-classical school
| Issue | New-classical (Lucas) | RBC (Kydland & Prescott) |
|-------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Distinction between short- and long-run | Maintained | **Removed** — RBC integrates growth theory with business cycle theory |
| Information of agents | Agents get *fooled* — cannot extract correct signal from price changes (signal-extraction problem) | Agents have **complete information** |
| Source of shocks | Monetary factors | **Real factors** |

#### Three policy implications of RBC
1. **Don't stabilise output**: government should stabilise *prices* — i.e., **inflation targeting**.
2. **Don't use fiscal policy on aggregate demand** (taxes/subsidies): may not be effective due to **Ricardian equivalence**.
3. **Use fiscal policy on the supply side**: provide positive supply shocks.

#### Criticisms of RBC
- Assumption of *instantaneous market clearing* is unrealistic — prices and wages are rigid in reality.
- Little empirical evidence of large *aggregate* technology shocks.

### Definitions
- **Real shock**: A shock to the production function, real government spending, or savings/consumption decisions; affects the IS curve. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Nominal shock**: A shock to money supply or money demand; affects the LM curve. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Supply shock / productivity shock**: A shock to the production function arising from technology changes, input quality, raw materials availability, weather, or regulation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Real Business Cycle (RBC) theory**: A theory that business cycles are caused by real (especially productivity) shocks acting on rational agents in continuously clearing markets. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ricardian equivalence**: The proposition that government deficit-financed spending may not be effective because households anticipate future tax liabilities. (Referenced as the reason fiscal demand-side policy fails in RBC.)

### Examples
**Example: Negative supply shock (e.g., adverse weather, oil-price spike).**
Marginal product of labour falls → demand for labour falls → real wage falls → labour supply falls → output falls → recession. Once the shock dissipates, employment and output return to natural levels.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: RBC theory blames money supply for cycles. → ✅ Correct: RBC explicitly says money has little role; *real* shocks drive cycles.
- ❌ Mistake: RBC and new-classical economics are identical. → ✅ Correct: RBC removes the short-run/long-run distinction, assumes complete information, and treats shocks as real (not monetary).
- ❌ Mistake: RBC supports active fiscal demand management. → ✅ Correct: RBC opposes fiscal demand management (Ricardian equivalence) and supports supply-side fiscal measures only.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Origin: Kydland & Prescott (1982), "Time to Build and Aggregate Fluctuations"
> - Cause: real shocks (productivity), not monetary
> - 5 sources of supply shocks: tech, input quality, raw materials, weather, regulation
> - Temporary adverse shock chain: MPL ↓ → labour demand ↓ → real wage ↓ → labour supply ↓ → output ↓
> - Policy: target inflation, no fiscal demand management, fiscal supply-side OK
> - Criticisms: instantaneous clearing unrealistic; weak evidence of aggregate technology shocks

### Connections
- Builds on: New-Classical View (this chunk) — RBC is a branch of new-classical macro.
- Contrasts with: Keynesian theory (Chunk 001: Introduction to Business Cycles) — Keynes blamed demand fluctuations and rigidities; RBC blames real productivity shocks in flexible markets.

## Section: A Baseline RBC Model 🔴
<!-- Reason: Core derivation; multiple equations the student must reproduce; named extension of Ramsey model -->

### Core Idea
The baseline RBC model extends the **Ramsey model** (a Walrasian general-equilibrium framework with a representative household and firm) by adding (i) **random shocks** to technology and government purchases, and (ii) **endogenous labour supply** (households choose between work and leisure via a utility function). The result is a microfounded, dynamic, stochastic general-equilibrium model whose macroeconomic fluctuations come from real disturbances.

> **In Simple Terms:** Take the Ramsey growth model (steady-state economy with one household, one firm), then add two twists: technology and government spending get random shocks, and households can choose how much to work. The model's fluctuations are real-shock-driven cycles.

### Key Concepts

#### Walrasian foundation
The Walrasian model is a competitive general-equilibrium model *without* (i) asymmetric information, (ii) externalities, (iii) missing markets, or (iv) other imperfections. RBC theory aims to show that such a Walrasian model can explain observed macroeconomic fluctuations — i.e., no fundamental departure from microeconomics is needed.

#### Two modifications to the Ramsey model
1. **Add disturbances** — technology shocks (production function changes) and government-purchase shocks. Both are *real* disturbances, not monetary.
2. **Endogenous labour supply** — instead of constant or smoothly growing labour supply, households allocate time between work and leisure via a utility function. Work ↑ ⇒ labour supply ↑ ⇒ leisure ↓.

#### Setup: large number of homogenous price-taking firms and households
- Two inputs: capital (K) and labour (L).
- Technology is exogenous, denoted Aₜ in period t.
- Production function is **Cobb-Douglas**.

#### Production function

```
Yₜ = Aₜ · F(Kₜ, Lₜ) = Kₜ^α · (Aₜ Lₜ)^(1−α),    where 0 < α < 1     ... (12.1)
```

#### Capital accumulation
Output is divided among consumption (C), investment (I), and government purchases (G). With δ = depreciation rate:

```
K_(t+1) = Kₜ + Iₜ − δKₜ
        = Kₜ + Yₜ − Cₜ − Gₜ − δKₜ                ... (12.2)
(Since Cₜ + Iₜ + Gₜ = Yₜ ⇒ Iₜ = Yₜ − Cₜ − Gₜ)
```

#### Factor prices = marginal products
Labour and capital are paid their marginal products. From partial derivatives of (12.1) w.r.t. L and K:

```
wₜ = (1 − α) · Aₜ · (Kₜ / (Aₜ Lₜ))^α                 ... (12.3)
rₜ = α · (Aₜ Lₜ / Kₜ)^(1−α)                          ... (12.4)
```

#### Household utility
Representative household maximises the expected value of:

```
U = Σ e^(−ρt) · u(cₜ, 1 − ℓₜ) · (Nₜ / H)             ... (12.5)
```
where:
- u(•) = instantaneous utility function with two arguments — *consumption per member* (cₜ) and *leisure per member* (1 − ℓₜ).
- ρ = discount rate (time preference).
- Nₜ = population, H = number of households; average members per household = Nₜ/H.
- Time endowment per member = 1 (normalised).
- Since households are homogenous: c = C/N, ℓ = L/N.

#### Population growth
Exogenous population growth rate is n:

```
ln Nₜ = N̄ + n·t,    where n < ρ                   ... (12.6)
⇒ Nₜ = e^(N̄ + n·t)
```

#### Technology process
Trend growth in technology with rate g:

```
ln Aₜ = Ā + g·t                                    ... (12.7)
```

To include random shocks, add a stochastic component Ãₜ:

```
ln Aₜ = Ā + g·t + Ãₜ                               ... (12.8)
```

The disturbance Ãₜ follows a **first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) process**:

```
Ãₜ = ρ_A · Ã_(t−1) + ε_(A,t),    where −1 < ρ_A < 1     ... (12.9)
```

with ε_(A,t) = stochastic error term (white noise: zero mean, zero correlation with other variables). This captures gradual decay of technology shocks: Ãₜ is a fraction ρ_A of last period's value plus white noise.

#### Government-purchases process
Same form: a trend component plus a white-noise component. Per-capita government purchases have a trend growth rate, but over time may become arbitrarily large or small relative to the economy.

```
ln Gₜ = Ḡ + (n + g)·t + G̃ₜ                         ... (12.10)
G̃ₜ = ρ_G · G̃_(t−1) + ε_(G,t),    −1 < ρ_A < 1       ... (12.11)
```

(Note the source uses "−1 < ρ_A < 1" in 12.10 and 12.11 too — likely a typo for ρ_G — but reproduced as-is from source.)

This completes the description of the baseline RBC model.

### Definitions
- **Walrasian model**: Competitive general-equilibrium model without asymmetric information, externalities, missing markets, or other imperfections. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ramsey model**: Baseline general-equilibrium growth model with a representative household and firm; converges to a balanced growth path. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **AR(1) (first-order autoregressive) process**: A stochastic process where the current value equals a fraction of the previous value plus a white-noise innovation. Used to model gradual decay of shocks. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **White-noise error**: Stochastic term with zero mean and zero correlation with other variables in the equation.
- **Cobb-Douglas production function (this model)**: Y = K^α · (AL)^(1−α), with constant returns to scale. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Decay of a technology shock:**
ε_(A,t) hits the economy → Ãₜ jumps → Ã_(t+1) = ρ_A · Ãₜ + ε_(A,t+1) → effect persists fractionally → eventually dies down.

**Capital accumulation identity:**
Yₜ = Cₜ + Iₜ + Gₜ ⇒ Iₜ = Yₜ − Cₜ − Gₜ ⇒ K_(t+1) = Kₜ(1 − δ) + Iₜ

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: RBC uses an exogenous (constant) labour supply like the Ramsey model. → ✅ Correct: RBC *endogenises* labour supply via the utility function; this is one of the two key modifications.
- ❌ Mistake: Aₜ is assumed deterministic in the full RBC model. → ✅ Correct: It has a trend component plus a stochastic Ãₜ that follows an AR(1) process.
- ❌ Mistake: Both shocks are required to be permanent. → ✅ Correct: Persistence depends on the AR(1) coefficient (ρ_A or ρ_G); shocks decay gradually.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Production: Yₜ = Kₜ^α · (Aₜ Lₜ)^(1−α), 0 < α < 1
> - Wage: wₜ = (1 − α) · Aₜ · (Kₜ/Aₜ Lₜ)^α
> - Interest rate: rₜ = α · (Aₜ Lₜ/Kₜ)^(1−α)
> - Population: ln Nₜ = N̄ + nt
> - Technology: ln Aₜ = Ā + gt + Ãₜ; Ãₜ = ρ_A · Ã_(t−1) + ε_(A,t)
> - Government: ln Gₜ = Ḡ + (n+g)t + G̃ₜ; G̃ₜ = ρ_G · G̃_(t−1) + ε_(G,t)
> - Two modifications: random shocks + endogenous labour supply

## Section: Inter-temporal Substitution in Labour Supply (Setup) 🟡
<!-- Continues in chunk 004 -->

### Core Idea
The two key features of RBC that distinguish it from Ramsey are (i) leisure in the utility function and (ii) random shocks to technology and government purchases. This section sets up the **one-period** household optimisation problem to show how labour supply responds to wages — and finds that, in the static one-period case with logarithmic utility and no initial wealth, **labour supply is independent of the wage rate**.

### Key Concepts

#### Static (one-period) household problem
Assume household lives one period, has one member, no initial wealth.
- Objective: maximise ln c + b · ln(1 − ℓ)
- Budget: c = w·ℓ
- Lagrangian:

```
ζ = ln c + b · ln(1 − ℓ) + λ(wℓ − c)            ... (12.12)
```

#### First-order conditions
With respect to c and ℓ:

```
1/c − λ = 0                                      ... (12.13)
−b/(1 − ℓ) + λw = 0                              ... (12.14)
```

From (12.13): λ = 1/c = 1/(wℓ) (using budget constraint).

Substituting into (12.14):

```
−b/(1 − ℓ) + (1/(wℓ)) · w = 0
⇒ −b/(1 − ℓ) + 1/ℓ = 0                          ... (12.15)
```

#### Result: labour supply independent of wage in static case
Equation (12.15) does not contain w → **labour supply is independent of the wage rate** in the one-period setup.

**Reason**: Utility is logarithmic in consumption, and the household has no initial wealth → the *income effect* and *substitution effect* of a wage change exactly offset each other.

### Definitions
- **Inter-temporal substitution in labour supply**: The effect of relative wages and interest rates *across* time periods on labour supply (or employment or leisure). ⭐ (exam-important) [Full definition continues into Chunk 004.]

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Set up Lagrangian with budget constraint c = wℓ
2. Derive FOCs for c and ℓ
3. Eliminate λ using budget
4. Result: equation in ℓ alone → wage cancels out under log utility + no initial wealth

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: A wage rise always increases labour supply. → ✅ Correct: Under log utility with no initial wealth in a one-period model, income and substitution effects exactly offset → wage doesn't affect labour supply.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Static one-period log-utility: labour supply is wage-independent
> - Reason: income effect = substitution effect
> - To see wage effects on labour, need ≥ 2 periods (continues in Chunk 004)

## Section: Two-Period Inter-temporal Labour Supply 🔴
<!-- See chunk 003 for start of this section -->
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "explain the inter-temporal substitution in labour supply"; Check Your Progress 2 Q2, Q3, Q4 -->

### Core Idea
Extending the household problem from one period (where labour supply was wage-independent) to **two periods** shows that **relative wages** and the **interest rate** *do* affect labour supply across time. The household optimises consumption and labour in both periods under a lifetime budget constraint; the FOCs yield a clean expression for the **ratio of labour supplies** in the two periods as a function of the **relative wage** and **interest rate**.

> **In Simple Terms:** When the household lives for two periods, "work now vs work later" becomes a real choice. If the wage is higher today than tomorrow, work more today. If interest rates rise, also work more today (earn now, save now, enjoy later).

### Key Concepts

#### Setup (two-period household)
- One member, no initial wealth, lives two periods.
- No uncertainty about interest rate or second-period wage.
- Lifetime budget constraint:

```
c₁ + (1/(1+r))·c₂ = w₁·ℓ₁ + (1/(1+r))·w₂·ℓ₂          ... (12.16)
```

where r = real interest rate.

#### Lagrangian
```
ζ = ln c₁ + b·ln(1 − ℓ₁) + e^(−ρ)·[ln c₂ + b·ln(1 − ℓ₂)]
    + λ[w₁ℓ₁ + (1/(1+r))·w₂ℓ₂ − c₁ − (1/(1+r))·c₂]    ... (12.17)
```

Choice variables: c₁, c₂, ℓ₁, ℓ₂.

#### First-order conditions for ℓ₁ and ℓ₂

```
b/(1 − ℓ₁) = λ·w₁                                    ... (12.18)
e^(−ρ)·b/(1 − ℓ₂) = (1/(1+r))·λ·w₂                   ... (12.19)
```

Divide (12.18) by w₁ and (12.19) by w₂/(1+r) to isolate λ:

```
b/[(1 − ℓ₁)·w₁] = λ
e^(−ρ)·b·(1+r)/[(1 − ℓ₂)·w₂] = λ                     ... (12.20)
```

Set the two expressions for λ equal and rearrange.

#### The relative-labour-supply equation

```
(1 − ℓ₁) / (1 − ℓ₂) = [b / (e^(−ρ)·b·(1+r))] · (w₂/w₁)
                    = [1 / (e^(−ρ)·(1+r))] · (w₂/w₁)     ... (12.21)
```

(Note: source text writes "(1−r)" in some places where "(1+r)" is intended — the derivation flow makes 1+r the consistent reading.)

#### What the equation tells us

(1 − ℓ₁) is *leisure* in period 1. So the equation relates **relative leisure** and equivalently **relative labour supply** to the relative wage and the interest rate.

##### Effect of a relative wage rise
If **w₁ rises relative to w₂**:
- Then ℓ₁ must increase (leisure (1 − ℓ₁) must decrease) to satisfy (12.21).
- ⇒ Labour supply rises in period 1 relative to period 2 — the household substitutes leisure between time periods to capture the higher first-period wage.
- The **elasticity of substitution** between leisure in the two time periods is **1** (because of log utility).

##### Effect of an interest-rate rise
If **r rises**:
- The right-hand side falls relative to the left (since (1+r) is in the denominator), so labour supply in period 1 must rise relative to period 2.
- Intuition: a higher r increases the incentive to **work more, earn more, and save more** in period 1 because saved earnings grow faster.
- This is why interest rates matter for cyclical employment.

#### What "inter-temporal substitution in labour supply" means
The effect of *relative wage* and *interest rate* on labour supply (or employment or leisure) is called **inter-temporal substitution in labour supply**.

### Definitions
- **Inter-temporal substitution in labour supply (full)**: The shifting of labour supply across time periods in response to changes in *relative wages* and the *interest rate*. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Elasticity of substitution between leisure in two periods**: Equals **1** under logarithmic utility — a 1% change in the relative wage produces a 1% change in relative leisure. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Wage rise in period 1 chain:**
w₁/w₂ ↑ → ℓ₁ ↑ relative to ℓ₂ → labour supply ↑ in period 1 → output ↑ in period 1 → expansion (or boom) phase

**Interest-rate rise in period 1 chain:**
r ↑ → incentive to save ↑ → work more in period 1 → labour supply in period 1 ↑ relative to period 2 → output ↑ in period 1

### Examples
**Example: Calculation flow for the relative labour-supply equation.**
1. Lifetime budget constraint (12.16).
2. Set up Lagrangian (12.17).
3. Take FOCs for ℓ₁ (12.18) and ℓ₂ (12.19).
4. Divide each FOC by the corresponding wage so λ stands alone (12.20).
5. Equate the two expressions for λ and rearrange to get (12.21).

This is the derivation a student is expected to reproduce per Check Your Progress 2 Q2 and Q3.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: A higher interest rate decreases labour supply in period 1. → ✅ Correct: A higher r raises the *return to saving*, so households work *more* in period 1 to earn and save more.
- ❌ Mistake: The static one-period result (wage doesn't matter) carries over to two periods. → ✅ Correct: Once the household lives two periods, *relative* wages and interest enter the labour-supply decision.
- ❌ Mistake: Inter-temporal substitution depends only on wages. → ✅ Correct: It depends on both relative wages **and** the interest rate.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - (1 − ℓ₁)/(1 − ℓ₂) = [1/(e^(−ρ)·(1+r))] · (w₂/w₁)
> - w₁/w₂ ↑ ⇒ ℓ₁ ↑ relative to ℓ₂
> - r ↑ ⇒ ℓ₁ ↑ relative to ℓ₂ (work more now to save more)
> - Elasticity of substitution between period leisures = 1 (log utility)
> - Static (1-period) result: wage irrelevant; dynamic (2-period): wage and r both matter

## Section: Impact of Supply Shocks to the Economy 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "assess the role of supply shocks"; Check Your Progress 3 Q1; central RBC mechanism -->

### Core Idea
A favourable, exogenous, **temporary** technology shock raises Aₜ in equation (12.1), which shifts the production function upward and makes it *steeper*. Marginal productivity of labour rises, so output rises at the existing labour input and rises further if labour input also expands. Households save part of the windfall — leading to higher capital stock in future periods. **Permanent** shocks behave differently: lower incentive to save, more consumption, and possibly smaller increase in labour input — but capital and employment still rise persistently.

> **In Simple Terms:** A productivity shock is like a sudden upgrade to the economy's machines. Workers get more productive (steeper production function), so firms pay more, hire more, and produce more. Households save part of the bonus, which means more machines tomorrow — the boom doesn't end overnight.

### Key Concepts

#### Mechanics of a temporary positive technology shock (Fig. 12.2)
- Initial level of technology: A₀ₜ → production function: Kₜ^α · (A₀ₜ Lₜ)^(1−α).
- L₀ is the optimal labour at this level → equilibrium output Y₀.
- Shock raises technology to A₁ₜ → production function shifts upward to Kₜ^α · (A₁ₜ Lₜ)^(1−α).
- The shifted production function is **steeper** at any L → marginal product of labour ↑.
- **At the same labour input L₀** → output rises from Y₀ to Y₁′.
- **If labour input rises to L₁** → output rises further to Y₁.
- The favourable shock thus shifts the *entire production possibilities frontier*.

#### Distribution of the increased output (saving vs consumption)
- One possibility: consume all of the increase. Unlikely for a *temporary* shock — households want to save part for future consumption.
- If saving rises, equation (12.2) implies investment rises.
- Higher investment → higher capital stock in next and future periods.
- Therefore: **temporary technology shock → higher future capital stock**.

#### Permanent shock — different response
A permanent (or long-lasting) shock causes higher output for a longer period. So:
- Incentive to save *falls* (no need to smooth a one-off windfall).
- Consumption *rises*.
- Increase in labour input may not be high.
- Nevertheless, a *persistent* productivity shock causes increases in output, capital stock, and employment.

| Shock type | Saving | Consumption | Labour input change | Capital stock |
|------------|--------|-------------|---------------------|---------------|
| Temporary positive | ↑ (smooth windfall) | small ↑ | ↑ | ↑ in future |
| Permanent positive | small/no ↑ (no need to smooth) | ↑↑ | smaller ↑ | ↑ persistently |

#### Persistence in RBC
The effect of technology shocks *persists* even after the shock ends, because optimising agents adjust capital, employment, and consumption gradually. This is RBC's answer to *why business cycles last so long*.

#### Keynesian criticism of RBC's persistence claim
Keynesian economists argue this persistence story **cannot explain the persistence of real-world business cycles**. RBC believers counter that the dynamic responses of optimising agents to changes in economic conditions have *sustained effects* that explain periods of persistently high or low activity.

### Definitions
- **Temporary supply shock**: A productivity shock lasting one period; households respond by saving part of the windfall.
- **Permanent supply shock**: A long-lasting shock to productivity; households consume more and save less because there is no need to smooth.
- **Persistence (in RBC)**: The property that shocks have *sustained* effects on output, capital, and employment because optimising agents adjust gradually. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Temporary positive technology shock (full chain):**
A₀ₜ → A₁ₜ (rise) → production function shifts up + steeper → MP_L ↑ → at L₀, Y₀ → Y₁′ → if L rises to L₁, Y rises further to Y₁ → households save part → I ↑ → K_(t+1) ↑ → boom persists into future periods even after shock ends

**Adverse temporary supply shock (recap from Chunk 003):**
A ↓ → MP_L ↓ → labour demand ↓ → real wage ↓ → labour supply ↓ → output ↓ → recession

### Examples
**Example: Effect of a positive technology shock (Fig. 12.2 in source).**
- Pre-shock: production function K^α·(A₀L)^(1−α); equilibrium at L₀, Y₀.
- Post-shock: production function K^α·(A₁L)^(1−α); same L₀ produces Y₁′ > Y₀; raising labour to L₁ produces Y₁ > Y₁′.
- Income distribution: part to consumption, part to saving → investment ↑ → future K ↑.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Temporary and permanent shocks have the same effect on consumption. → ✅ Correct: Permanent shocks raise consumption *more* (no smoothing needed); temporary shocks raise saving more.
- ❌ Mistake: A positive shock only shifts the production function up — slope is unchanged. → ✅ Correct: The production function also becomes *steeper* (marginal product of labour rises).
- ❌ Mistake: After the shock ends, the economy returns instantly to pre-shock state. → ✅ Correct: There is **persistence** — capital accumulated during the shock keeps output and employment elevated.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Positive shock: production function shifts up *and* gets steeper
> - Same L₀ → higher Y (Y₀ → Y₁′); more L (L₁) → even higher Y (Y₁)
> - Temporary shock ⇒ households save → future K ↑ → persistence
> - Permanent shock ⇒ households consume more, save less, smaller L change, but persistent ↑ in Y, K, employment
> - Keynesian criticism: persistence story can't fully explain real-world cycle length
> - RBC counter: dynamic responses of optimisers create sustained effects

## Section: New-Keynesian View on Business Cycle 🔴
<!-- Reason: Unit objective "discuss new-Keynesian approach"; Check Your Progress 3 Q2 -->

### Core Idea
**New-Keynesian macroeconomics** keeps Keynes' central insight (market imperfections, sticky wages and prices, activist government role) but addresses Lucas's two main criticisms by adding **microfoundation** and **rational expectations** — and adopting **DSGE models**. Methodologically it looks much like new-classical macro; the key differences are *imperfect competition* and *price/wage rigidities*, captured by price-setting and wage-setting equations with adjustment costs (e.g., menu costs), mark-ups, and a Taylor-rule-type interest-rate policy.

> **In Simple Terms:** New-Keynesians say: "We agree with the math (microfoundations, rational expectations, DSGE) but we don't agree that prices adjust instantly. Real businesses face menu costs, mark-up power, and sticky wages — once you put those in, government policy regains its bite."

### Key Concepts

#### Where new-Keynesian comes from
- **New-classical assumption:** continuous market clearing → perfect competition + flexible wages and prices → adjustments instantaneous (this also matched classical economics).
- **Keynes' departure:** rejected the above — emphasised market imperfections; advocated an *activist* role for government.
- **Lucas's two criticisms of Keynes:** (i) no microfoundation; (ii) does not take expectations about future prices into account.

**New-Keynesian fix:** include microfoundation and rational expectations *while retaining* market imperfections and price/wage rigidities → DSGE models with frictions.

#### Methodology vs assumptions
| Dimension | New-Classical | New-Keynesian |
|-----------|--------------|---------------|
| Microfoundation | Yes | Yes |
| Rational expectations | Yes | Yes |
| DSGE framework | Yes | Yes |
| Continuous market clearing | Yes (flexible prices/wages) | **No** — sticky prices/wages |
| Imperfect competition | No | **Yes** |
| Use of menu costs / mark-ups / Taylor rule | No (typically) | **Yes** |

So methodology is largely shared; the *key* contrast is in market clearing assumptions.

#### What new-Keynesian DSGE models include
To bring imperfections into DSGE, new-Keynesians add:
- **Price-setting and wage-setting equations** (rather than instantaneous adjustment).
- **Adjustment costs** such as **menu costs** in wage setting.
- **Mark-up in price setting** (a measure of market imperfection / imperfect competition).
- **Some form of Taylor Rule** for interest rate setting (a policy rule).

### Definitions
- **New-Keynesian economics**: A macroeconomic school that combines microfoundation and rational expectations with sticky prices/wages and imperfect competition; uses DSGE models with frictions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Menu costs**: Adjustment costs faced by firms in changing posted prices or wages — one micro reason for nominal rigidity. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mark-up (in price setting)**: A wedge between price and marginal cost reflecting imperfect competition; introduced into new-Keynesian DSGE to capture market imperfection. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Taylor Rule**: A policy rule for setting the central-bank interest rate, included in new-Keynesian DSGE models as the monetary-policy block. ⭐ (exam-important)

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: New-Keynesian and new-classical models are methodologically incompatible. → ✅ Correct: They share microfoundation, rational expectations, and DSGE structure; the difference lies in market clearing and imperfections.
- ❌ Mistake: New-Keynesian retains old Keynesian assumption that agents are non-optimising. → ✅ Correct: New-Keynesian agents *are* rational optimisers — but the markets they operate in have rigidities and imperfect competition.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - New-Keynesian fixes Lucas's criticisms while keeping Keynesian rigidities
> - Three retained Keynesian features: market imperfections, sticky prices, sticky wages
> - Three adopted new-classical tools: microfoundation, rational expectations, DSGE
> - DSGE add-ons: menu costs, mark-ups, Taylor rule
> - Difference from new-classical = market clearing assumption (NK rejects continuous clearing)

### Connections
- Contrasts with: New-Classical View on Business Cycle (Chunk 003: Introduction to Unit 12 (Real Business Cycles)) — same methodology, different market-clearing assumption.
- Contrasts with: Real Business Cycle theory (Chunk 003: Introduction to Unit 12 (Real Business Cycles)) — RBC is a strict-clearing branch of new-classical; NK is the rigidity-friendly response.

## Section: Unit 12 Summary 🟢

### Core Idea
RBC theory rests on microeconomic foundations: it analyses firm behaviour through production functions and household behaviour through consumption-leisure substitution, all within a general-equilibrium framework with rational expectations. New-Keynesian macroeconomics challenges RBC's continuous-market-clearing assumption and incorporates adjustment costs, staggered prices, and mark-up pricing into DSGE models.

### Key Concepts

#### What RBC is, in one paragraph
- Microfoundation: production function for firms; consumption function with labour-leisure substitution for households.
- Rational expectations: agents optimise with all available information.
- General equilibrium framework for empirical estimation.
- Source of cycles: real shocks, especially productivity.

#### What new-Keynesian adds
While adopting microfoundation, rational expectations, and the general-equilibrium framework, new-Keynesian economists incorporate:
- Adjustment costs
- Staggered prices
- Mark-up pricing
into DSGE (DGSE in source) models.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - RBC = microfoundation + rational expectations + GE + real shocks + market clearing
> - New-Keynesian = microfoundation + rational expectations + GE + market imperfections (adjustment costs, staggered prices, mark-ups)
