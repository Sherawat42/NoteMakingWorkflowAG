## Section: Introduction & Goals of Fiscal Policy 🟢

### Core Idea
Fiscal policy is the use of government taxation, transfer payments and government expenditure to manage aggregate demand and stabilise the economy. Classical economists believed the economy would self-correct, but the Great Depression of the 1930s showed no automatic mechanism worked, leading Keynes to argue for government intervention. Fiscal policy and monetary policy are the two main tools of macroeconomic policy.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of the economy as a car that sometimes overheats (inflation) or stalls (recession). Fiscal policy is the government using its spending pedal and tax brakes to keep the car cruising at the right speed.

### Key Concepts

#### Demand Management
Fiscal policy works as a demand-management tool: it influences aggregate demand (AD) through government expenditure, taxes and transfer payments. The objective is to keep output close to **potential output** and to reduce the size and duration of business-cycle fluctuations. Government uses it to offset shocks in autonomous consumption, investment and exports that would otherwise push the economy away from equilibrium.

#### Channels of Influence
Taxes and transfers act on AD **indirectly** through disposable income → consumption expenditure. Government expenditure acts **directly** on AD. The net tax rate also alters the size of the **expenditure multiplier** (Keynesian / spending multiplier), so changes in tax rates, transfer rates, and G shift the AD curve.

#### Government Budget & Budget Balance
The government budget describes goods and services purchased, transfer payments made, and how they will be financed. Three states:
- Revenues = spending → **balanced budget**
- Revenues > spending → **budget surplus**
- Revenues < spending → **budget deficit**

| Policy Action | Used To Cure | Effect on Budget |
|---------------|--------------|------------------|
| Expansionary (↑G or ↓T) | Recession | Deficit |
| Contractionary (↓G or ↑T) | Inflation | Surplus |

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Policy**: Government's use of taxes, transfer payments and expenditure on goods and services to manage aggregate demand. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Government Budget Balance (BB)**: BB = NT − G = (tY − G), where G = government purchases, NT = net tax revenue, t = tax rate, Y = output. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Budget Function**: Shows the different budget balances for one fiscal-policy programme at different levels of national income.
- **Expansionary Fiscal Policy**: ↑G and/or ↓T to cure recession.
- **Contractionary Fiscal Policy**: ↓G and/or ↑T to control inflation.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Three things determine the budget balance:
   1. Net tax rate **t** (set by government)
   2. Level of expenditure **G** (set by government)
   3. Level of output **Y** (determined by AE and AD)
2. Reading Fig. 18.1: At income Y₁ → BB = 0; if Y < Y₁ → deficit (BB < 0); if Y > Y₁ → surplus (BB > 0). Any change in fiscal plan → new BB line (new budget function).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - BB = tY − G
> - Tools of fiscal policy: taxes, transfers, government expenditure
> - Expansionary → cures recession → causes deficit
> - Contractionary → cures inflation → causes surplus

## Section: Discretionary Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
Discretionary fiscal policy is **deliberate** change in government expenditure and taxes by the government to influence national output and prices. It is non-mandatory, used in response to recession or economic turbulence. It uses spending and taxation to plug a recessionary gap (expansionary policy) or to close an inflationary gap (contractionary policy).

> **In Simple Terms:** Discretionary policy is the government actively deciding to step on the gas or the brake. It's not automatic — somebody has to make the call.

### Key Concepts

#### Curing Recession — Two Tools
A recession occurs when AD falls below the full-employment level due to a fall in investment, consumption or net exports, opening a **deflationary / recessionary gap**.

**(a) Increase in Government Expenditure (↑G)**
- Government undertakes public works (buys goods, employs labour).
- **Direct effect**: incomes of suppliers and workers rise; output of public works rises.
- **Indirect effect (multiplier)**: those earning extra income spend on consumer goods according to their MPC.
- Fig. 18.2: Initial AE₀ intersects 45° line at E₁, giving Y₀ < Y_f → deflationary gap Y₀Y_f. Increase in G by E₁H shifts AE₀ → AE₁; equilibrium moves to Y_f. The increase in output (Y₀Y_f) **exceeds** ΔG = E₁H due to the multiplier.

**(b) Reduction in Taxes (↓T)**
- Reduces tax burden → ↑disposable income → ↑consumption spending → upward shift of consumption function.
- A fall in tax rate also **increases the slope of AE** (raises the multiplier value).
- Fig. 18.3: AE₀ shifts to AE₁ (steeper), recessionary gap Y₀Y_f closes; equilibrium moves from E₁ to E₂ at Y_f.

#### Controlling Inflation — Two Tools
Excess demand can build inflationary pressures from: (i) rise in household consumption, (ii) rise in firm investment, (iii) rise in government expenditure, or from (iv) excessive money supply growth. Anti-cyclical policy reduces aggregate expenditure.

**(a) Reducing Government Expenditure (↓G)**
- Fig. 18.4: AE₀ intersects 45° line at E₁ giving Y₀ > Y_f → inflationary gap Y₀Y_f. ↓G by E₁H shifts AE₀ → AE₁; equilibrium returns to Y_f. Multiplier causes a **multiple decline** in output for a given cut in G.

**(b) Increase in Taxes (↑T)**
- Fig. 18.5: ↑t reduces disposable income → ↓consumption → reduces slope of AE → AE shifts down to AE₁ → equilibrium returns to Y_f.

#### Comparison: Expansionary vs Contractionary Discretionary Policy

| Aspect | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Used when | Recessionary / deflationary gap | Inflationary gap |
| Spending | ↑G | ↓G |
| Taxes | ↓T (or ↓t) | ↑T (or ↑t) |
| AE curve | Shifts up / steeper | Shifts down / flatter |
| Budget effect | Deficit | Surplus |
| Diagrams | Fig. 18.2, Fig. 18.3 | Fig. 18.4, Fig. 18.5 |

### Definitions
- **Discretionary Fiscal Policy**: Deliberate change in government expenditure and taxes to influence the level of national output and prices — non-mandatory changes by government in response to economic events. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Recessionary / Deflationary Gap**: The shortfall of equilibrium output below the full-employment level when AD is below the full-employment level. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Inflationary Gap**: Excess of equilibrium output (and AD) above the full-employment level, generating rising prices.

### Examples
**Example: Multiplier effect of higher G (Fig. 18.2)**
- Initial: AE₀ → equilibrium at E₁ → output Y₀ (< Y_f).
- Government spending ΔG = E₁H.
- AE shifts up to AE₁ → new equilibrium at Y_f.
- Output rises by Y₀Y_f, **greater than** ΔG, because of the multiplier.

**Example: Tax-rate cut (Fig. 18.3)**
- Initial: AE₀ → Y₀ < Y_f.
- Δt < 0 (cut in net tax rate) → slope of AE rises (steeper) → multiplier rises.
- AE rotates to AE₁ → equilibrium at Y_f.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking ΔY = ΔG (one-for-one) → ✅ Correct: ΔY > ΔG because of the multiplier.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating a tax-rate cut as merely an upward parallel shift of AE → ✅ Correct: A cut in t **also raises the slope** of AE (and hence the multiplier).
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing budget deficit with bad policy → ✅ Correct: A deficit is the natural by-product of expansionary fiscal policy in a recession.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Recession → ↑G or ↓T (expansionary) → AE curve shifts up
> - Inflation → ↓G or ↑T (contractionary) → AE curve shifts down
> - ΔY/ΔG > 1 due to multiplier
> - Tax-rate change alters slope of AE (changes the multiplier)

## Section: Time Lags in Discretionary Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
Stabilisation goals are hard to hit because the economy responds to policy with delays of unpredictable length. Economists distinguish two broad lags — the **inside lag** (between shock and policy action) and the **outside / response lag** (between action and effect on the economy). The inside lag is further split into recognition and implementation lags.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine adjusting the temperature on a giant oven that responds 20 minutes later. By the time the dial does its work, the food might be too hot or too cold. That mismatch is what time lags do to fiscal policy.

### Key Concepts

#### Inside Lag vs Outside Lag

| Lag | Definition | Source of Delay |
|-----|-----------|-----------------|
| Inside lag | Time between a shock and the policy action responding to it | Recognition + implementation |
| Outside / response lag | Time between policy action and its influence on the economy | Spending and income revisions take time; multiplier process is slow |

#### Three Components

1. **Recognition Lag** — Policymakers need data (much of it quarterly) to detect a boom or slump. If a slump starts on January 1, preliminary first-quarter data may only arrive at the end of April.
2. **Implementation Lag** — Even after diagnosis, putting the policy into effect takes time, especially for fiscal policy (legislative process, programme design).
3. **Response Lag** — Even after implementation, the multiplier takes time to reach its full value because households and firms do not revise spending plans instantly. In practice, a tax or G change takes about a year to have its full effect.

### Definitions
- **Inside Lag**: Time between a shock to the economy and the policy action responding to that shock. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Outside Lag (Response Lag)**: Time between a policy action and its influence on the economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Recognition Lag**: Delay before policymakers detect that a boom or slump has begun.
- **Implementation Lag**: Delay between recognising the problem and putting the corrective policy into effect.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Shock → (Recognition Lag) → diagnosis → (Implementation Lag) → policy enacted → (Response Lag) → full effect on output, employment, prices.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Conflating inside lag with outside lag → ✅ Correct: Inside = before action; outside = after action.
- ❌ Mistake: Believing fiscal action takes effect immediately → ✅ Correct: It typically takes ~1 year for a tax/spending change to have its full effect.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Lag length is **hard to predict**, which is itself a source of policy risk.
- Active stabilisation **can be destabilising** if the economy's condition has changed by the time the policy bites — e.g., stimulus arrives just as the economy is heating up.
- Active-policy advocates concede that lags require caution but argue against fully passive policy in severe and protracted downturns.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Inside lag = recognition + implementation
> - Outside lag = response lag (multiplier takes time)
> - Full fiscal effect ≈ 12 months
> - Lag-length unpredictability → risk of destabilising the economy

## Section: Non-Discretionary Fiscal Policy (Automatic Stabilisers) 🔴
<!-- Continues from Unit 18 in chunk 001 -->

### Core Idea
Non-discretionary fiscal policy refers to **built-in** tax and expenditure mechanisms that automatically raise aggregate demand during a recession and reduce it during inflation, without requiring any deliberate action by government or parliament. Because they are wired into the budget through the choice of tax structure and expenditure pattern, they operate continuously and side-step the inside lags that plague discretionary action.

> **In Simple Terms:** Automatic stabilisers are like cruise control on the economy — when speed (income) drops, taxes and welfare automatically lighten up; when speed climbs, they tighten. No driver intervention needed.

### Key Concepts

#### Income Taxes as Stabilisers
At given net tax rates, when Y falls in a recession, tax revenue falls automatically. This prevents AE from falling proportionately with Y, because disposable income falls by **less than** Y. The slope of the AE function — `c(1 − t) − m` — is lower with positive t, so the **multiplier is smaller**, dampening the downswing. In a boom, net tax revenue rises and disposable income rises by less than Y, dampening the upswing.

#### Transfer Payments as Stabilisers
- In recession: unemployment ↑ → government spends more on unemployment compensation, food stamps, rent subsidies, farm subsidies → cushions the fall in incomes.
- In boom: unemployment ↓ → social-benefit programmes shrink → curbs inflation.

#### Discretionary vs Non-Discretionary

| Aspect | Discretionary | Non-Discretionary (Automatic) |
|--------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| Trigger | Deliberate government decision | Built into tax/expenditure structure |
| Lags | Recognition + implementation + response | Mostly free of inside lag |
| Effect on cycle | Can offset autonomous shocks | Only **moderates** them — does not offset |
| Typical instruments | One-off ↑G or ↓T legislation | Net tax rate t; transfer programmes |
| Diagram (Fig. 18.6) | **Shifts** the BB function | Movement **along** BB₀ as Y fluctuates |

#### Reading Fig. 18.6
- Budget function: BB₀ = t₀Y − G₀.
- **Structural budget balance (SBB₀)** is measured at potential output Y_f (here SBB₀ = 0).
- Automatic stabilisation: as Y fluctuates between Y₁ and Y₂, BB moves **along** BB₀.
- Discretionary stabilisation: changes in G or t **shift** the BB function up or down.

### Definitions
- **Non-Discretionary Fiscal Policy / Automatic Stabilisers / Built-in Stabilisers**: Built-in tax or expenditure mechanism that automatically increases AD when recession occurs and reduces AD when there is inflation, without any deliberate government action. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Structural Budget Balance (SBB)**: Budget balance evaluated at potential output Y_f.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Recession: Y ↓ → tax revenue ↓ + transfer spending ↑ → disposable income falls less than Y → AE falls less → multiplier ↓ → recession dampened.
2. Boom: Y ↑ → tax revenue ↑ + transfer spending ↓ → disposable income rises less than Y → AE rises less → boom dampened.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing automatic stabilisers can fully offset autonomous shocks → ✅ Correct: They only **moderate** fluctuations; they do not offset shocks.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating an increase in t as automatic stabilisation → ✅ Correct: Changing t is **discretionary** (shifts BB); stabilisation occurs when Y changes at given t (movement along BB).

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Automatic stabilisers do **not** offset autonomous expenditure disturbances — they only damp them.
- Both policy types should be used **together**: automatic for cushioning shocks, discretionary for genuine offsets.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Automatic stabiliser slope: c(1 − t) − m → t ↑ ⇒ multiplier ↓
> - Movement along BB₀ = automatic; shift of BB = discretionary
> - Stabilisers **dampen** but do not **offset** the cycle
> - No inside lag for automatic stabilisers

- Solution to: Time Lags in Discretionary Fiscal Policy (Chunk 001: Introduction & Goals of Fiscal Policy)

## Section: Unit 18 — Let Us Sum Up 🟢

### Core Idea
Fiscal policy combines automatic and discretionary changes in taxation, public expenditure and borrowing to pursue growth, full employment, equity and stabilisation. Discretionary requires deliberate government action; non-discretionary triggers itself with the state of the economy. Time lags make discretionary policy difficult to control; automatic stabilisers escape these lags but only damp shocks. The policy lesson: **use both in tandem**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Goals: growth, full employment, equity, stabilisation
> - Discretionary: deliberate, lagged, can offset shocks
> - Automatic: instant, lag-free, only damps shocks
> - Best policy = use both together

## Section: Fiscal Sustainability — Concept & Solvency 🔴

### Core Idea
**Fiscal sustainability** is the government's ability to meet its current and future obligations, maintain its current policies, and satisfy its lifetime budget constraint without defaulting on its debt. It is a long-run solvency concept: a fiscal/monetary policy mix that, if maintained indefinitely, would lead to insolvency is unsustainable. Analysis focuses on the **policy changes needed to avoid eventual default**, not on default itself, because governments typically change course before defaulting.

> **In Simple Terms:** Fiscal sustainability is like asking: if a household keeps its current spending and earning habits forever, can it keep paying its credit-card bills? If not, the policy is unsustainable.

### Key Concepts

#### Why Sustainability Matters
- High and rising debt harms a government's fiscal position and can spawn a vicious cycle of growing debt that diverts funds from productive investment, reducing growth potential.
- Failure to **service** debt (repay principal + interest) = default.
- Governments typically change policy before insolvency materialises; sustainability analysis identifies how much adjustment is needed.

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Sustainability**: Government's ability to meet current and future obligations, maintain current policies, and satisfy its lifetime budget constraint without defaulting. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Default (on Debt)**: Government failure to service debt — to repay principal and accrued interest.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Sustainable ⇔ government solvent on indefinite policy horizon
> - High debt → vicious cycle → less growth
> - Default usually averted by **policy changes**, not actually realised

## Section: Government Budget Constraint & Budget Deficit 🔴

### Core Idea
The government budget constraint is an **identity** that links the change in government debt to the inherited stock of debt (which generates interest payments) and to current spending and taxes. The official deficit — annual expenditure minus tax/non-tax receipts — is the main indicator of fiscal imbalance. Decomposing it into interest payments and the primary surplus is the key analytical step.

### Key Concepts

#### The Deficit Equation (Real Terms)
**Budget Deficit_t = rB_{t-1} + G_t − T_t**  …(19.1)
- B_{t-1} = government debt at end of t − 1 (= start of year t)
- r = real interest rate (assumed constant)
- rB_{t-1} = real interest payments on existing debt
- G_t = government purchases of goods and services in year t
- T_t = taxes minus transfers in year t

#### Debt Accumulation Identity
**B_t − B_{t-1} = rB_{t-1} + G_t − T_t**  …(19.2)
**B_t − B_{t-1} = rB_{t-1} − X_t**  …(19.2a)

Equivalent stock form: **B_t = (1 + r)B_{t-1} − X_t**  …(19.2b)

Where X_t = primary balance = T_t − G_t.

- Government runs a deficit → debt **increases**.
- Government runs a surplus → debt **decreases**.

#### Decomposition of the Deficit
| Component | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| rB_{t-1} | Interest payment on existing debt |
| (T_t − G_t) | Primary surplus (a deficit if negative) |

Intuition: Debt at end of t = (debt at t−1) + (interest on it) − (primary surplus in t).

### Definitions
- **Government Budget Constraint**: Identity linking the change in government debt to the inherited debt stock (and its interest payments) and to current G and T. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Primary Balance / Primary Surplus**: X_t = T_t − G_t — the budget balance excluding interest payments. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Annual Official Deficit**: Annual expenditure (G + transfers + interest) minus tax and non-tax receipts.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Existing debt B_{t-1} → interest accrual rB_{t-1} → adds to deficit → adds to next-period debt B_t → larger interest bill → snowball.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Deficit_t = rB_{t-1} + G_t − T_t
> - B_t = (1 + r)B_{t-1} − X_t
> - X_t (primary surplus) = T_t − G_t
> - Deficit ⇒ debt rises; surplus ⇒ debt falls

## Section: Current vs Future Taxes — Repaying & Stabilising Debt 🔴

### Core Idea
A one-period tax cut financed by debt **must eventually** be paid back through higher taxes (or lower spending). The longer the government waits, or the higher the interest rate, the larger the eventual fiscal correction must be. If instead the government merely stabilises the debt, it must run a primary surplus equal to the interest bill **forever**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Borrowing today is just borrowing from your future self at interest. The longer you delay paying it back, the bigger the bill grows.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup
Until year 1 the government has a balanced budget, so initial debt = 0. In year 1, the government **cuts taxes by 1** for one year, so debt at the end of year 1 is **B₁ = 1**. Three scenarios follow.

#### 19.3.1 Full Repayment in Year 2
Budget constraint year 2: **B₂ = (1 + r)B₁ + G₂ − T₂**  …(19.3)
With B₂ = 0 and B₁ = 1:
**T₂ − G₂ = (1 + r)**  …(19.4)

The government must run a primary surplus of (1 + r) in year 2 to fully repay. Adjustment via taxes ⇒ the year-1 tax cut of 1 is offset by a year-2 tax hike of (1 + r).

#### 19.3.2 Full Repayment in Year t
If the government keeps the primary deficit at zero from year 2 to year t−1 and only repays in year t, then debt grows at rate r:
- B₂ = (1 + r)¹
- B₃ = (1 + r)²
- B_{t-1} = (1 + r)^{t-2}  …(19.7)

Year-t budget constraint: B_t = (1 + r)B_{t-1} + (G_t − T_t)  …(19.8)
Setting B_t = 0:
**T_t − G_t = (1 + r)^{t-1}**  …(19.10)

**Two key conclusions:**
1. If government spending is unchanged, a tax cut **must eventually** be offset by a tax hike.
2. The longer the government waits, or the higher the real interest rate, the **larger** the eventual tax hike must be.

#### 19.3.3 Debt Stabilisation in Year t
Stabilising debt means keeping it constant from a given year onwards (B_t = B_{t-1}). Setting B₂ = B₁ = 1 in (19.3):
1 = (1 + r) + G₂ − T₂  …(19.11)
**T₂ − G₂ = r**  …(19.12)

So to stabilise the debt, the government must **eliminate the deficit** by running a primary surplus equal to the **interest payments on existing debt**, and taxes must be permanently higher by r every year from year 1 on.

**Two stabilisation conclusions:**
1. To stabilise the debt, the deficit must be eliminated.
2. To eliminate the deficit, the primary surplus must equal interest payments on existing debt — requiring **permanently higher taxes**.

### Definitions
- **Debt Stabilisation**: Changing taxes or spending so that debt remains constant from a given period onwards.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Repayment in year 2** → required surplus = (1 + r) → one-shot.
**Repayment in year t** → required surplus = (1 + r)^{t-1} → grows with delay.
**Stabilisation from year 2 on** → required surplus = r → forever.

### Examples
**Example: One-year tax cut, repaid in year 5**
- Year 1: ΔT = −1 → B₁ = 1.
- Years 2–4: primary deficit = 0 → B grows at r → B₄ = (1 + r)³.
- Year 5: T₅ − G₅ = (1 + r)⁴ to repay the debt fully.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking debt vanishes if you just keep the primary deficit at zero → ✅ Correct: With positive existing debt, interest still accrues; debt grows at rate r unless a primary surplus pays interest.
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing **debt repayment** (B → 0) with **debt stabilisation** (B constant) → ✅ Correct: They demand different surpluses — (1 + r)^{t-1} once vs r every year.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Repay in year 2: surplus = (1 + r)
> - Repay in year t: surplus = (1 + r)^{t-1}
> - Stabilise debt: surplus = r forever
> - Delay → higher eventual tax bill

## Section: Debt-GDP Ratio Dynamics 🔴

### Core Idea
In a growing economy, the relevant indicator is the **debt-to-GDP ratio**, not the level of debt. The change in this ratio is driven by the gap between the real interest rate and growth (r − g) times the existing ratio, plus the primary deficit relative to GDP. The ratio rises faster when r is high, g is low, the existing ratio is high, or the primary deficit is large.

> **In Simple Terms:** Worry about debt **relative to your income**, not in absolute rupees. If income grows faster than the debt's interest rate, debt becomes lighter even if the principal is unchanged.

### Key Concepts

#### Derivation
Start: B_t = (1 + r)B_{t-1} + G_t − T_t  …(19.2b)
Divide by Y_t: (B_t/Y_t) = (1 + r)(B_{t-1}/Y_t) + (G_t − T_t)/Y_t  …(19.13)
Rewrite B_{t-1}/Y_t as (B_{t-1}/Y_{t-1})(Y_{t-1}/Y_t):
(B_t/Y_t) = (1 + r)(Y_{t-1}/Y_t)(B_{t-1}/Y_{t-1}) + (G_t − T_t)/Y_t  …(19.14)

With output growth g, Y_{t-1}/Y_t = 1/(1 + g) and approximation (1 + r)/(1 + g) ≈ 1 + r − g:
(B_t/Y_t) = (1 + r − g)(B_{t-1}/Y_{t-1}) + (G_t − T_t)/Y_t  …(19.15)

Reorganise to get the **debt-dynamics equation**:
**(B_t/Y_t) − (B_{t-1}/Y_{t-1}) = (r − g)(B_{t-1}/Y_{t-1}) + (G_t − T_t)/Y_t**  …(19.16)

#### Drivers of the Debt Ratio
The change in the debt ratio = (interest-growth differential × existing ratio) + (primary deficit / GDP). The ratio grows **faster** when:
1. The real interest rate r is **higher**.
2. The growth rate of output g is **lower** (so r − g rises).
3. The existing debt ratio is **higher**.
4. The ratio of primary deficit to GDP is **higher**.

| Variable ↑ | Effect on Δ(B/Y) |
|------------|------------------|
| r | ↑ |
| g | ↓ |
| existing B/Y | ↑ |
| primary deficit / Y | ↑ |

### Definitions
- **Debt-GDP Ratio**: B_t / Y_t, the stock of debt scaled by output.
- **Interest-Growth Differential**: r − g. When negative, the debt ratio shrinks for a given primary balance.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking that a positive primary surplus always reduces the debt ratio → ✅ Correct: It depends on (r − g). If (r − g)(B/Y) exceeds the surplus, the ratio still rises.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Δ(B/Y) = (r − g)(B/Y)_{t-1} + (G − T)/Y
> - r ↑, g ↓, B/Y_initial ↑, primary deficit ↑ ⇒ B/Y rises faster
> - r < g + primary surplus ⇒ debt ratio falls

## Section: The Dangers of High Debt 🔴
<!-- Continues from Debt-GDP Ratio Dynamics in chunk 002 -->

### Core Idea
A high debt ratio is dangerous because it can launch a self-reinforcing **debt-explosion** spiral: investors demand higher interest rates to compensate for default risk → bigger primary surplus required to stabilise debt → politically costly fiscal contraction → recession → lower growth → still larger required surplus → eventual loss of control. The remedy is to aim at **decreasing** the debt ratio over time through a combination of primary surpluses, high growth and low real interest rates.

> **In Simple Terms:** High debt is like balancing a tall stack of plates — small wobbles (a rate hike, a slow year) can topple the lot. Bring the stack down before it falls on its own.

### Key Concepts

#### Worked Numerical Example (from §19.4)
Equation: Δ(B/Y) = (r − g)(B/Y)_{t-1} + (G − T)/Y  …(19.16)

| Scenario | r | g | B/Y | Required Primary Surplus | Verification |
|----------|---|---|-----|--------------------------|--------------|
| Initial | 3% | 2% | 100% | 1% of GDP | (3 − 2) · 100 + (−1) = 0 |
| Investors panic — r rises to 8% | 8% | 2% | 100% | 6% of GDP | (8 − 2) · 100 + (−6) = 0 |

The required primary surplus jumps from 1% → 6% of GDP just to keep B/Y constant.

#### The Debt Explosion Cycle
1. Investors fear default → demand higher r.
2. Higher r → larger surplus needed to stabilise debt.
3. Larger surplus → painful spending cuts / tax hikes → political uncertainty, more default risk → r rises again.
4. Sharp fiscal contraction → recession → growth g falls → (r − g) widens → surplus required is **even larger**.
5. At some point, government cannot raise the surplus enough → B/Y rises → markets demand higher r → debt explosion.

#### Policy Lesson
When inheriting a high debt ratio, the goal must be to **decrease it over time** via:
- Primary surpluses
- High growth rates
- Low real interest rates

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Stabilising debt at any level is enough → ✅ Correct: Stabilising at a high level still leaves the economy fragile to interest-rate shocks; aim to bring B/Y **down**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Required primary surplus to stabilise B/Y = (r − g)(B/Y)
> - r rises by 5pp → required surplus rises by 5pp of GDP (at B/Y = 100%)
> - Vicious cycle: ↑r → ↑surplus → recession → ↑(r − g) → ↑surplus …

## Section: Debt Default 🟡

### Core Idea
When facing very high interest rates, a government may **default** on its debt. Default is often partial, with creditors taking a "haircut". Default looks superficially attractive — it shrinks required fiscal consolidation and lowers required taxes — but it carries severe costs: pension funds suffer, banks may go bankrupt, and access to foreign borrowing can be lost for a long time. Hence, governments are rightly very reluctant to default.

### Key Concepts

#### Haircut
A 30% haircut means creditors receive only 70% of what they were owed.

#### Costs of Default
| Holder of Debt | Consequence |
|----------------|-------------|
| Pension funds | Retirees suffer reduced pensions |
| Banks | Risk of bankruptcy → broader financial damage |
| Foreigners | Loss of reputation; difficulty borrowing abroad |

### Definitions
- **Debt Default**: Government failure to repay principal and accrued interest on its debt.
- **Haircut**: The percentage reduction creditors accept on their claim during a partial default. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Default = partial or full non-repayment
> - Haircut x% → creditors receive (100 − x)%
> - Costs: bank failures, lost foreign-borrowing access, hit to retirees

## Section: Lessons from the Past — Capital Flight & Sovereign-Debt Crises 🟡

### Core Idea
High government debt invites **capital flight** — abrupt withdrawal of foreign demand for a country's assets — which can spark currency crises and sovereign-debt crises. History records repeated examples, from medieval England through 1980s Latin America, 1998 Russia and the 2010-era European sovereign-debt crisis.

### Key Concepts

#### Historical Episodes Listed in the Source
| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1335 | England's King Edward III defaults on debt to Italian bankers |
| 1980s | Several Latin American countries default |
| 1998 | Russia defaults |
| Early 1990s | Mexico — default appeared likely; capital-flight pressure |
| 2009 | Greece reveals it had under-reported debt for years |
| 2007–08 | Ireland bails out its entire banking system |
| 2007–10 | Irish debt-GDP rises from **25% → ~100%**; fiscal deficit from **0% → >30%** of GDP |
| Fall 2008 → Jan 2012 | Yield spread between German and Greek debt rises by **33 percentage points** |
| Greek banks held ~20% of Greek sovereign debt | Loss of confidence → rollover crisis |
| Since 2010 | EU + IMF extend > **€1 trillion** in loan packages; ECB injects up to €1 trillion of liquidity |

#### Mechanism of Capital Flight
High debt → fear of default → international investors withdraw → currency crisis. Sudden loss of confidence is the key trigger — it converts a slow-burn fiscal problem into an acute crisis.

### Definitions
- **Capital Flight**: An abrupt decline in the demand for a country's assets in world financial markets. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sovereign-Debt Crisis**: Investor loss of confidence in a sovereign's debt, triggering rate hikes and rollover difficulties.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - High debt + fear of default → capital flight → currency crisis
> - Greece 2009: hidden debt revealed → trigger
> - Ireland 2007–10: B/Y 25 → ~100; deficit 0 → >30% GDP
> - Greek-German yield spread: +33 pp by Jan 2012

## Section: Money Finance (Seignorage and the Limits of Debt Monetisation) 🔴

### Core Idea
When deficits are large, the debt ratio is high and interest rates are punitive, governments are tempted to finance themselves by **money creation** — issuing new bonds bought by the central bank, which prints money in exchange. This is **debt monetisation**, and the resulting real revenue is called **seignorage**. But seignorage has a self-defeating dynamic: high money growth drives inflation, which reduces real money demand, which forces still higher money growth, which drives **hyperinflation**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Printing money to pay your bills works at first, but the more you print, the less people want to hold the cash. So you have to print even faster — until the currency becomes worthless.

### Key Concepts

#### Mechanism of Debt Monetisation
1. Government issues new bonds.
2. Government instructs the central bank to **buy** them.
3. Central bank pays the government with newly created money.
4. Government uses that money to finance the deficit.

#### Seignorage Formula
Let H = stock of central-bank money; ΔH = change in nominal money stock.

**Seignorage = ΔH / P**  …(19.17)

Decompose: ΔH/P = (ΔH/H)(H/P)  …(19.18)

So: **Seignorage = (ΔH / H) × (H / P)**  …(19.19) — seignorage is the **product** of nominal money growth and the real money stock.

Dividing by monthly GDP Y₁:
**Seignorage / Y₁ = (ΔH/H) × ((H/P) / Y₁)**  …(19.20)

#### Numerical Example
Government wants to finance a deficit of **10% of GDP** with H/(P·Y₁) = 1.
Then (ΔH/H) × 1 = 10% ⇒ **ΔH/H = 10% per month**.

But: high money growth → inflation → people **reduce** their demand for money → H/P falls → government must raise ΔH/H even further to get the same seignorage → still more inflation → **hyperinflation**.

#### Real-World Reference
- Venezuela has experienced inflation in the range of **300,000% per year**.

### Definitions
- **Debt Monetisation**: Government bond issuance financed by central-bank money creation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Seignorage**: Real revenue the government generates by creating new money — ΔH/P. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Hyperinflation**: Extreme rates of inflation that emerge when seignorage-financing spirals out of control.

### Mechanisms / Processes
↑ΔH/H → ↑inflation → ↓real money demand (H/P) → revenue (ΔH/H × H/P) falls → ↑ΔH/H further → ↑inflation further → hyperinflation → eventually government forced to stop, but only after substantial costs.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing that sustained high money growth steadily yields more revenue → ✅ Correct: Real money demand collapses with inflation, so seignorage runs into a self-defeating limit.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Seignorage = ΔH/P = (ΔH/H)(H/P)
> - To finance 10% deficit when H/(P·Y₁) = 1 ⇒ need ΔH/H = 10%/month
> - High money growth ⇒ ↓H/P ⇒ need still higher ΔH/H ⇒ hyperinflation
> - Venezuela: inflation up to ~300,000%/year

## Section: Effects of a Decrease in Budget Deficit 🔴

### Core Idea
A decrease in the budget deficit (e.g., G falls from G to G′ with T unchanged) is **contractionary in the short run** but **neutral on output and beneficial for investment in the medium run**. In the short run, AD shifts left → output and prices fall. In the medium run, the price level keeps falling, real money supply expands, the LM curve shifts down, output returns to its natural level Y_n, and the interest rate ends up **lower** — boosting investment.

> **In Simple Terms:** Deficit reduction is bitter medicine: it hurts output today, but in the long run it leaves the economy with the same job count and **cheaper** borrowing — better for long-term investment.

### Key Concepts

#### Short-Run Adjustment (AS-AD, Fig. 19.1 panel a)
- Start at A: Y = Y_n, price level P.
- ↓G shifts AD curve **left** to AD′.
- For given price level, equilibrium moves to **A′**: Y falls to Y′ and price falls to P′.
- Output is below natural output → AS curve will start shifting down.

#### Medium-Run Adjustment (AS-AD, Fig. 19.1 panel a)
- AS curve shifts down progressively (because Y < Y_n) until it reaches AS″.
- Economy reaches **A″**: output back at Y_n, but at a **lower** price level P″.

#### IS-LM Story (Fig. 19.1 panel b)
- Initial equilibrium A: IS ∩ LM at output Y_n, interest rate i.
- Deficit reduction shifts IS left to IS′. With unchanged P, would move to B (lower Y, lower i).
- But P falls → real money stock M/P rises → LM shifts down to LM′.
- Net short-run move: A → A′ (lower Y and lower i).
- As long as Y < Y_n, P keeps falling → LM keeps shifting down along IS′.
- Eventually reaches A″: Y back at Y_n, interest rate **i″ < i**.

#### Comparison: Short Run vs Medium Run

| Variable | Short Run | Medium Run |
|----------|-----------|------------|
| Output Y | Falls (Y_n → Y′) | Returns to Y_n |
| Price level | Falls (P → P′) | Falls further (P → P″) |
| Interest rate | Falls (i → i′) | Falls further (i′ → i″) |
| Investment | Higher (lower i) | Even higher (still lower i) |

#### Key Conclusion
In the medium run, deficit reduction **unambiguously**:
1. Returns output to its natural level Y_n.
2. **Lowers** the real interest rate (i ↓).
3. **Increases** investment (because i ↓).

### Definitions
- **Natural Level of Output (Y_n)**: Output level the economy returns to in the medium run.

### Mechanisms / Processes
↓G → AD shifts left → short-run ↓Y, ↓P → AS shifts down → medium-run Y → Y_n, P further down → real M/P rises → LM shifts down → i further down → I rises.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating deficit reduction as harmful in the long run because of the short-run recession → ✅ Correct: In the medium run, output recovers to Y_n and the lower interest rate **boosts investment**.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Adjustment depends on prices being able to fall — sticky prices delay the medium-run gains.
- The recession can be politically destabilising, complicating the path to medium-run equilibrium.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Short run: ↓G → ↓Y, ↓P, ↓i
> - Medium run: Y back to Y_n, but i still lower → I higher
> - Mechanism: ↓P → ↑M/P → LM shifts down → ↓i

## Section: Unit 19 — Let Us Sum Up & Sustainability Roadmap 🟢

### Core Idea
Fiscal sustainability is the ability of the government to meet current and future obligations — the long-run "solvency" of public finances. The issue gained special importance after the global financial crisis. A sustainable policy is one where the **debt-to-GDP ratio is ultimately stable or declining**. Practical levers include: reducing tax-system distortions and waste, improving tax administration, broadening the tax base, eliminating wasteful expenditure, and providing public goods efficiently.

### Key Concepts

#### Pillars of Sustainable Fiscal Policy
1. Reduce distortions and waste in the tax system.
2. Improve tax administration.
3. Broaden the tax base.
4. Eliminate wasteful government expenditure.
5. Provide public goods and services efficiently.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Sustainable ⇔ B/Y ultimately stable or falling
> - Levers: tax base ↑, tax distortions ↓, waste ↓, public-goods efficiency ↑
> - Sustainability rose to prominence after the global financial crisis
