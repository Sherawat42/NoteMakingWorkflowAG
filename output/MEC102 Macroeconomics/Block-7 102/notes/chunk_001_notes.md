## Section: Introduction & Goals of Fiscal Policy 🟢

### Core Idea
Fiscal policy is the use of government taxation, transfer payments and government expenditure to manage aggregate demand and stabilise the economy. Classical economists believed the economy would self-correct, but the Great Depression of the 1930s showed no automatic mechanism worked, leading Keynes to argue for government intervention. Fiscal policy and monetary policy are the two main tools of macroeconomic policy.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of the economy as a car that sometimes overheats (inflation) or stalls (recession). Fiscal policy is the government using its spending pedal and tax brakes to keep the car cruising at the right speed.

### Key Concepts

#### Demand Management
Fiscal policy works as a demand-management tool: it influences aggregate demand (AD) through government expenditure, taxes and transfer payments. The objective is to keep output close to **potential output** and to reduce the size and duration of business-cycle fluctuations. Government uses it to offset shocks in autonomous consumption, investment and exports that would otherwise push the economy away from equilibrium.

#### Channels of Influence
Taxes and transfers act on AD **indirectly** through disposable income → consumption expenditure. Government expenditure acts **directly** on AD. The net tax rate also alters the size of the **expenditure multiplier** (Keynesian / spending multiplier), so changes in tax rates, transfer rates, and G shift the AD curve.

#### Government Budget & Budget Balance
The government budget describes goods and services purchased, transfer payments made, and how they will be financed. Three states:
- Revenues = spending → **balanced budget**
- Revenues > spending → **budget surplus**
- Revenues < spending → **budget deficit**

| Policy Action | Used To Cure | Effect on Budget |
|---------------|--------------|------------------|
| Expansionary (↑G or ↓T) | Recession | Deficit |
| Contractionary (↓G or ↑T) | Inflation | Surplus |

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Policy**: Government's use of taxes, transfer payments and expenditure on goods and services to manage aggregate demand. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Government Budget Balance (BB)**: BB = NT − G = (tY − G), where G = government purchases, NT = net tax revenue, t = tax rate, Y = output. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Budget Function**: Shows the different budget balances for one fiscal-policy programme at different levels of national income.
- **Expansionary Fiscal Policy**: ↑G and/or ↓T to cure recession.
- **Contractionary Fiscal Policy**: ↓G and/or ↑T to control inflation.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Three things determine the budget balance:
   1. Net tax rate **t** (set by government)
   2. Level of expenditure **G** (set by government)
   3. Level of output **Y** (determined by AE and AD)
2. Reading Fig. 18.1: At income Y₁ → BB = 0; if Y < Y₁ → deficit (BB < 0); if Y > Y₁ → surplus (BB > 0). Any change in fiscal plan → new BB line (new budget function).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - BB = tY − G
> - Tools of fiscal policy: taxes, transfers, government expenditure
> - Expansionary → cures recession → causes deficit
> - Contractionary → cures inflation → causes surplus

## Section: Discretionary Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
Discretionary fiscal policy is **deliberate** change in government expenditure and taxes by the government to influence national output and prices. It is non-mandatory, used in response to recession or economic turbulence. It uses spending and taxation to plug a recessionary gap (expansionary policy) or to close an inflationary gap (contractionary policy).

> **In Simple Terms:** Discretionary policy is the government actively deciding to step on the gas or the brake. It's not automatic — somebody has to make the call.

### Key Concepts

#### Curing Recession — Two Tools
A recession occurs when AD falls below the full-employment level due to a fall in investment, consumption or net exports, opening a **deflationary / recessionary gap**.

**(a) Increase in Government Expenditure (↑G)**
- Government undertakes public works (buys goods, employs labour).
- **Direct effect**: incomes of suppliers and workers rise; output of public works rises.
- **Indirect effect (multiplier)**: those earning extra income spend on consumer goods according to their MPC.
- Fig. 18.2: Initial AE₀ intersects 45° line at E₁, giving Y₀ < Y_f → deflationary gap Y₀Y_f. Increase in G by E₁H shifts AE₀ → AE₁; equilibrium moves to Y_f. The increase in output (Y₀Y_f) **exceeds** ΔG = E₁H due to the multiplier.

**(b) Reduction in Taxes (↓T)**
- Reduces tax burden → ↑disposable income → ↑consumption spending → upward shift of consumption function.
- A fall in tax rate also **increases the slope of AE** (raises the multiplier value).
- Fig. 18.3: AE₀ shifts to AE₁ (steeper), recessionary gap Y₀Y_f closes; equilibrium moves from E₁ to E₂ at Y_f.

#### Controlling Inflation — Two Tools
Excess demand can build inflationary pressures from: (i) rise in household consumption, (ii) rise in firm investment, (iii) rise in government expenditure, or from (iv) excessive money supply growth. Anti-cyclical policy reduces aggregate expenditure.

**(a) Reducing Government Expenditure (↓G)**
- Fig. 18.4: AE₀ intersects 45° line at E₁ giving Y₀ > Y_f → inflationary gap Y₀Y_f. ↓G by E₁H shifts AE₀ → AE₁; equilibrium returns to Y_f. Multiplier causes a **multiple decline** in output for a given cut in G.

**(b) Increase in Taxes (↑T)**
- Fig. 18.5: ↑t reduces disposable income → ↓consumption → reduces slope of AE → AE shifts down to AE₁ → equilibrium returns to Y_f.

#### Comparison: Expansionary vs Contractionary Discretionary Policy

| Aspect | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Used when | Recessionary / deflationary gap | Inflationary gap |
| Spending | ↑G | ↓G |
| Taxes | ↓T (or ↓t) | ↑T (or ↑t) |
| AE curve | Shifts up / steeper | Shifts down / flatter |
| Budget effect | Deficit | Surplus |
| Diagrams | Fig. 18.2, Fig. 18.3 | Fig. 18.4, Fig. 18.5 |

### Definitions
- **Discretionary Fiscal Policy**: Deliberate change in government expenditure and taxes to influence the level of national output and prices — non-mandatory changes by government in response to economic events. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Recessionary / Deflationary Gap**: The shortfall of equilibrium output below the full-employment level when AD is below the full-employment level. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Inflationary Gap**: Excess of equilibrium output (and AD) above the full-employment level, generating rising prices.

### Examples
**Example: Multiplier effect of higher G (Fig. 18.2)**
- Initial: AE₀ → equilibrium at E₁ → output Y₀ (< Y_f).
- Government spending ΔG = E₁H.
- AE shifts up to AE₁ → new equilibrium at Y_f.
- Output rises by Y₀Y_f, **greater than** ΔG, because of the multiplier.

**Example: Tax-rate cut (Fig. 18.3)**
- Initial: AE₀ → Y₀ < Y_f.
- Δt < 0 (cut in net tax rate) → slope of AE rises (steeper) → multiplier rises.
- AE rotates to AE₁ → equilibrium at Y_f.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking ΔY = ΔG (one-for-one) → ✅ Correct: ΔY > ΔG because of the multiplier.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating a tax-rate cut as merely an upward parallel shift of AE → ✅ Correct: A cut in t **also raises the slope** of AE (and hence the multiplier).
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing budget deficit with bad policy → ✅ Correct: A deficit is the natural by-product of expansionary fiscal policy in a recession.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Recession → ↑G or ↓T (expansionary) → AE curve shifts up
> - Inflation → ↓G or ↑T (contractionary) → AE curve shifts down
> - ΔY/ΔG > 1 due to multiplier
> - Tax-rate change alters slope of AE (changes the multiplier)

## Section: Time Lags in Discretionary Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
Stabilisation goals are hard to hit because the economy responds to policy with delays of unpredictable length. Economists distinguish two broad lags — the **inside lag** (between shock and policy action) and the **outside / response lag** (between action and effect on the economy). The inside lag is further split into recognition and implementation lags.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine adjusting the temperature on a giant oven that responds 20 minutes later. By the time the dial does its work, the food might be too hot or too cold. That mismatch is what time lags do to fiscal policy.

### Key Concepts

#### Inside Lag vs Outside Lag

| Lag | Definition | Source of Delay |
|-----|-----------|-----------------|
| Inside lag | Time between a shock and the policy action responding to it | Recognition + implementation |
| Outside / response lag | Time between policy action and its influence on the economy | Spending and income revisions take time; multiplier process is slow |

#### Three Components

1. **Recognition Lag** — Policymakers need data (much of it quarterly) to detect a boom or slump. If a slump starts on January 1, preliminary first-quarter data may only arrive at the end of April.
2. **Implementation Lag** — Even after diagnosis, putting the policy into effect takes time, especially for fiscal policy (legislative process, programme design).
3. **Response Lag** — Even after implementation, the multiplier takes time to reach its full value because households and firms do not revise spending plans instantly. In practice, a tax or G change takes about a year to have its full effect.

### Definitions
- **Inside Lag**: Time between a shock to the economy and the policy action responding to that shock. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Outside Lag (Response Lag)**: Time between a policy action and its influence on the economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Recognition Lag**: Delay before policymakers detect that a boom or slump has begun.
- **Implementation Lag**: Delay between recognising the problem and putting the corrective policy into effect.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Shock → (Recognition Lag) → diagnosis → (Implementation Lag) → policy enacted → (Response Lag) → full effect on output, employment, prices.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Conflating inside lag with outside lag → ✅ Correct: Inside = before action; outside = after action.
- ❌ Mistake: Believing fiscal action takes effect immediately → ✅ Correct: It typically takes ~1 year for a tax/spending change to have its full effect.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Lag length is **hard to predict**, which is itself a source of policy risk.
- Active stabilisation **can be destabilising** if the economy's condition has changed by the time the policy bites — e.g., stimulus arrives just as the economy is heating up.
- Active-policy advocates concede that lags require caution but argue against fully passive policy in severe and protracted downturns.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Inside lag = recognition + implementation
> - Outside lag = response lag (multiplier takes time)
> - Full fiscal effect ≈ 12 months
> - Lag-length unpredictability → risk of destabilising the economy
