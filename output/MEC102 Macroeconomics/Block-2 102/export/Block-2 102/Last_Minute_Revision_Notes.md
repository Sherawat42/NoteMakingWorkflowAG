## Topics

- **Inflation**: persistent rise, or tendency towards persistent rise, in the general level of prices. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Unemployment**: state in which a healthy person fails to get employment at the prevailing wage. ⭐ (exam-important)

**Quick Recall:**
- Classical view: monetary–real **dichotomy**; inflation cannot affect unemployment.
- Keynesian view: monetary changes DO affect real variables.
- Three broad inflation types: demand-pull, cost-push, built-in.

### Types of Unemployment 🔴

**Labour force vs. work force**
- **Labour force** — all persons aged **16–64** willing to work. Includes both employed *and* unemployed. Excludes retirees, those too ill to work, homemakers, and those not looking for work.
- **Work force** — narrower; only the **employed**.
- **Unemployed = Labour force − Work force**.

**Three types of unemployment**
| Type | Cause | Example / Mechanism |
|------|-------|---------------------|
| **Frictional** | Imperfect information; switching jobs; migration; matching frictions | Worker between two jobs; tenure ends and search begins |
| **Structural** | Mismatch between supply of and demand for skills | Surplus of engineers/management graduates in India taking low-qualification jobs; institutions discontinuing engineering programmes |
| **Cyclical** | Fluctuations in aggregate demand (business cycle) | AD ↓ → labour demand ↓ → unemployment ↑ ; pro-cyclical with the cycle |

**Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR)**
- Varies across countries and over time.
- Splits by gender into male LFPR and female LFPR; usually a gap.
- In India, female LFPR is much lower than male LFPR and has been declining recently due to **cultural and structural** reasons.

**Unemployment rate**
- **Labour force**: all persons aged 16–64 willing to work; includes both employed and unemployed. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Work force**: the employed persons only.
- **Involuntary unemployment**: a person is willing to work at the prevailing wage rate but cannot find work. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Frictional unemployment**: arises because workers switch jobs; reflects search/matching frictions in an economy with imperfect information.
- **Structural unemployment**: results from mismatch between supply and demand for different kinds of jobs; takes time to adjust to structural shifts.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: arises from fluctuations in aggregate demand; pro-cyclical with the business cycle.
- **Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR)**: ratio of labour force size to population aged 16–64. ⭐ (exam-important)
- India's labour force ≈ **50 crore** vs. estimated population of **138 crore** in 2020.
- Persons above 65 and children below 15 should be excluded when comparing labour force to total population.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: treating homemakers as "unemployed" → ✅ Correct: they're not in the labour force at all.
- ❌ Mistake: assuming unemployment rate uses total population in the denominator → ✅ Correct: denominator is the **labour force**, not population.

**Quick Recall:**
- Labour force − Work force = Unemployed.
- Three types: **Frictional, Structural, Cyclical** (mnemonic: F-S-C).
- LFPR uses ages **16–64** in the denominator.
- Cyclical unemployment is **pro-cyclical**: rises in recession, falls in boom.

### The Phillips Curve 🔴

**Phillips' original equation**
- $\dot{w}$ = rate of wage increase
- $a, b$ = constants ($b > 0$)
- $u$ = rate of unemployment

**From wage growth to inflation**

**Policy interpretation (1960s–early 1970s)**
- **High unemployment** → expansionary monetary policy → more money in hands → inflation rises, unemployment falls.
- **High inflation** → contractionary policy → inflation falls, unemployment rises.
- Conclusion: inflation–unemployment **trade-off** is a policy menu; the government picks any point on the curve.
- This **justifies discretionary stabilization policy**.

**The 1970s breakdown — stagflation**
- During the **late 1970s–early 1980s** the prescription failed.
- Economies experienced **both high inflation AND high unemployment** simultaneously, plus stagnant output growth.
- Keynesian-style higher government expenditure simply pushed inflation up further without solving unemployment.
- This combination of stagnation + inflation = **stagflation**.
- Cause: **supply shocks from the oil crises of 1973 and 1979**.
- Stagflation forced economists to look beyond the simple Phillips curve and bring in **expectations**.
- **Phillips curve**: empirical inverse relationship between the rate of unemployment and the rate of inflation (originally wage increase). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Inflation–Unemployment Trade-off**: the policy choice along the Phillips curve — lower unemployment is achievable only at the cost of higher inflation, and vice versa. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Stagflation**: simultaneous occurrence of stagnation (low growth, high unemployment) and high inflation.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: thinking the original Phillips curve plotted inflation directly → ✅ Correct: Phillips' 1958 paper used **nominal wage growth**; inflation was substituted later.
- ❌ Mistake: viewing the Phillips curve as a permanent law → ✅ Correct: stagflation in the 1970s broke the simple trade-off and forced the expectations-augmented version.

**Quick Recall:**
- $\dot{w} = a - bu$ — Phillips' 1958 equation.
- Curve is **convex to the origin** in many empirical fits.
- Trade-off justified **discretionary** stabilization policy.
- 1973 & 1979 **oil crises** → stagflation → Phillips curve "shattered."

### Natural Rate of Unemployment 🔴

**Components of $u^*$**
- Cyclical unemployment is **NOT** part of $u^*$ — it's the deviation from $u^*$.
- $u^*$ varies across countries and over time within the same country.

**Empirical estimates**
- US natural rate ≈ **3.5%–4.5%**.
- Many countries do not report any estimate of $u^*$.

**Why the concept matters**
- It reshaped macroeconomic analysis after the 1960s.
- It paves the way for the role of **expectations** in determining the shape and position of the Phillips curve.
- **Natural rate of unemployment ($u^*$)**: the unemployment rate that exists due to frictions and imperfections in the economy; the sum of frictional + structural unemployment. Introduced by **Friedman and Phelps**. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Full employment**: state where the economy is operating at the natural rate of unemployment — NOT zero unemployment.

**Quick Recall:**
- $u^* =$ frictional + structural.
- Coined independently by **Friedman & Phelps**, 1960s.
- Full employment ≠ zero unemployment.
- US estimate: **3.5%–4.5%**.

### Expectation-Augmented Phillips Curve 🔴

**Why real (not nominal) wages matter**

**Two flavours of expectations**
| Type | Update rule |
|------|-------------|
| **Adaptive expectations** | Today's expected inflation depends on past observed inflation; agents revise *gradually* after errors |
| **Rational expectations** | Agents use *all* available information — past, present, and forecast — and the true model of the economy; errors are random and zero on average |

**Mechanism — the shift (Fig. 6.2)**
1. Start at point A: expected inflation $\pi_1$ (say 3%), unemployment at $u^*$ (say 6%). Curve = SRPC₁. No surprise; equilibrium stable.
2. Government runs **expansionist fiscal/monetary policy** (↑G, ↓T, ↑M, ↓i). Aggregate demand rises → prices rise → inflation jumps to $\pi_2$ (say 6%).
3. Because nominal wages are stuck under contracts, **real wages fall** → firms hire more labour → unemployment falls to $u_2$ < $u^*$.
4. Output exceeds potential output. **Point B** on SRPC₁.
5. Workers eventually realize prices have risen — unexpected inflation — and demand higher nominal wages.
6. SRPC shifts UP to SRPC₂. New equilibrium is **point C**: inflation = $\pi_3$, unemployment back at $u^*$.

**NAIRU and the long run**
- When $u = u^*$, inflation is stable; this is the **Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)**.
- If $u < u^*$, inflation **accelerates** continuously.
- If $u > u^*$, inflation decelerates.
- This explains hyperinflation: persistently keeping $u$ below $u^*$ spirals inflation upward.
- "Natural rate" suggests social optimality; **NAIRU** does NOT — it just describes price-stability.

**Conclusion under adaptive expectations**
- **Short-run Phillips curve**: downward-sloping (trade-off exists).
- **Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)**: vertical at $u^*$ → **no trade-off** between inflation and unemployment in the long run.

**Setup**

**Implication**
- Actual inflation = expected inflation (on average).
- Workers and firms commit no systematic error during wage negotiations.
- Therefore **no trade-off between inflation and unemployment**, even in the short run.
- Unemployment stays at the **natural rate**.

**Policy effectiveness — the punchline**
- If the government's expansionary policy is **expected/announced**, agents factor it into wage contracts → no impact on real variables.
- Only **unexpected (surprise)** policy can move unemployment temporarily.
- Bluntly: **expected government policy has no real impact**.
- **Adaptive expectations**: expectations about future inflation are formed by gradually updating from past observed values. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Rational expectations**: expectations formed using all available information and the true economic model; on average, expected = actual, with random errors. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment)**: unemployment rate at which inflation neither accelerates nor decelerates. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Long-Run Phillips Curve (LRPC)**: vertical line at $u^*$ showing no trade-off between inflation and unemployment in the long run. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Short-Run Phillips Curve (SRPC)**: downward-sloping curve showing a temporary trade-off, drawn for a given level of expected inflation.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: thinking NAIRU implies social optimality → ✅ Correct: NAIRU only describes price-stability; it carries no welfare claim, unlike "natural rate" which historically did.
- ❌ Mistake: confusing the Phillips curve **shift** with movement *along* the curve → ✅ Correct: a change in **expected** inflation **shifts** SRPC; movement along SRPC happens at fixed expected inflation.
- ❌ Mistake: under rational expectations, ALL policy is ineffective → ✅ Correct: only **anticipated** policy is ineffective; **unanticipated** (surprise) policy still moves output.
- The simple Phillips curve still works as a *short-run* description **if** expectations don't change.
- Hyperinflation episodes are explained by sustained $u < u^*$, where adaptive expectations push the SRPC up repeatedly.

- Adaptive: SRPC slopes down, **LRPC vertical at $u^*$**.
- Rational: even SRPC is vertical for **anticipated** shocks.
- **NAIRU = $u^*$** in adaptive models.

### 7.2 AS-AD Model and Non-Neutrality of Money 🔴

**Aggregate Demand (AD) function**
- Increasing in **G** (government expenditure), **T** (taxes — note book's wording), and **M/P** (real money balances).
- AD curve is **downward-sloping** in $(P, Y)$ space: lower $P$ → higher real money balances → lower interest rates → higher investment → higher AD.

**Aggregate Supply (AS) — derivation**
1. **Wage equation**: $W = P^e f(u, z)$, where $W$ = wage, $P^e$ = expected price, $u$ = unemployment, $z$ = other factors.
2. **Price-setting (cost-plus)**: $P = (1 + m)W$, where $m$ = mark-up (lower for more competitive economy).

**When $P = P^e$ (long run)**
- Real wage is what firms and workers jointly determine in the labour market given $u$ and $z$.
- The resulting unemployment rate is the **natural rate of unemployment**.
- Any gap between $P$ and $P^e$ closes over time as workers reduce expectational errors → natural rate prevails in the long run.

**When $P \neq P^e$ (short run)**
- Workers' expected real wages aren't met → AS curve shifts → output deviates from the natural level $Y_N$.

**Iterative adjustment to a money supply shock (Fig. 7.1)**
1. AD shifts right: $AD_0 \rightarrow AD_1$.
2. New short-run equilibrium: $Y_1 > Y_N$, $P_1 > P_0$.
3. Workers initially keep $P^e = P_0$ (adaptive); they're fooled into supplying more labour.
4. When workers spend wages, they realize $P_1 > P_0$ → demand higher nominal wages → AS shifts up to $AS_2$ ($P^e = P_1$).
5. New equilibrium: $P_2 > P_1$, $Y_2 < Y_1$ but $Y_2 > Y_N$ (still short run).
6. Process iterates until AS reaches $AS_f$ where $P = P^e$ again, output returns to $Y_N$, but at a permanently higher price level.

**"Modified" Phillips curve restatement**
- Price expectations rise when $P > P^e$.
- Therefore only an **unexpected** price rise (above what was expected) raises employment / output.
- In these AS-AD fooling models, **firms know real wages have fallen; workers don't** → asymmetric information.

**Adaptive expectations in this model**
- The model assumes $P^e_t = P_{t-1}$ — workers form expectations from the **past period's** price.
- This is a form of **adaptive expectations**.
- **Aggregate Demand function**: $Y = f(G, T, M/P)$, downward sloping in $(P, Y)$. ⭐
- **Aggregate Supply function**: $P = P^e(1+m)f(u, z)$ — derived from wage equation and cost-plus pricing. ⭐
- **Mark-up ($m$)**: price-cost margin in the price-setting equation; lower for more competitive economies.
- **Natural rate of unemployment (in AS-AD)**: rate prevailing when $P = P^e$.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: assuming AD is downward-sloping because of the law of demand → ✅ Correct: it slopes down via the **real money balance / interest rate / investment** channel.
- ❌ Mistake: thinking firms are fooled in the AS-AD short run → ✅ Correct: only **workers** are fooled; firms know real wages have fallen and hire more.

**Quick Recall:**
- $AS$: $P = P^e(1+m)f(u, z)$.
- $AD$: $Y = f(G, T, M/P)$.
- Short run: $P \neq P^e$ → output deviates from $Y_N$.
- Long run: $P = P^e$ → output back at $Y_N$.
- In AS-AD, expectation rule is $P^e_t = P_{t-1}$ — adaptive.
- Builds on: Expectation-Augmented Phillips Curve (Chunk 001).
- Contrasts with: Lucas Supply Curve (Chunk 003) where workers AND firms are symmetrically fooled.

### 7.3 The Lucas Critique 🔴

**Why "fooling" can't last**
- The **true model / actual process** generating prices.
- **All relevant information** that comes up.

**The critique formally**

**Worked illustration 1 — expansionary money**
- Policy maker increases growth of money supply.
- Expected price = $P_0$, actual price = $P_1 > P_0$.
- Agents adapt: next period's expectation $= P_1$.
- Actual price now $P_2 > P_1$ → workers still trail behind → real wages still falling.
- This "fooling" is *exactly* what Lucas questions.

**Worked illustration 2 — disinflation announcement**
- Government announces it will reduce inflation by slowing money supply growth, and people **believe** it (past credibility).
- Rational agents incorporate the announcement → reduce expected price → reduce nominal wage demands.
- When actual prices fall, **real wages don't rise** and unemployment doesn't rise either.
- Disinflation can be costless if credible.
1. Past data → estimated relationship (e.g., Phillips curve).
2. Policy maker exploits relationship → policy environment changes.
3. Agents update expectations using the new policy environment.
4. The estimated relationship breaks down → policy fails.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: thinking the Lucas Critique just means "models are wrong" → ✅ Correct: it specifically says **estimated parameters are not invariant** to policy regime changes.
- ❌ Mistake: applying the critique only to monetary policy → ✅ Correct: it generalizes to any policy that changes the environment in which agents form expectations.
- **Lucas Critique**: past statistical relationships in macroeconomic data are based on a particular set of expectations under a given policy regime; once the policy environment changes, those expectations and relationships change. ⭐ (exam-important)

**Quick Recall:**
- Concept: Muth (1961). Brought into economics: **Lucas & Sargent**, 1970s.
- Past relationships ≠ structural laws — they are **conditional on the policy regime**.
- Credible disinflation announcements can be costless under rational expectations.
- Critiques: AS-AD adaptive-expectations story (this chunk).
- Sets up: REH (Chunk 003), Lucas Supply Function (Chunk 003), Policy Ineffectiveness Theorem (Chunk 003).
- **Perfect foresight**: a state in which the AS-AD model's equations and parameters are known so that $P^e = P$ always; expectational error $= 0$; money is neutral even in the short run. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: equating perfect foresight with rational expectations → ✅ Correct: perfect foresight has **no random component**; rational expectations does, with random error mean = 0.
- ❌ Mistake: thinking perfect foresight requires only knowing past data → ✅ Correct: it requires knowing the **true model** AND having flexible wages/prices.

**Quick Recall:**
- Perfect foresight $\Rightarrow$ $P^e = P$ exactly.
- Money is neutral **even in the short run**.
- Real world ≠ perfect foresight → use rational expectations as the realistic limit.

### 7.5 Rational Expectations Hypothesis (REH) — Full Treatment 🔴

**Stochastic version of the true model**

**Rational expectation of $Y_t$**

**Expectational error properties**
- **Mean = 0** (because if not, we can adjust $\alpha$ to make it so).
- **Variance = $\sigma^2$** (known, finite).
- $\nu_t$ is unknowable until time $t$ — pure noise.

**Why RE is the most accurate expectation**
- It's based on the **true model** generating the variable.
- All other methods (astrology, dart-throwing, naive past trends, plain adaptive updating) ignore the underlying process and produce systematically worse forecasts on average.

**Example — consumption**
- Simple model: $C_t$ depends on past wealth, past consumption, past disposable income.
- RE-value of $C_t$ = average/expected consumption of a population.
- Each individual's actual consumption varies around this average (different preferences) — the deviations are the $\nu_t$ for each individual.

**What REH assumes about agents**
1. The **relevant variables** ($X, Y, Z$ etc.).
2. The **parameters** ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \eta$).
3. The **functional form** (linear here, or quadratic, exponential, etc.).

**Criticisms of REH**
- People in general are **not aware** of the underlying economic processes — assuming full model knowledge is unrealistic.
- Some relevant information is **expensive** to procure.
- However, modern access to **expert forecasts via public media** narrows the gap.
- Even with imperfect knowledge, RE forecasts will still be correct **on average**, just with larger variance.

**Why economists still adopt REH despite criticisms**
- It gives a **microfoundation** for macro theory: agents optimize and decisions are rational and most beneficial to them.
- With **flexible wages and prices**, demand and supply for output and labour clear in their respective markets.
- → no involuntary unemployment, no deviation of output from $Y_N$ on average.
- Microfoundation is an integral part of REH.

**One key macro implication**

**Open question raised**
- **Stochastic true model**: $Y_t = \alpha + \beta X_{t-1} + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \eta Z_{t-1} + \nu_t$ — equation (7.3); REH foundation. ⭐
- **Rational expectational error**: $\nu_t = Y_t - E_{t-1}Y_t$; mean 0, variance $\sigma^2$. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Microfoundations**: building macro relationships from optimizing decisions of individual agents (firms maximize profit, workers maximize utility).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: assuming RE means agents are *always right* → ✅ Correct: they are right **on average**; individual errors $\nu_t$ are random and non-zero.
- ❌ Mistake: equating RE with perfect foresight → ✅ Correct: RE includes $\nu_t$; perfect foresight has $\nu_t = 0$.
- ❌ Mistake: thinking RE rules out **all** policy effects → ✅ Correct: it rules out only **anticipated** policy; surprise policy still works (see 7.7).

**Quick Recall:**
- Equation (7.3): adds $\nu_t$ to the deterministic model.
- Expectational error: mean 0, variance $\sigma^2$.
- REH provides **microfoundations** for new-classical macro.
- **Anticipated** policy → no real effect; **unanticipated** policy → real effect.

### 7.6 Lucas Supply Function 🔴

**What agents care about: relative prices**
- A **firm** cares about price of its output relative to prices of inputs it buys.
- A **worker** cares about real wage = nominal wage / prices of goods bought.
- Higher relative price for a firm → more output (assuming substitution > income effect).
- Higher real wage for a worker → more labour supply.

**Two signal-extraction problems**
1. **Aggregate vs. relative price change**: when an agent sees ↑ price for their good, is it only their good (relative ↑) or all goods (aggregate ↑)? Each agent must extract this signal.
2. **Temporary vs. permanent change**: temporary relative price increases are more important — agents must act fast to capture profitable opportunities. If history says general price increases mix relative + aggregate components, agents observing a price rise will assume **part of it** is relative → supply more.

**Information asymmetry — local vs. global**

**Anticipated vs. surprise inflation under RE**
- If agents anticipate / know about a general ↑P (e.g., from announced ↑M), they understand all prices have risen proportionately → no relative price change → **no change in output or labour supply**.
- If there's a **price surprise** ($P_t > E_{t-1}P_t$), agents wrongly attribute part of it to relative ↑ → output / labour supply rise above $Y_N$.

**The Lucas Supply Curve**
- $Y_t$ = output in period $t$.
- $Y_p$ = potential / full-employment output.
- $P_t$ = price of product.
- $E_{t-1}P_t$ = price of product expected one period prior.
- Lower-case = log of upper-case variable.

**Stable vs. volatile-price economies**
| Environment | Effect of monetary expansion |
|-------------|------------------------------|
| Prices generally stable; past ↑P often = relative ↑ | Agents fooled → ↑ output, ↑ labour supply |
| Prices generally volatile (high inflation, e.g., some Latin American economies) | Agents NOT fooled → no output / labour effect |

**Crucial contrast with AS-AD**
- **AS-AD (Section 7.2)**: workers fooled, firms not — **asymmetric** information.
- **Lucas Supply Curve**: workers AND firms **symmetrically** fooled by local-only information.

**Lucas explanation of the business cycle**
- **Lucas Supply Function**: $y_t - y_p = f(p_t - E_{t-1}p_t)$; output deviates from full-employment output only with a price surprise. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Signal extraction problem**: each agent's task of distinguishing a relative-price change from a general-price change using only local information. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Local vs. global information**: agents know more about prices of what they sell than what they buy.
- **Price surprise**: $P_t - E_{t-1}P_t > 0$; the unanticipated component of inflation.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: treating the Lucas Supply Curve as a Phillips curve in disguise → ✅ Correct: it's a *supply* function with **anticipated** changes having NO effect, unlike the original Phillips curve.
- ❌ Mistake: assuming Lucas's framework allows expected inflation to lower unemployment → ✅ Correct: only **unexpected** inflation does.
- ❌ Mistake: applying Lucas's mechanism to high-inflation economies the same way → ✅ Correct: in volatile-price economies the fooling is much weaker because agents *expect* general inflation.
- High-inflation economies (Latin American examples cited) erode the fooling mechanism.
- The "fooling" here is symmetric — different from AS-AD's asymmetric fooling story.

**Quick Recall:**
- Equation (7.6): $y_t - y_p = f(p_t - E_{t-1}p_t)$.
- Output deviates only with a **price surprise**.
- Workers AND firms are symmetrically fooled (vs. AS-AD).
- In high-inflation economies, mechanism is weak → money close to neutral.

- Sets up: Policy Ineffectiveness Theorem (this chunk).

### 7.7 Policy Ineffectiveness Theorem 🔴

**Setup**
- Only **monetary** policy is considered (no fiscal).
- Agents have **rational expectations** about the central bank's money-supply rule.
- A random component $\epsilon_t$ in money supply represents what the central bank cannot fully control (capital flows, banks holding excess reserves, public preferring cash, etc.).

**The four-equation model**
- $M_t$: log money supply.
- $V_t$: log velocity (constant).
- $P_t$: log price level.
- $y_t$: log real output.
- $y_p$: log full-employment output.
- ${}_{t-1}P^e_t$: log expected price for period $t$, formed in period $(t-1)$.
- Output exceeds $y_p$ only if there is a positive price surprise.
- $\alpha y_{t-1}$ is the **systematic / anticipatable** part of money supply, derived from past observed output (the central bank's reaction function).
- $\epsilon_t$ is the **unpredictable / random** part with conditional mean zero.

**Graphical preview (Figs. 7.2 and 7.3)**
- Without RE (Fig. 7.2): ↑M shifts AD right; AS unchanged → both $P$ and $y$ rise — looks like the original Phillips curve trade-off.
- With RE (Fig. 7.3): in period $(t-1)$ agents already anticipate the systematic shift in AD due to expected ↑M → AS shifts up by exactly the expected price increase. The dotted AD curve $AD^e_1$ shows the *anticipated* shift; the solid $AD_1$ may be to the right of $AD^e_1$ if the random component $\epsilon_t > 0$.
- Only the **gap** between $AD_1$ and $AD^e_1$ (the unanticipated part) generates a price surprise → output deviates.

**Algebraic derivation — step-by-step**

**Interpretation of (7.15) and (7.16)**
- (7.15): the price surprise is driven entirely by $\epsilon_t$ — the **unanticipated** part of money supply.
- (7.16): output exceeds $y_p$ **only** when $\epsilon_t \neq 0$.
- (7.15) also implies $P_t = {}_{t-1}P^e_t + \epsilon_t/(1+\beta)$ → both **expected** price and the **unanticipated** money component affect actual prices, but only **unanticipated** money affects **output**.

**The two key results**
1. Only **unanticipated (surprise)** monetary policy moves output, given RE and a known money-supply rule.
2. Whatever functional form the rule takes, the moment it's known, agents incorporate it → no real effect.

**Money's neutrality status**
- Lucas Supply Curve & Policy Ineffectiveness → money is **NOT neutral** in the short run (because of $\epsilon_t$).
- But **systematic** money is neutral — only unsystematic surprises matter.

**Keynesian critique**
- Correct information should be available **within a short time** → booms / recessions spanning **several years** are not well explained by RE-based fooling.
- The assumption that all markets clear (implying voluntary unemployment) is implausible during high unemployment.
- Despite criticism, RE remains "an appealing way to incorporate microeconomic foundations" of macro relations.
- It has further implications elsewhere — e.g., the **random walk of consumption** and **Ricardian Equivalence**.
1. Agents observe past money-supply behaviour → infer rule $\alpha y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$.
2. Anticipate systematic component $\alpha y_{t-1}$ → adjust $P^e$ → AS shifts up correspondingly.
3. Actual $M_t$ may differ from $E_{t-1}M_t$ by $\epsilon_t$ → AD shifts beyond expected position.
4. Difference $P_t - {}_{t-1}P^e_t = \epsilon_t/(1+\beta)$ creates a price surprise.
5. Lucas Supply: $y_t = y_p + \beta\epsilon_t/(1+\beta)$ → output deviation depends ONLY on $\epsilon_t$.
- **Policy Ineffectiveness Theorem (Sargent & Wallace, 1976)**: under rational expectations and a known money-supply rule, only the **unanticipated** part of monetary policy moves output; any predictable rule has no real effect. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Money supply rule**: $M_t = \alpha y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$, where $\alpha y_{t-1}$ is anticipatable, $\epsilon_t$ is random with conditional mean 0. ⭐
- **Endogenous expectations**: expected value derived within the same model that explains the actual variable (eq. 7.10).
1. Substitute $M_t$ and $y_t$ into AD equation → (7.11).
2. Take $E_{t-1}$ of (7.11), use $E(\epsilon_t)=0$ → solve for ${}_{t-1}P^e_t$ → (7.12).
3. Express $P_t$ from AD and substitute → (7.13).
4. Subtract (7.12) from (7.13) → (7.14).
5. Solve for price surprise → $P_t - {}_{t-1}P^e_t = \epsilon_t / (1 + \beta)$ → (7.15).
6. Substitute into Lucas supply → $y_t = y_p + \beta\epsilon_t/(1 + \beta)$ → (7.16).
