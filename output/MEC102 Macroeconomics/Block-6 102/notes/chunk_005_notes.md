## Section: Impact of a Higher Inflation Target 🔴

<!-- See chunk 004 for the consensus model setup (Taylor rule, IS curve, AD curve) -->

### Core Idea
Within the new-consensus framework, **raising the inflation target only delivers a temporary output boost**. A higher target lowers the policy rate (via Taylor's rule), which expands AD and pushes output above natural; but the accelerationist Phillips Curve drives inflation up until the economy lands at the *new* (higher) inflation target with output back at $y'$. The neutral rate is unchanged. **Net result: same output, higher inflation.**

> **In Simple Terms:** If the central bank decides "we'll tolerate higher inflation now," it cuts rates and the economy briefly grows faster. But inflation catches up — and the rate cut has to be reversed. Eventually output is back where it started; the only change is that inflation is permanently higher.

### Mechanisms / Processes

Use Fig. 17.3.

**Step 1**: Initial equilibrium at A on AD line, with $y = y'$ and $x = x'$.
**Step 2**: Central bank raises inflation target $x'$ → AD line shifts up to $AD'$ (intercept rises per eq. 17.14).
**Step 3**: Higher inflation target → lower interest rate (Taylor rule) → ↑ AD.
**Step 4**: Economy moves from A to **C**: actual output $y > y'$.
**Step 5**: At C, $y > y'$ → from accelerationist PC, inflation rises. Output falls back along $AD'$.
**Step 6**: New equilibrium at **B**, where $y = y'$ but $x = x''$ (the new, higher target).

#### What Stays Constant, What Changes
| Variable | Change |
|----------|--------|
| Inflation target | $x' \to x''$ (higher) |
| Long-run output | $y'$ (unchanged) |
| Long-run inflation | Higher (= new target) |
| Neutral rate of interest | Unchanged |
| Long-run interest rate | Unchanged ($r' = c/d$) |
| Short-run interest rate | Temporarily lower |
| Short-run output | Temporarily higher |

### Key Insight
**Fixing a higher inflation target results only in a temporary reduction in the interest rate.** It causes a short-run increase in AD and output. **In the long run, rate of interest cannot diverge from its neutral level.**

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Higher inflation target → AD shifts up → short-run output gain.
> - Long run: output back at $y'$; only inflation is permanently higher.
> - Neutral rate $r' = c/d$ is unchanged.

### Connections
- Builds on: Taylor's Rule, IS curve, AD curve (Chunk 004).
- Builds on: Friedman's no-long-run-trade-off claim (Chunk 004).

## Section: Impact of Expansionary Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
In the consensus model, **expansionary fiscal policy is impotent**: it raises neither output nor inflation in the long run. The only lasting effect is **complete crowding out of private expenditure** through a higher neutral interest rate. This is in *sharp contrast* to the Keynesian view of fiscal policy.

### Mechanisms / Processes

Use Fig. 17.4.

**Step 1**: Government expenditure rises → parameter c (in IS curve) rises.
**Step 2**: At given inflation, AD shifts outward from AD to $AD'$ — economy moves A → **C**.
**Step 3**: At C, $y > y'$ → inflation rises, output falls along $AD'$ → economy reaches **B**.
**Step 4**: B is *not* an equilibrium because $x > x'$.
**Step 5**: To bring inflation back to target: an increase in c **also raises the neutral rate of interest** $r' = c/d$. So central bank raises $r'$.
**Step 6**: Higher $r'$ contracts AD → economy moves B → **D**.
**Step 7**: From D, inflation falls and output rises along the (new) AD until economy returns to **A** (original equilibrium).

#### Net Result
| Variable | Long-run change |
|----------|-----------------|
| Output | Unchanged ($y'$) |
| Inflation | Unchanged ($x'$) |
| Neutral interest rate | **Higher** (because c is higher) |
| Private expenditure | **Crowded out** by higher rate |

### Key Concepts

#### Crowding Out — Two Versions Compared
| | Standard analysis | New consensus |
|---|------------------|---------------|
| **Mechanism** | Higher public investment → excess of total investment demand over savings → interest rate rises to clear loanable-funds market | Central bank fixes interest rate; expansionary fiscal policy raises inflation → bank pushes up rate to keep inflation at target |
| **Common conclusion** | Private spending crowded out by higher rate | Same |

### Definitions ⭐
- **Crowding out (consensus version)**: an expansionary fiscal policy displaces private expenditure because the central bank, forced to maintain the inflation target, raises the interest rate.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Thinking expansionary fiscal policy boosts output → ✅ Long run, output and inflation unchanged in this model.
- ❌ Confusing the consensus crowding-out mechanism with the loanable-funds version → ✅ In the consensus, the central bank *chooses* to raise the rate to defend the target; in the standard view the market clears via savings-investment.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- **The Keynesian view (from Unit 2) sharply contradicts this**: in Keynes' framework fiscal policy boosts output via the multiplier.
- This model assumes the central bank is committed to inflation targeting and reacts mechanically to defend $x'$.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Fiscal expansion: **no long-run effect on output or inflation**.
> - Only effect: **higher neutral interest rate** → **crowding out** of private expenditure.
> - Sharply contradicts Keynesian view from Unit 2.

### Connections
- Builds on: Taylor's Rule and AD curve (Chunk 004).
- Contrasts with: Keynesian fiscal multiplier (referenced as Unit 2 of course).

## Section: Summary of the Consensus View 🟡

### Four Core Conclusions
1. **Stabilisation role**: Central banks can stabilise output at its natural level and inflation at a pre-defined target.
2. **Output above natural is impossible long-run**: Stimulating output above $y'$ via monetary policy fails in the long run. Higher inflation target → higher inflation, no output gain.
3. **Setting r below neutral fails too**: Reducing the rate of interest below the neutral rate (the *unanswered exercise* in the textbook: think about what happens to inflation if r < r' — inflation accelerates) — this policy will also fail to deliver long-run output gains.
4. **No role for activist fiscal policy**: Fiscal policy cannot control inflation or stabilise output. These are the central bank's job, equipped with an appropriate interest-rate rule.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Consensus = **monetary policy is neutral** in the long run.
> - Activist fiscal policy has **no space** in the framework.
> - Stabilisation around natural level, *not* improvement of natural level.

## Section: Path Dependence (Hysteresis) — The Critique 🔴

### Core Idea
The consensus model assumes natural output $y'$ is **exogenous**. Critics (notably **M. Lavoie**) argue that natural output is itself **endogenous to aggregate demand**: high actual output promotes learning-by-doing, capital formation, and labour-force expansion — all of which raise the productive capacity. Once $y'$ depends on $y$, **activist monetary or fiscal policy regains its potency** and there is no unique equilibrium — the resting point depends on the starting point. This is **path dependence** (also called **hysteresis**).

> **In Simple Terms:** The consensus says "the economy has a fixed ceiling." Critics say "the ceiling rises when you push hard against it" — through learning, investment, and labour entry. So activist policy doesn't just stimulate temporarily, it can permanently lift potential output.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Reasons Natural Output May Depend on Demand
1. **Learning-by-doing**: high actual output improves labour productivity / reduces per-unit labour cost.
2. **Capital formation**: higher output induces more investment → higher capital stock → improved labour productivity → lower labour costs.
3. **Labour-force expansion**: higher AD encourages potential workers to enter the labour force, augmenting labour supply.

> *"Simply put, the capacity to produce depends on the amount actually produced."*

#### Extending the Model
Add equation:
$$\Delta y' = f(y - y') \quad \dots (17.17)$$

Change in natural output is positively related to deviations of actual output from natural.

Assuming the central bank fixes $r'$ per (17.15), AD reduces to:
$$y = y' + \left[\frac{da(x' - x)}{1 + bd}\right] \quad \dots (17.18)$$

Taking first differences and substituting:
$$\Delta y = \left[\frac{f - dae}{1 + bd}\right] (y - y') + \left[\frac{da \, \Delta x'}{1 + bd}\right] \quad \dots (17.19)$$

For a given policy stance, $\Delta x' = 0$. From (17.17) and (17.19): when $y = y'$, neither $y$ nor $y'$ change. So **all points on the $y = y'$ line in Fig. 17.5 represent equilibria** — there is no unique equilibrium.

#### Path Dependence Illustrated (Fig. 17.5)
- Initial equilibrium: B (on $y = y'$ line).
- Central bank raises inflation target → $\Delta x' > 0$ → $\Delta y > 0$.
- Economy temporarily shifts B → **D**.
- Higher inflation target → lower interest rate → AD stimulus → output rises.
- At D, $y > y'$. Although $\Delta y$ may turn negative (if the bracketed term in 17.19 is negative), the economy will *not* return to B because $y > y'$ also makes $y'$ rise (by 17.17).
- Economy moves north-west to a **new equilibrium E**, with both $y$ and $y'$ permanently higher.

#### Caveat — Speed of Response
Factors that raise natural output (worker entry, immigration, productivity) **take time**. If, in the meanwhile, excess demand causes rapid inflation acceleration, the central bank is forced to raise rates sharply → deviations from natural output are short-lived.

The mainstream (Friedman) sees the natural rate of unemployment as having a **knife-edge property**: any deviation triggers rapid inflation acceleration/deceleration. In equation (17.19), this corresponds to high values of $e$.

#### Empirical Evidence
- **Most studies show** unemployment can stay below natural for extended periods with only **mild** inflation increases.
- The **short-run Phillips Curve is concave** — there *is* a trade-off between unemployment and inflation in the short run.
- In the long run, Phillips Curve is **vertical** at the natural rate of unemployment (consensus position).
- **Conclusion**: unemployment can be driven below its natural rate at *low cost* in inflation terms.

#### Implications — How Hysteresis Overturns the Consensus
| | Consensus / Monetarist / New-Classical | With path dependence |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural output** | Exogenously given | Endogenous to AD |
| **Activist policy** | Cannot raise output | Can permanently raise both $y$ and $y'$ |
| **Equilibrium** | Unique | Path-dependent — depends on starting point |
| **Long-run Phillips Curve** | Vertical | Effectively concave / not vertical |

### Definitions ⭐
- **Path dependence (hysteresis)** ⭐: macroeconomic outcome where the long-run equilibrium depends on the starting point and the path taken — typically because natural output (or natural unemployment) depends on actual output (or unemployment).
- **Knife-edge property of natural unemployment** (Friedman): the claim that any deviation from natural unemployment triggers rapidly accelerating or decelerating inflation.
- **Learning-by-doing**: productivity improvement that arises from cumulative experience of producing.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Treating natural output as fixed by technology and labour endowments alone → ✅ It can depend on AD via learning, investment, and labour-force expansion.
- ❌ Believing the long-run Phillips Curve is *always* vertical → ✅ Empirically, the short-run PC is concave, and persistent low-unemployment periods don't always trigger runaway inflation.
- ❌ Assuming policy can only stabilise around an exogenous target → ✅ Under hysteresis, policy can shift the target itself.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- High values of $e$ (Phillips-curve sensitivity) shorten how long output can deviate from natural — restoring the consensus result.
- Hysteresis effects take time to materialise; rapid inflation may force the bank to act before they appear.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Three sources of hysteresis**: learning-by-doing, capital formation, labour-force expansion.
> - $\Delta y' = f(y - y')$ — natural output rises when actual output exceeds it.
> - Under hysteresis, **no unique equilibrium** — depends on initial point (Fig. 17.5: B → D → E).
> - **Short-run PC is concave**, not vertical.
> - **Lavoie**, among others, championed this critique.

### Connections
- Builds on: Consensus model and AD curve (Chunk 004).
- Contradicts: Friedman's knife-edge view (Chunk 004).
- Contradicts: Higher inflation target conclusion (Chunk 005, prior section).

### Open Questions
1. How can policy makers tell whether the economy is in a "knife-edge" or "hysteresis" regime?
2. Empirically, are the productivity / labour-force responses to demand strong enough to validate hysteresis as a policy guide?

## Section: Unit 17 — Summary 🟢

### Key Takeaways
1. **Rules vs Discretion**:
   - Friedman: constant money-growth rule.
   - Barro: zero/low inflation rule.
   - New-Keynesians: equation-based policy-rate rules.
   - Mainstream: rules > discretion (despite no consensus on definition of "rules").

2. **Consensus View**:
   - Monetary policy provides the **nominal anchor** — fixes inflation at target.
   - Cannot influence output beyond its natural level.
   - Revival of **Friedmanite neutrality**: market forces deliver full employment.
   - Best a central bank can do: reduce fluctuations around natural output via interest-rate policy.

3. **Critique (Path Dependence)**:
   - Natural output may not be exogenously given.
   - High AD → higher actual *and* natural output → higher equilibrium.
   - Activist policy regains potency.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Three big debates** in monetary theory: rules vs discretion, neutrality, exogeneity of natural output.
> - **Mainstream prefers rules** but disagrees on what "rule" means.
> - **Hysteresis critique**: capacity depends on what you actually produce.
