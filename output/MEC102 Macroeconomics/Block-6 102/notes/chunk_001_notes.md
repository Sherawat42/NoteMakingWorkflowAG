## Section: Money — Evolution and Functions 🟢

### Core Idea
Before money, the **barter system** required a "double coincidence of wants" — both traders had to want what the other offered, which made exchange clumsy. Money was invented to remove this constraint and is defined as anything commonly accepted for the exchange of goods and services. Modern paper currency is **fiat money**: it has no intrinsic value and cannot be redeemed for any precious commodity, yet circulates because everyone accepts it.

> **In Simple Terms:** Barter is like trying to swap a haircut for bread when the baker doesn't need a haircut. Money breaks that deadlock — everyone takes it, so the baker sells bread, then buys whatever they actually want later.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Functions of Money
1. **Unit of account / measure of value** — prices express market value in money terms.
2. **Medium of exchange** — accepted in every transaction; eliminates the coincidence-of-wants problem.
3. **Store of value** — money can be held now and spent later; it confers purchasing power across time.

#### Five Criteria Money Must Satisfy
1. Standardisation (uniform units)
2. General acceptability
3. Divisibility (exchangeable across denominations)
4. Portability (easy to carry)
5. Longevity

Commodity money (salt, cattle, grain, precious metals) failed most of these tests; modern paper money satisfies all five.

### Definitions
- **Barter system**: trading goods/services directly for other goods/services, without money.
- **Double coincidence of wants**: both parties must simultaneously want what the other offers — a precondition for barter.
- **Fiat money**: money with no intrinsic value and not redeemable for any commodity; accepted by social/legal convention.
- **Commodity money**: money whose material has intrinsic value (gold coins, salt, cattle).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Barter fails because of the **double coincidence of wants** problem.
> - Money's three functions: **unit of account, medium of exchange, store of value**.
> - Fiat money = no intrinsic value; commodity money = intrinsic value.

## Section: Measures of Money Supply 🔴

### Core Idea
**Money supply** is the total amount of currency and other liquid instruments held by the *public* (households, firms, local authorities, companies) — it excludes money held by the RBI and the government because that money is out of circulation. There is no single, universally agreed definition: instead, the RBI publishes a graded set of monetary and liquidity aggregates ranging from the most liquid (M0) to the broadest (L3), each capturing a different "store of value" or "medium of exchange" instrument.

> **In Simple Terms:** Picture a stack of containers. The smallest container holds only cash and central-bank reserves. Each bigger container adds another layer of less-liquid deposits — savings accounts, fixed deposits, post-office deposits — until you reach the broadest "money" available in the economy.

### Key Concepts

#### Why money supply has multiple definitions
Money is defined by the *functions* it performs. Different instruments perform the "store of value" or "medium of exchange" function with different degrees of liquidity (cash vs savings vs fixed deposits vs cheques vs electronic transfers). Restricting "money supply" to currency alone would ignore most of how value is actually held and exchanged.

#### What is excluded from money supply
- Money held as **CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)** with the RBI.
- Money held as **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)** with commercial banks.
- Money held by the government.
These are out of public circulation, so they are excluded.

#### History of monetary aggregate working groups
| Year | Body | Role |
|------|------|------|
| 1961 | First Working Group on Money Supply (FWG) | First framework |
| 1977 | Second Working Group (SWG) | Revision |
| 1998 | Working Group on Money Supply: Analytics and Methodology of Compilation (WGMS) | Introduced NM0–NM3 and L1–L3 aggregates |

Since **July 1935**, the RBI has been the sole authority to compile and disseminate India's monetary statistics.

### Definitions — Old (M-series) Monetary Aggregates ⭐ (exam-important)

| Aggregate | Components | Nickname |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **M0** | Currency in circulation + Bankers' deposits with RBI + 'Other deposits' with RBI | Reserve money / monetary base / high-powered money / central bank money |
| **M1** | Currency with the public + Demand deposits with banks + 'Other deposits' with RBI | Narrow money |
| **M2** | M1 + Savings deposits of post office savings banks | — |
| **M3** | M1 + Time deposits with the banking system | Broad money |
| **M4** | M3 + All deposits with post office savings banks (excl. National Savings Certificates) | — |

**In practice in India**: M0, M1, and M3 are widely used; M2 and M4 are rarely used.

### Definitions — New (NM-series) Monetary & Liquidity Aggregates ⭐ (exam-important)
Introduced by RBI on WGMS (1998) recommendations.

| Aggregate | Components |
|-----------|-----------|
| **NM0** | Monetary base = Currency in circulation + Bankers' deposits with RBI + 'other deposits' with RBI |
| **NM1** | Currency with the public + Demand deposits with the banking system + 'Other deposits' with RBI (non-interest-bearing monetary liabilities of the banking sector) |
| **NM2** | NM1 + Short-term time deposits of residents (≤ 1 year contractual maturity) — represents transactions balances |
| **NM3** | NM2 + Long-term time deposits of residents + Call/Term funding from financial institutions |

**Liquidity Aggregates:**
| | Components |
|---|-----------|
| **L1** | NM3 + All deposits with the post office savings banks (excl. NSCs) |
| **L2** | L1 + Term deposits with term lending and refinancing institutions (FIs) + Term borrowing by FIs + Certificates of deposit issued by FIs |
| **L3** | L2 + Public deposits of non-banking financial companies |

#### Why NM2/NM3 cover *residents* only
Currency and deposits held by non-resident Indians normally appear in the **balance of payments** as international capital flows. But non-resident *rupee* deposits are integrated into the domestic monetary market, so they are partially included. Specifically excluded as **external liabilities** are non-resident repatriable foreign-currency fixed deposits: **FCNR(B)** (Foreign Currency Non-Resident bank), **RIBs** (Resurgent India Bonds), and **IMDs** (India Millennium Deposits).

### Mechanisms / Processes
- **Total stock of money** = money with public + money with RBI + money with government.
- **Money supply** = first component only (money held with the public).

### Examples
- **Narrow money** = M0, M1 (easily converted into cash).
- **Broad money** = M3 (less liquid, includes time deposits).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Including CRR/SLR balances in money supply → ✅ They are excluded; they are out of circulation.
- ❌ Treating "currency in circulation" as the entire money supply → ✅ Currency is just one component; demand deposits and other deposits also count.
- ❌ Conflating M0 with M1 → ✅ M0 is reserve money (central-bank money); M1 adds demand deposits at commercial banks.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - M0 = Reserve / high-powered money. M3 = Broad money.
> - M1 includes demand deposits; M3 adds time deposits.
> - Money supply = money held by the **public only** (excludes RBI, government, CRR, SLR).
> - Sole authority compiling monetary stats since **July 1935**: RBI.
> - WGMS (1998) introduced NM0–NM3 and L1–L3.

## Section: Money Multiplier 🔴

### Core Idea
Commercial banks operate on a **fractional reserve system**: a deposit creates further deposits as the bank lends out a fraction of every rupee it receives. The **money multiplier (m)** is the multiple by which the monetary base (M0) expands into the broader money supply (M1). Mathematically, m = M1/M0, and equals the inverse of the reserve ratio in the simplest case.

> **In Simple Terms:** You deposit ₹1,000. The bank keeps part as reserves (CRR + SLR) and lends out the rest. Whoever borrows the ₹775 spends it; the receiver re-deposits some portion; the bank lends again. Each cycle adds money, so a single ₹1,000 of base money supports several thousand rupees of total deposits.

### Key Concepts

#### Numerical illustration (from text, India 2022)
- Deposit: ₹1,000.
- CRR = 4.5% → ₹45 to RBI.
- SLR = 18% → ₹180 in cash, gold, government securities.
- Available for lending: **₹775**.
This ₹775 becomes income for borrowers, who deposit part of it back, enabling further lending — the **money multiplier process**.

#### Mathematical Derivation
**Notation:**
- C = currency held by general public
- D = demand deposits held by general public
- R = bank reserves (cash held by banks + their demand deposits at central bank)
- $cd = C/D$ → desired **currency–deposit ratio** (chosen by the non-bank public)
- $rd = R/D$ → required **reserve ratio** (set by central bank via CRR + bank's excess reserves)

**Steps:**
$$M1 = C + D = cd \cdot D + D = (cd + 1) D \quad \dots (15.1)$$

$$M0 = C + R = cd \cdot D + rd \cdot D = (cd + rd) D$$

$$D = \frac{M0}{cd + rd} \quad \dots (15.2)$$

Substituting (15.2) into (15.1):

$$M1 = \frac{cd + 1}{cd + rd} \cdot M0 = m \cdot M0$$

$$\boxed{m = \frac{cd + 1}{cd + rd}} \quad \dots (15.3)$$

**Worked example from text:** if $cd = 0.4$ and $rd = 0.1$, then
$$m = \frac{0.4 + 1}{0.4 + 0.1} = \frac{1.4}{0.5} = 2.8$$

**Simple-rule example:** if $rd = 0.25$ (and assuming $C = 0$), $m = 1/0.25 = 4$.

#### Four Implications of equation (15.3)
| # | Implication |
|---|---|
| (a) | Multiplier is **higher** for broader money measures (e.g. M3 > M1) |
| (b) | $m$ depends on $cd$ and $rd$; both vary with expected returns, liquidity, and risk on alternative assets |
| (c) | $\Delta M1 = m \cdot \Delta M0$ — knowing $m$ lets the central bank target $M1$ via $M0$ |
| (d) | Higher $rd$ → lower $m$ → less money supply (this is how raising **CRR/SLR** tightens monetary policy) |

#### Alternative Interpretation — Currency-Money Ratio
Define $cm = C/M1$ (public's desired currency-to-money ratio).

From $M1 = (cd + 1) D$:
$$cm = \frac{cd}{cd + 1} \quad \dots (15.4)$$
$$1 - cm = \frac{1}{cd + 1} \quad \dots (15.5)$$

Substituting in (15.3):
$$M1 = \frac{1}{1 - (1 - rd)(1 - cm)} \cdot M0 \quad \dots (15.6)$$

This form reveals the multiplier as a **geometric series**: through OMO, ΔM0 increases M0 by the amount ΔM0; the bond-seller keeps fraction cm as cash and deposits (1−cm); the bank holds rd of (1−cm)ΔM0 as reserves and lends out (1−rd)(1−cm)ΔM0; round 3 adds $(1-rd)^2 (1-cm)^2 \Delta M0$; and so on.

$$\Delta M1 = \frac{1}{1 - (1 - rd)(1 - cm)} \Delta M0 \quad \dots (15.7)$$

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Fractional-reserve sequence:**
Deposit ₹1,000 → bank holds CRR (₹45) + SLR (₹180) → lends ₹775 → borrower spends → receiver deposits portion → bank lends fraction again → repeats indefinitely → final deposit base ≫ initial deposit.

### Examples
- **Great Depression (1929–1933, US):** money multiplier fell sharply. Depositors panicked and withdrew, raising $cd$; banks held more reserves, raising $rd$ — both pulled $m$ down. *Lesson:* multiplier is stable in normal times but breaks during banking crises.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Using $m = 1/rd$ as the universal formula → ✅ That ignores currency holdings; the full formula is $m = (cd+1)/(cd+rd)$.
- ❌ Treating $cd$ and $rd$ as constants → ✅ They shift with risk perception, liquidity preference, and crises.
- ❌ Thinking the multiplier creates *real* wealth → ✅ It creates deposit *money*, not goods.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- $rd$ includes both required CRR and any **excess reserves** banks voluntarily hold for liquidity/lending-risk reasons.
- The multiplier assumes leakages (cash withdrawals) are stable; in crises this assumption fails.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Money multiplier formula**: $m = \dfrac{cd + 1}{cd + rd}$.
> - $\Delta M1 = m \cdot \Delta M0$.
> - $cd$ chosen by **public**; $rd$ set by **central bank** (CRR) + bank's excess reserves.
> - Higher CRR/SLR → lower $m$ → tighter money supply.
> - Multiplier collapses in banking panics (Great Depression example).

## Section: Open Market Operations (OMO) — Introduction 🔴

### Core Idea
**Open Market Operations** are the central bank's most popular tool for influencing the monetary base: the bank **buys government securities** to inject reserves (expansionary policy) or **sells government securities** to drain reserves (contractionary policy). Because the monetary base feeds through the money multiplier, even modest OMO transactions can produce sizeable changes in the total money supply.

> **In Simple Terms:** The RBI uses OMO like a tap. To make more money flow, it *buys* bonds from banks and pays them with new reserves. To slow the flow, it *sells* bonds and absorbs reserves out of the system.

### Key Concepts

#### Four Functions of a Central Bank
1. **Banker's bank / lender of last resort** — maintains solvency and liquidity of commercial banks.
2. **Promotes stable economic growth.**
3. **Stabiliser of purchasing power** — maintains stable inflation.
4. **Manages exchange rate** — reduces volatility.

These are complementary but can conflict — e.g. inflation targeting may suppress growth; crisis-time liquidity injections create inflationary pressure.

#### Expansionary vs Contractionary OMO
| | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|---|---|---|
| Action | Central bank **buys** government securities | Central bank **sells** government securities |
| Effect on monetary base | Increases | Decreases |
| Bank balance sheet | Shifts from income-producing securities → non-income-producing excess reserves | Reverse |
| Bank response | Convert excess reserves to new loans by **lowering interest rate** | Reduces lending |
| Used when | Higher demand for liquidity; rates need to be kept in check | Money supply needs to be shrunk |

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Expansionary OMO chain:** RBI buys bonds → pays banks via reserve-account credit → banks' excess reserves rise → banks cut lending rates to deploy reserves → loans expand → money supply rises by $m \times \Delta M0$.

### Definitions
- **Open Market Operations (OMO)** ⭐: process of increasing or reducing the monetary base by buying (expansionary) or selling (contractionary) government securities in the open market.
- **Expansionary monetary policy**: central-bank stance that increases monetary base / lowers interest rates to stimulate the economy.
- **Contractionary monetary policy**: stance that reduces monetary base / raises interest rates to curb demand.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "OMO = printing money" → ✅ OMO swaps central-bank reserves for securities; it changes M0's *composition* and effective supply, not raw note printing.
- ❌ Thinking OMO directly determines lending rates → ✅ It changes reserves; *banks* then choose to lower lending rates to deploy them.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Buy bonds = expansion**; **sell bonds = contraction**.
> - OMO works *through* the money multiplier — small ΔM0 → larger ΔM1.
> - Four central-bank functions: lender of last resort, growth, price stability, exchange-rate management.
