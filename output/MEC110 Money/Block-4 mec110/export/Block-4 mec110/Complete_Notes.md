## Section: Concept and Features of the Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The money market is the segment of the financial market that handles short-term (up to one year in India) financial assets which are close substitutes for money. It functions as an **over-the-phone / online wholesale market** that equates short-term surpluses of lenders with the short-term cash needs of borrowers, and it is the first point at which the central bank's monetary policy is transmitted to the rest of the financial system.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of the money market as a giant overnight parking lot for cash. Banks, companies and the government with extra cash for a few days drive in and lend it; those who need cash for a few days drive in and borrow it. The "rent" is the short-term interest rate — and the RBI is the parking attendant who can change rates to influence the whole economy.

### Key Concepts

#### Money market vs capital market
The financial market splits into (i) the **money market** for short-term funds (≤ 1 year) and (ii) the **capital market** for long-term funds. Both connect surplus sectors (households, institutions) with deficit sectors (corporates, government), but the money market emphasises **liquidity and safety**, while the capital market emphasises **return and growth**.

| Aspect | Money Market | Capital Market |
|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Maturity | ≤ 1 year (overnight to 364 days) | > 1 year (long term) |
| Instruments | T-bills, CPs, CDs, call money, repo | Shares, bonds, debentures |
| Returns | Lower, but high liquidity & safety | Higher, with more risk |
| Function | Parks short-term surplus / meets short-term deficit | Mobilises long-term capital |
| Influence | Sets short-term interest rates; transmits monetary policy | Funds investment, growth |

#### Distinctive features (six)
1. **Negotiable instruments** — used by companies to raise funds; safe due to high liquidity.
2. **Short-term + low cost of turnover** — equilibrates short-term lender surpluses with borrower needs.
3. **A market of sub-markets** — call/notice/term money, repos, T-bills, commercial bills, CDs, CPs, inter-bank participation certificates, swaps, futures, options. Free flow of funds across sub-markets is a key requirement.
4. **Geographical concentration** — activities cluster in centres (London, New York globally; Mumbai in India). With screen-based trading, multiple centres rapidly integrate into one national market.
5. **Impersonal character** — no shops or counters; competition is relatively pure.
6. **Wholesale trading** — volumes traded are very large.

### Definitions
- **Money market**: Market for short-term money and financial assets that are close substitutes for money; in India, "short-term" = up to one year. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **RBI definition**: "The centre for dealings, mainly of short-term character, in money assets. It meets the short-term requirements of borrowers and provides liquidity or cash to the lenders." ⭐
- **Vaghul Committee definition**: "The money market is a market for short-term financial assets that are close substitutes for money. The important feature of money market instruments is that it is liquid and can be turned over quickly at low cost, and it provides an avenue for equilibrating the short-term surplus funds of lenders and the requirements of borrowers." ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Money market = short-term, liquid, low-cost, wholesale, over-the-phone.
> - Term "short-term" in India = up to 1 year.
> - Mumbai is the principal Indian money market centre.

## Section: Objectives and Functions of the Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The money market exists to (i) absorb surplus short-term funds, (ii) meet short-term deficits, (iii) give cheap quick access to short-term finance, and (iv) provide the central bank a venue to influence liquidity. It performs critical economy-wide functions — financing trade, transmitting monetary policy, supporting business working capital, and giving banks self-sufficiency.

### Key Concepts

#### Four objectives
1. A parking place to employ short-term surplus funds.
2. Room for overcoming short-term deficits.
3. Reasonable access to short-term funds quickly, adequately, and at reasonable cost.
4. The Central Bank's avenue to influence and regulate liquidity through intervention.

#### Five functions in the economy
1. **Financing trade** — discounts bills of exchange; finances local and international trade; supplies funds to agriculture and small-scale industries.
2. **Implementing central bank policies** — provides the operational base for monetary policy. An integrated money market lets RBI influence sub-markets and align liquidity & short-term rates with policy.
3. **Growth of business and industries** — corporates with surplus cash invest in short-term securities; corporates with shortfalls borrow. Capital market interest rates **benchmark off** money market rates.
4. **Self-sufficiency to commercial banks** — banks invest excess reserves at interest while keeping liquidity, and can borrow from the money market (often cheaper than the central bank) when liquidity-strapped.
5. **Other functions** — promotes savings/investment, supplies non-inflationary finance to the government via T-bills (issuing T-bills does not raise prices the way printing money would).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Compact list: equilibration mechanism, focal point for central-bank intervention, reasonable access at realistic interest rates.
> - Money market rates → benchmark for capital market rates.
> - T-bills = non-inflationary government finance.

## Section: Requisites of a Good Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
A "developed" money market satisfies seven structural conditions covering institutional depth, central-bank strength, mobility of funds, instrument variety, sub-market integration, settlement systems, and supervised participants. London and New York best embody these; Paris, Zurich, Frankfurt, Amsterdam and Vienna substantially do.

### Mechanisms / Processes
The seven requisites:
1. **Institutional development** — political stability + a well-organised commercial banking and financial system.
2. **Strong central bank** — credibility, supervision, ability to implement monetary policy via Open Market Operations.
3. **Financial mobility** — easy inter-sectoral fund flows.
4. **Continuous supply of negotiable securities** — bills of exchange, T-bills, short-term government bonds, with **varied maturity and risk profiles** to match different appetites. Multiple instruments add depth.
5. **Multiple integrated sub-markets** — each specialising in a particular short-term asset; funds must flow between segments to exploit arbitrage; integration ensures uniform interest rates and channels savings into productive working-capital uses.
6. **Integration with the rest of the financial system** — efficient payment & settlement (EFT, Depository System, Delivery-versus-Payment, High-Value Inter-bank Payment System).
7. **Integrity & supervision** — because deals are concluded over the phone with later written confirmation, integrity is *sine qua non*; participants must be licensed and supervised.

### Examples
- **Best examples** of developed money markets: London, New York.
- **Substantially developed**: Paris, Zurich, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, Vienna.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 7 requisites mnemonic: **Institutions, Central bank, Mobility, Variety, Sub-markets, Integration, Integrity**.
> - "Sine qua non" trait of money market: **integrity** (because trading is over the phone).

## Section: Money Market in India — Structure 🟡

### Core Idea
The Indian money market has a **dichotomic structure**: a tightly RBI-regulated organised sector coexists with a largely unregulated unorganised sector of indigenous bankers, moneylenders, finance companies, chit funds, and nidhis. The organised market is dominated by a small number of large players, with Mumbai as its leading centre.

### Key Concepts

#### Organised sector
- Fully under RBI control with standardised rules and high institutionalisation/instrumentalisation.
- Transactions are large-volume, dominated by few large players.
- Still **underdeveloped** by international standards.
- A **conglomeration of sub-markets**: call money market, bill market, and other sub-markets.
- **Leading centres**: Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, Delhi, Bangalore, Ahmedabad. Mumbai houses RBI HQ, two leading stock exchanges, the bullion exchange, and an organised G-sec market — putting it on par with London and New York.

### Definitions
- **Dichotomic structure**: Simultaneous existence of organised and unorganised money markets in India. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Indian money market = Organised (RBI-regulated) + Unorganised (indigenous).
> - Mumbai → at par with London & New York for Indian money market activity.

## Section: Instruments of the Organised Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The organised money market is a portfolio of instruments — call/notice/term money, T-bills, commercial bills (BRS), CDs, CPs, banker's acceptance, repos. Common features: **short duration, large volume, deregulated interest rates, high liquidity, high safety**. Each instrument plays a specific role in liquidity management.

### Key Concepts

#### Call / Notice / Term money market 🔴
- **Unsecured** segment for very short liquidity management.
- **Maturity tiers**:
  - Call money: overnight (up to 1 day).
  - Notice money: > 2 days, up to 14 days.
  - Term money: 15 days to 1 year.
- **Inter-bank market** — banks with surplus lend to needy banks; commonly used to meet **statutory reserve requirements** with the RBI.
- **No collateral, no brokers**; settlement via current accounts at RBI.
- Low documentation + same-day settlement → strong incentive for intra-day funding.
- Interest rates are **highly volatile** and considered the **barometer of the money market** — rates in other instruments are pegged to call rates.
- **Seasonality**: April, May, October → high rates and high volatility; January, August → lower and steady.
- **DFHI** (Discount and Finance House of India), set up 1988, was meant to broaden the call market and act as **market maker**, but has limited resources.
- Mumbai handles **85–90%** of call business; other centres: Kolkata, Delhi, Chennai, Bengaluru, Ahmedabad.
- **Major players**: commercial banks + DFHI (both borrow and lend); UTI, LIC, IDBI, NABARD permitted **as lenders only**.

#### Treasury bills (T-bills) 🔴
- Short-term **government** security; promissory notes; sold by RBI on behalf of Government of India.
- Three current maturities: **91 days, 182 days, 364 days**.
- **Zero-coupon**: pay no interest. Issued at a discount, redeemed at face value at maturity.
- **No default risk** — government paper.
- **Approved SLR assets** for banks.
- Sold by **auction to highest bidder**; **price-based, multiple-price** auctions.
- DFHI is the market maker.
- Best instrument for **hedging against call-loan-rate volatility**.

**Worked example (T-bill discount):**
- 91-day T-bill, face value ₹100, issued at ₹98.20.
- Discount = ₹100 − ₹98.20 = ₹1.80.
- Investor's return = redemption value − issue price = ₹100 − ₹98.20 = ₹1.80 over 91 days.

#### Bills Rediscounting Scheme (BRS) — commercial bill market 🟡
- Operative since **1970**.
- Commercial banks issue **derivative usance promissory notes** (≤ 90 days maturity) **backed by genuine trade bills** of similar tenor.
- These can be **rediscounted** by the holder via endorsement and delivery.
- **Stamp duty waived** on usance promissory notes since **1989**.
- Eligible issuers: all commercial banks; eligible discounters: commercial banks, select financial institutions, urban cooperative banks.
- DFHI provides daily bid rates → assures liquidity.
- **Major source of funds for banks**: borrowing under BRS is **not counted as a liability** when calculating Net Demand and Time Liability (NDTL).
- Attractive for investors due to high liquidity, safety, and returns.

### Definitions
- **Call money** ⭐: Unsecured, overnight (up to 1 day) inter-bank lending for very short-term liquidity management.
- **Notice money** ⭐: 2–14 day money.
- **Term money** ⭐: 15 days to 1 year money.
- **Treasury bill (T-bill)** ⭐: Short-term zero-coupon government security (91/182/364 days), issued at discount and redeemed at face value.
- **DFHI**: Discount and Finance House of India (1988) — market maker in call and T-bill markets. ⭐
- **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)**: Portion of total deposits a commercial bank must keep in government or government-approved securities.
- **NDTL (Net Demand and Time Liability)**: Bank's net liability base used by RBI for reserve calculations.
- **Usance promissory note**: A promissory note payable at a fixed period after sight or after a specified date (used as the legal vehicle in BRS).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treasury bills carry coupon interest. → ✅ Correct: T-bills are **zero-coupon**; the return is the discount on issue price.
- ❌ Mistake: Call money is collateralised. → ✅ Correct: It is **unsecured**, no collateral, no brokers.
- ❌ Mistake: Notice money = overnight money. → ✅ Correct: Notice money = > 2 days up to 14 days; **call** = overnight.
- ❌ Mistake: All RBI-issued money market instruments pay interest. → ✅ Correct: T-bills pay no interest — they are issued at a discount.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Call < Notice < Term: ≤1 day | 2–14 days | 15 d–1 yr.
> - T-bill maturities: **91 / 182 / 364 days** (zero-coupon, multiple-price auction).
> - BRS bills ≤ 90 days; backing: genuine trade bills; stamp duty waived 1989.
> - Call rate = barometer of money market; Mumbai ≈ 85–90% of call business.
> - DFHI established 1988 as market maker.

## Section: Other Sub-Market Instruments — CDs, CPs, Banker's Acceptance, Repos 🔴

<!-- See chunk 001 for start of "Instruments of Organised Markets" section. Chunk 001 ended mid-section after BRS. -->

### Core Idea
Beyond call money, T-bills and BRS, the organised money market deepens through **Certificates of Deposit (CDs)**, **Commercial Papers (CPs)**, **Banker's Acceptance**, and **Repurchase Options (Repos)**. Each tackles a distinct short-term need: bulk deposit-raising for banks, working-capital finance for corporates, trade-payment guarantee, and collateralised short-term liquidity respectively.

### Key Concepts

#### Certificate of Deposit (CD) 🔴
- Document **of title to a time deposit**, distinguished from a conventional time deposit by its **free negotiability**.
- Issued by **banks** (Commercial banks initially; **RRBs from 2021**) to raise short-term cash.
- **Term**: generally 3 months to 5 years; restricts demand-withdrawal but money can be withdrawn early on penalty.
- **Transferable** by endorsement and delivery, but only **after 45 days** from date of issue.
- Issued **at a discount** (front-end or rear-end); discount rate is **market-determined**.
- Returns are **higher than T-bills** (because higher risk than sovereign).
- Introduced in Indian money market in **March 1989**.
- Important source of **bulk fund mobilisation at low operational cost** for banks.
- **Return method matters**: Annual Percentage Yield (APY, compound) vs Annual Percentage Rate (APR, simple).
  - If interest is paid **once a year**, APY = APR.
  - If interest is paid **more than once a year**, APY > APR → **prefer APY**.

#### Commercial Paper (CP) 🔴
- **Usance promissory note** issued by a **corporate** at a discount, with fixed maturity, evidencing the issuer's short-term obligation.
- **Maturity**: 1 to 270 days (typical), used for accounts receivables, inventories, short-term liabilities.
- Introduced in India in **1989**.
- **Bypasses bank intermediation** → low-cost alternative to bank loans.
- **Unsecured**, not zero-risk; only **firms with high credit ratings** find buyers easily without offering large discounts.
- Yields **higher returns than T-bills** (less secure).
- Issued by corporates and financial institutions; freely transferable in **demat form**; actively traded in **secondary market**.

**Worked example (CP):**
- Company has ₹1 crore receivables, 6-month credit period; needs cash now.
- Issues 6-month CP at **10% discount** on ₹1 crore face value.
- Buyer pays ₹95 lakh now, receives ₹1 crore at maturity → buyer earns **₹5 lakh interest over 6 months**.
- Company immediately liquidates its receivables.

#### Banker's Acceptance 🟡
- Money-market instrument for short-term liquidity.
- Company gives a **bank guarantee** to a seller to pay the agreed amount on a future date.
- If buyer defaults, seller invokes the bank guarantee.
- Typically used to **finance exports and imports**.

#### Repurchase Options (Repo) / Ready Forward (RF) 🔴
- Borrower places acceptable security with the lender against funds received and **agrees to reverse the transaction** on a predetermined future date at an agreed interest cost.
- **No fixed period prescribed**; in practice **min 14 days, max 1 year**.
- Interest is **market-determined**, built into the repo structure.
- Eligible parties: commercial banks, financial institutions, brokers, DFHI.
- Borrower advantage: raises funds against securities **without altering asset mix**.
- Lender advantage: safe, attractive return; predictable reversal date eases fund management.

### Definitions
- **Certificate of Deposit (CD)** ⭐: Negotiable bank-issued title to a time deposit, issued at discount, market-determined rate.
- **Commercial Paper (CP)** ⭐: Unsecured short-term usance promissory note issued by a corporate at a discount.
- **Banker's Acceptance**: Bank guarantee given to a seller on behalf of a buyer, mostly for export-import finance.
- **Repo / Ready Forward**: Sale of securities with agreed repurchase at a future date and agreed price, used for short-term collateralised borrowing.
- **APY (Annual Percentage Yield)**: Compound-interest based annual return.
- **APR (Annual Percentage Rate)**: Simple-interest based annual return.

#### CD vs CP — comparison

| Aspect | Certificate of Deposit (CD) | Commercial Paper (CP) |
|--------|------------------------------|------------------------|
| Issuer | Banks (Commercial; RRBs since 2021) | Corporates / financial institutions |
| Underlying | Title to time deposit | Unsecured promissory note |
| Tenor | 3 months – 5 years | 1 – 270 days |
| Purpose | Bulk fund mobilisation by bank | Working-capital, bypass bank loans |
| Risk | Bank-backed, lower | Corporate, slightly higher |
| Transfer | After 45 days, by endorsement & delivery | Freely transferable, demat |
| Introduced | March 1989 | 1989 |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - CD: bank-issued, ≥ 45 days lock before transfer, APY > APR if compounded.
> - CP: corporate-issued, 1–270 days, only top-rated firms get attractive pricing.
> - Repo: collateralised, min 14 days, max 1 year, market-determined rate.
> - Banker's acceptance: bank's guarantee for trade payment; export/import finance.

## Section: The Unorganised Money Market 🟡

### Core Idea
The unorganised market consists of **indigenous bankers, unregulated non-bank intermediaries (finance companies, chit funds, nidhis), and moneylenders** who lend to retailers, small traders, farmers, artisans and the poor at high rates outside RBI control. Despite RBI's 1991 NBFC directions (Reserve Bank Directions, 1998), regulation remains inadequate and instruments are non-standardised.

### Key Concepts

#### Indigenous Bankers
- Individuals/private firms acting like mini banks (deposits + loans).
- Activities **unregulated** and uninfluenced by the RBI bank rate.
- Footprint shrunk with growth of commercial and cooperative banks; now a few thousand operate, mainly in western & southern India.
- **Four sub-groups**:

| Sub-group | Main location |
|-----------|---------------|
| Gujarati Shroffs | Mumbai, Kolkata, Gujarat industrial/trading cities — **most powerful by volume** |
| Multani / Shikarpuri Shroffs | Mumbai, Chennai |
| Chettiars | Mostly South India |
| Marwari Shroffs | Mumbai, Kolkata, tea gardens of Assam, parts of NE India |

- Mostly mix banking and non-banking business; charge **high interest rates**.

#### Unregulated Non-Bank Financial Intermediaries
- **Finance companies**: collect substantial deposits/borrowings; lend to wholesalers, retailers, artisans, self-employed at **36–48% p.a.**
- **Chit funds**: spread across states, concentrated in **Tamil Nadu and Kerala**.
- **Nidhis**: South-Indian mutual benefit funds, restricted to members.

#### Moneylenders
- Advance loans to small/marginal farmers, agricultural labour, artisans, factory/mine workers, low-paid staff, small traders at very high rates; common malpractices in record-keeping.
- **Three types**:
  1. Professional moneylenders.
  2. Itinerant moneylenders (Kabulis, Pathans).
  3. Non-professional moneylenders.
- Localised, non-uniform methods; **unregulated, unsupervised**, leading to exploitation. A "necessary evil" given inadequate institutional credit access.

### Definitions
- **Indigenous bankers** ⭐: Private individuals/firms taking deposits and lending — operate outside RBI regulation.
- **Nidhi**: South-Indian mutual benefit fund restricted to members.

#### Indigenous Bankers vs Moneylenders — comparison

| Aspect | Indigenous Bankers | Moneylenders |
|--------|---------------------|---------------|
| Activity | Deposits + loans (mini-bank) | Loans only |
| Borrowers | Traders, businesses | Small farmers, labour, poor |
| Subgroups | Gujarati, Multani, Chettiar, Marwari | Professional, Itinerant, Non-professional |
| Rates | High | Very high + malpractices |
| Regulation | Not regulated | Not regulated |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Unorganised market = Indigenous bankers + Non-bank intermediaries + Moneylenders.
> - 4 indigenous-banker groups; **Gujarati Shroffs** = most powerful.
> - Finance companies' rates: **36–48% p.a.**
> - Chit funds concentrated in **Tamil Nadu and Kerala**.

## Section: Regulatory Measures to Streamline the Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
India's money market has been overhauled by reforms initiated through three landmark committees and a series of RBI/SEBI policy measures since the late 1980s — adding institutions (DFHI, SEBI, credit rating agencies), introducing newer instruments (CDs, CPs, MMMFs), deregulating interest rates from **1 May 1989**, and integrating the money market with foreign exchange, capital and gilt markets.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Regulators**: RBI **and** SEBI jointly regulate Indian money markets.

**Three landmark committees:**

| Committee | Year | Mandate |
|-----------|------|---------|
| **Chakravarty Committee** | 1985 | Improvement of the monetary system |
| **Vaghul Working Group** | 1986 | Activate and vitalise the money market |
| **Narasimham Committee** | 1991 | Streamline the financial system |

**Institutional reforms:**
1. **DFHI** established **1988** — broadens and deepens money market; market maker.
2. **SEBI** set up **1992** — apex regulator for security markets.
3. **Credit rating agencies** promoted — **CRISIL (1987), ICRA (1991)**, etc. — to ease fund raising in the money market.

**Instrument & rate reforms:**
- New sub-markets introduced: CDs, call money, collateral loan, commercial paper, MFs (MMMFs).
- **Interest-rate deregulation from 1 May 1989** → market-determined rates.
- Apex agencies: RBI, DFHI, **STCI (Securities Trading Corporation of India)**.
- Operating level: commercial banks, public financial institutions, selected corporates.
- Banks: can both **buy and sell** instruments; PFIs and corporates: typically only **lending** subject to conditions.
- **CRR and SLR** are used as credit-control tools to tone money market operations in the commercial banking sector.

**Repo reforms:**
- **November 1996**: Repos (ready forward) reintroduced — to mop up excess liquidity, reduce volatility in call money and forex markets.
- **21 October 1997**: Repo facility extended to **corporate debt and PSU bonds**.
- RBI introduced **reverse repo** for primary dealers in G-sec at the bank rate, on a discretionary basis.

**Macroeconomic role of money market rates:**
- Higher rates → reduce liquidity → reduce economic activity.
- Lower rates → raise liquidity, lower cost of capital → raise investment.
- Helps RBI control general money supply and **inflation**.

### Definitions
- **CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)**: Share of bank deposits to be held as reserves with the RBI (referenced as a credit-control tool).
- **MMMF (Money Market Mutual Fund)**: Fund pooling investors' money to invest in money-market instruments.
- **Reverse repo**: RBI's facility to absorb liquidity by selling securities with agreement to buy back later.
- **STCI (Securities Trading Corporation of India)**: Apex agency dealing in money market instruments.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three committees: **Chakravarty (1985) → Vaghul (1986) → Narasimham (1991)**.
> - DFHI 1988, SEBI 1992, CRISIL 1987, ICRA 1991.
> - **Interest rates deregulated 1 May 1989**.
> - Repo reintroduced **Nov 1996**; extended to corporate debt/PSU bonds **21 Oct 1997**.

## Section: Limitations of the Indian Money Market 🟡

### Core Idea
Despite reforms, the Indian money market remains **less developed than world standards** — characterised by inadequate short-term assets, dichotomy between organised and unorganised sectors, lack of intermediaries, weak rural banking penetration, and disparate interest rates.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Key drawbacks:
1. **Inadequate / discontinuous supply of short-term assets** (T-bills, bills of exchange, short-term G-secs).
2. **No dedicated short-term assets dealers** acting as intermediaries between government and the banking system.
3. **Underdeveloped bill market** despite RBI efforts.
4. **Lack of coordination** among constituents → fails to attract **foreign funds**.
5. **Dichotomy** between organised and unorganised sectors makes uniform monetary-policy implementation difficult.
6. **Wasteful competition** within and between sectors.
7. **Inadequate banking institutions** relative to country size; rural areas under-served.
8. **Disparities in interest rates** disrupt smooth functioning.

### Examples
- DFHI and MMMFs cited as recent improvements that have advanced the Indian money market.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Organised vs unorganised dichotomy = **biggest structural weakness**.
> - Bill market still **underdeveloped** despite RBI push.
> - Reform progress = DFHI + MMMFs + interest deregulation.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Indian money market is fully integrated. → ✅ Correct: It still has a **dichotomic** structure with weak coordination.
- ❌ Mistake: Money market interest rates are administered. → ✅ Correct: Deregulated and **market-determined since 1 May 1989**.

## Section: Summary, Key Words, and Wrap-up 🟢

### Core Idea
The money market is the high-liquidity, ≤1-year debt segment of the financial system. It enables governments, banks and large institutions to fund short-term cash flows; lets individuals invest small amounts in low-risk assets; and gives the central bank a venue to align market rates with the policy rate. Capital markets (long-term) sit alongside it.

### Key Concepts

#### Key Words
- **Bill of Exchange**: Negotiable instrument with three parties — drawer, drawee, payee. A commercial bank cheque is an example (a draft on the bank by the depositor payable to a designated payee).
- **Credit Controls**: Regulatory restrictions on bank lending.
- **Financial Integration**: How financial markets are tied together geographically (domestically and internationally).
- **Market Maker**: Dealer who buys/sells from inventory ensuring continuous market for investors.
- **Monetary Policy**: Management of money supply and its links to prices, interest rates, and other economic variables.
- **Open Market Operations (OMO)**: Purchase and sale of securities by the central bank in financial markets.
- **Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)**: Established 1975 under the **Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976**, to develop the rural economy.
- **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: Share of total deposits a commercial bank must keep in government or government-approved securities.
- **Underwriters**: Intermediaries who subscribe to the unsubscribed portion of a company's issued securities.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - SLR is the part kept in **G-secs** (vs CRR which is cash with RBI).
> - RRBs: 1975, statutory framework via Regional Rural Banks Act 1976.
> - Bill of exchange: drawer / drawee / payee.

## Section: Purpose and Uses of Capital Markets 🔴

### Core Idea
Capital markets are the mechanism that brings together buyers and sellers of long-term financial assets so prices are formed for them. They link investors (postponing consumption for higher returns) with borrowers (firms, governments, individuals) who need financing — through debt instruments (bonds, debentures) or equity (shares). They make information transparent, enable quick valuation, lower transaction/search costs, and integrate sectors of the economy.

> **In Simple Terms:** If money markets are the parking lot for cash, capital markets are the housing market — buyers and sellers meet to exchange long-term claims on businesses and governments. The price you pay for a share or bond reflects what the market thinks that future cash stream is worth.

### Key Concepts

#### Capital market structure
- **Money market** — markets for short-term funds (≤1 year).
- **Capital market** — markets for long-term funds (> 1 year).
- Capital markets split into:
  - **Debt markets** — long-term debt contracts (bonds, debentures).
  - **Equity markets** — financial claims on a firm's profits (shares).
- Each splits into:
  - **Primary market** — where new issues are first sold.
  - **Secondary market** — where existing issues are traded.

#### Borrowers and lenders
- **Borrowers**: individuals (mortgages, durables, education), companies (cash-flow + growth), governments (national debt), public-sector firms, foreign agencies.
- **Lenders**: individuals, financial institutions, companies (with idle cash, in money markets).
- **Securities** are receipts (financial claims) issued by borrowers to lenders. They state amount owed, repayment date, and interest rate.

#### Five functions of capital markets
1. Mechanism for matching buyers and sellers of financial assets and **determining their prices**.
2. Make **information processing and dissemination** easier — better investment/sale decisions.
3. Enable **quick valuation** of debt and equity instruments.
4. Provide **operational efficiency** by simplifying transactions and lowering search/transaction costs.
5. Develop **integration**: real ↔ financial sector, debt ↔ equity, short-term ↔ long-term, domestic ↔ foreign funds; channel funds into efficient uses.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Capital market = long-term funds; Money market = short-term funds.
> - Primary = new issues; Secondary = trading of existing issues.
> - 5 functions: matching, information, valuation, efficiency, integration.

## Section: Debt vs Equity as Means of Raising Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
A firm financing its assets must choose how much to raise via **debt** (borrowing) and how much via **equity** (issuing shares). This **capital-structure** decision is informed by financial-ratio analysis across four families: **liquidity, activity, financial leverage, profitability**. Each family answers a different stakeholder concern (creditor, shareholder, manager).

### Key Concepts

#### Capital structure
- If the firm is entirely **equity-financed**, all cash flows go to shareholders.
- If the firm uses **debt + equity**, lenders get risk-free claims first; shareholders get the residual.
- The **capital structure** = mix of debt and equity used.

#### Stakeholders and their concerns
| Stakeholder | Primary concern |
|-------------|------------------|
| Stockholders, bondholders | Long-term profitability |
| Creditors | Whether firm pays on time (short-term solvency) |
| Management | Long-, short-, and day-to-day performance |

#### Four families of ratios

| Family | What it measures |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Liquidity** | Ability of current assets to meet current liabilities |
| **Activity** | Efficiency of resource use |
| **Financial leverage** | Effectiveness of debt management |
| **Profitability** | Ability to generate income relative to sales / assets / equity |

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Liquidity ratios
- **Current ratio** = Current Assets / Current Liabilities.
- **Quick (acid-test) ratio** = (Current Assets − Inventories) / Current Liabilities.
  - Excludes inventories because they are the **least liquid** current asset.

#### Activity ratios
- **Accounts receivable turnover** = Net Credit Sales / Receivables.
- **Average collection period** = (365 × Receivables) / Net Credit Sales.
- **Total asset turnover** = Net Sales / Total Assets.

#### Financial leverage ratios (a.k.a. "gearing" in Britain)
- More debt-financed operations → more risk.
- **Debt ratio** = Total Liabilities / Total Assets.
- **Debt-equity ratio** = Total Liabilities / Equities.
- **Identity** (since Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Equity):
  - **Debt-equity ratio = Debt ratio / (1 − Debt ratio)**.
- **Cash flow coverage** (from income statement) =
  (EBIT + Lease/Rental Payments + Depreciation) / [Interest + Lease/Rental Payments + Principal Repayment / (1 − T)]
  - EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes; T = tax rate.

#### Profitability ratios
- **Gross profit margin** = Gross Profits / Net Sales.
- **Net profit margin** = Net Income / Net Sales.
- **Operating profit margin** = EBIT / Net Sales.
- **Return on Assets (ROA / ROI)** = Net Income / Total Assets.
- **Return on Equity (ROE)** = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity.

### Definitions
- **Capital structure** ⭐: Mix of debt and equity used to finance the firm's assets.
- **Working capital**: Current Assets − Current Liabilities.
- **Leverage / Gearing**: Firm's ability to service its debt; the more debt-financed, the higher the leverage and risk.
- **EBIT**: Earnings Before Interest and Taxes.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Quick ratio includes inventories. → ✅ Correct: Quick ratio **excludes** inventories.
- ❌ Mistake: Debt-equity ratio uses only long-term debt. → ✅ Correct: It uses **total liabilities** (current + long-term).
- ❌ Mistake: A high ROE always means a healthy firm. → ✅ Correct: ROE can be inflated by **high leverage**, which raises risk.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 ratio families: **Liquidity, Activity, Leverage, Profitability**.
> - Quick = (CA − Inv) / CL.
> - D/E = D / (1 − D).
> - ROE > ROA ⇒ leverage benefit.

## Section: Debt Market Instruments and Their Pricing 🔴

### Core Idea
The main long-term debt instruments are **bonds** — fixed-income securities paying periodic interest and principal at maturity. **Debentures** are bonds with a clause to convert into equity. Beyond plain-vanilla bonds, modern markets have evolved zero-coupon, floating-rate, embedded-option, and commodity-linked bonds. Pricing rests on the **present value of expected cash flows**, with three yield measures (current yield, yield to maturity, yield to call).

### Key Concepts

#### Bond types

| Type | Distinguishing feature |
|------|-------------------------|
| Plain vanilla / straight | Fixed coupon over life, principal at maturity |
| Zero-coupon | No coupon; issued at large discount, redeemed at face value (sometimes with call/put options) |
| Floating-rate | Coupon linked to a benchmark (e.g., T-bill rate) |
| Embedded options | Convertible (debenture), callable (issuer can redeem early), puttable (investor can sell back early) |
| Commodity-linked | Return tied to specified commodity prices |

- Issuers: Government of India (largest borrower; sells short/medium-term bonds via RBI), state governments, government agencies (with guarantee), public-sector firms, financial institutions, private-sector firms.

#### Bond valuation (assuming risk-free, fixed coupon, annual payments, par redemption)

**Bond value formula:**

$$A = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{X}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{M}{(1+r)^T}$$

- A = bond value (₹)
- T = number of years
- X = annual coupon payment (₹)
- r = required return (discount rate)
- M = maturity (face) value
- t = period when cash flow received

#### Price–yield and price–time relationships
- **Price ↔ yield: inverse**. As yield rises, present value falls, so price falls.
- **Price ↔ time: convergence to par**. Bond price must equal par at maturity (assuming no default risk).
  - **Premium bond** — current price (before redemption) > par.
  - **Discount bond** — current price (before redemption) < par.

#### Bond yield measures

| Measure | Formula / definition | Limitation |
|---------|----------------------|-------------|
| **Current Yield** | Annual Interest / Price | Ignores capital gain/loss and time value of money |
| **Yield to Maturity (YTM)** | Discount rate r solving $P = \sum X/(1+r)^t + M/(1+r)^T$ | Often needs trial-and-error |
| **Yield to Call (YTC)** | Same as YTM but uses call price (instead of M) and call date (instead of T) | Only relevant for callable bonds |

### Definitions
- **Bond** ⭐: Long-term debt instrument paying periodic interest and principal at maturity.
- **Debenture** ⭐: Bond with a clause to convert into equity on pre-specified terms.
- **Plain vanilla / straight bond**: Bond with fixed coupon and principal repayment at maturity.
- **Zero-coupon bond**: Issued at deep discount, redeemed at face value, no coupons.
- **Floating-rate bond**: Coupon tied to a benchmark interest rate.
- **Callable bond**: Issuer can redeem early.
- **Puttable bond**: Investor can sell back early.
- **Yield to Maturity (YTM)** ⭐: Discount rate that equates a bond's PV of cash flows to its current price.
- **Yield to Call**: Yield computed assuming bond is held until the call date.
- **Current Yield**: Annual coupon / market price; ignores capital gain and time value.

### Examples
- In India, the **Government of India** is the largest borrower; bonds are sold through RBI.
- State governments and government agencies (with guarantee) also issue bonds.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Bond price = PV of all future cash flows.
> - **Price ↔ Yield: inverse**.
> - At maturity, bond price = par (if no default).
> - 3 yields: **Current Yield, YTM, YTC**.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Current yield includes capital gains. → ✅ Correct: It uses **only annual interest / price**.
- ❌ Mistake: Higher coupon always means higher YTM. → ✅ Correct: YTM depends on **price + cash flows**, not coupon alone.

## Section: Equity Markets — Introduction 🟡

### Core Idea
A stock exchange regulates company listings, forms prices, supervises trading, authorises members, settles transactions, and publishes trade data and prices. Equity markets are split into **primary** (new issues) and **secondary** (existing issues). A handful of capital concepts — authorised, issued, paid-up, par value, equity premium, book value, market value — describe how shareholders' capital and its valuation work.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine a company as a pizza. The "authorised capital" is the maximum number of slices you're allowed to bake; "issued capital" is the slices you actually brought to the table; "paid-up capital" is what people have already paid for. The price someone is willing to pay you for a slice today is its **market value**; the company's accounting basis for one slice is its **book value**.

### Key Concepts

#### Capital concepts (equity)

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Authorised capital** | Maximum capital a company can issue |
| **Issued capital** | Capital the company offers to investors |
| **Paid-up capital** | Capital subscribed and paid by investors (assuming no calls outstanding) |
| **Par / face value** | Equity capital ÷ number of equity shares |
| **Equity premium** | Issue price − Par value (when issue price > par) |
| **Book / intrinsic value** | (Paid-up capital + Reserves & Surplus) / Number of shares = Net Assets / Number of shares |
| **Market value** | Price at which the share is traded in the market |

#### Primary vs secondary equity markets

| Aspect | Primary | Secondary |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| What is sold | New issues | Already-issued shares |
| Where funds go | Issuer receives | Original buyer receives |
| Function | Capital raising | Liquidity and price discovery |

#### Valuation ratios
- **Price-Earnings (P/E) ratio** = Market price per share / Earnings per share.
  - **Earnings per share (EPS)** = (After-tax profit − Preference dividend) / Number of equity shares.
  - Reflects risk, sentiment, liquidity, and growth prospects.
  - Higher P/E → higher earning per share → may increase share demand.
- **Yield (total return)** = (Dividend + Price change) / Initial price.
  - Decomposable as **Dividend yield + Capital-gains/loss yield**.
  - Low-growth firms → high dividend yield, low capital gains; opposite for high-growth firms.
- **Market Value to Book Value ratio** = MV per share / BV per share.
  - > 1 ⇒ wealth has been generated.
  - = 1 ⇒ Earnings/Price ratio (= 1/PE), Yield, and Return on Equity all coincide.

### Definitions
- **Authorised capital** ⭐: Maximum capital a company is allowed to issue.
- **Issued capital** ⭐: Capital offered to investors.
- **Paid-up capital** ⭐: Subscribed capital actually paid (assuming no outstanding calls).
- **Par value (face value)** ⭐: Equity capital ÷ number of equity shares.
- **Equity premium** ⭐: Issue price − par value when issue price > par.
- **Book value (intrinsic value) of a share** ⭐: (Paid-up capital + Reserves & Surplus) / Number of equity shares.
- **Market value**: Trading price.
- **EPS**: After-tax profit − preference dividend, divided by number of equity shares.
- **P/E ratio**: Market price per share / EPS.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Authorised ≥ Issued ≥ Paid-up.
> - Equity premium triggered only when **issue price > par**.
> - Book value uses paid-up + reserves; market value is what the market pays.
> - Total yield = dividend yield + capital-gains yield.

## Section: Tobin's q & Functions of Secondary Markets 🟡

<!-- See chunk 003 for start of "Equity: Markets and Volatility". This continues that section. -->

### Core Idea
Beyond simple Market-to-Book, **Tobin's q** values a firm by relating market value of *all* claims (equity + liabilities) to the *replacement cost* of its assets. Secondary markets serve issuers (price feedback) and investors (liquidity, escape valve), and they reduce search and transaction costs.

### Key Concepts

#### Tobin's q ratio (named after Nobel laureate James Tobin)
$$q = \frac{\text{Market value of equities and liabilities}}{\text{Estimated replacement cost of assets}}$$

| Difference vs Market/Book | Tobin's q |
|---------------------------|-----------|
| Numerator includes equity only | Includes equity **+ liabilities** (all wealth) |
| Denominator uses **book value** | Uses **replacement cost** of assets |

- **Interpretation:** q > 1 ⇒ market values the firm's claims above what it would cost to rebuild it ⇒ wealth generated; q < 1 ⇒ the opposite.

#### Functions of secondary markets

**To the issuer:**
- **Price information**: latest prices reveal what the market thinks of the firm; helps the issuer evaluate the use of past primary-market funds and gauge appetite for new issues.

**To investors:**
- **Escape clause**: original IPO buyers can sell for cash if the share underperforms — or to lock in capital gains.
- Without secondary-market liquidity, investors would demand higher returns/dividends → harder for firms to raise capital.

**General economic functions:**
- Provide **fair price information** and **liquidity**.
- Reduce **search costs** by matching buyers and sellers.
- Reduce **transaction costs**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Tobin's q numerator = equity + liabilities; denominator = **replacement** cost (not book).
> - Secondary markets: price info + liquidity + lower search/transaction costs.

## Section: Stock-Market Health Indicators — Volatility, Liquidity, Size, Cost 🔴

### Core Idea
A stock market's developmental maturity is gauged across four parameters: **volatility** (price fluctuation frequency), **liquidity** (trade volume without large price impact), **size** (market capitalisation as % of GDP), and **transaction cost** (explicit + implicit). High volatility deters investors; high liquidity, large size, low costs all signal a developed market.

### Key Concepts

#### Volatility
- Measure of how frequently a stock's price changes over time.
- High volatility → investors avoid; **affects outside fund flows** → macroeconomic dimension.
- Drivers: speculation, trading & settlement systems, government policies, interest rates, inflation, regulatory framework.

#### Liquidity (two measures)
| Measure | Formula | Captures |
|---------|---------|----------|
| **Turnover ratio** | Total value of domestic shares traded / Total market capitalisation | Trading activity relative to market size |
| **Value-traded ratio** | Total value of domestic shares traded on major exchanges / GDP | Macro-level liquidity (organised trading as share of GDP) |

- **High turnover** often signals **low transaction costs**.
- A large market with low trading volume can still have low turnover.

#### Size of the market
- **Market capitalisation ratio** = Market capitalisation / GDP.
  - Market capitalisation = market price × number of equity shares (for all listed firms).
- Assumption: market size positively correlates with capital-mobilising and risk-diversifying ability.

#### Transaction costs
- **Explicit**: brokerage, stamp duty, commission.
- **Implicit**: clearing & settlement costs, bad delivery, paperwork.
- Low costs → higher trading volume → higher value-traded ratio (a positive feedback loop).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 parameters: **Volatility, Liquidity, Size, Cost**.
> - 2 liquidity measures: **Turnover ratio** (vs market cap) and **Value-traded ratio** (vs GDP).
> - Size measure: **Market capitalisation ratio** (cap / GDP).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: A larger market always means high liquidity. → ✅ Correct: Size and liquidity are different — a large market with low trading is illiquid.
- ❌ Mistake: Tobin's q = Market/Book. → ✅ Correct: q's numerator includes **liabilities**, and the denominator uses **replacement cost**.

## Section: Indices of Share Prices 🔴

### Core Idea
A stock-price index uses a representative basket of stocks to track market behaviour. Good indices use **high market capitalisation + high liquidity** stocks. Indices can be **price averages** (Dow Jones, Nikkei 225 — equal weighting / arithmetic or geometric mean) or **price indices weighted by market cap** (BSE Sensex, NSE Nifty). Within market-cap weighting, refinements include **full**, **free-float**, and **modified** capitalisation methods.

### Key Concepts

#### Characteristics of a good index
1. Stocks have **high market capitalisation**.
2. Stocks are **highly liquid**.

Both ensure the index reflects movements in shares that are actively traded and economically significant.

#### How indices are calculated
- For a given day, the index = **percentage of aggregate market value of selected stocks today** to **average market value of the same stocks during the base period**.
- Example: BSE Sensex = weighted average of prices of **30 select stocks**.
- Top constituents change as share prices change → index composition is dynamic.

#### Price averages vs price indices
| Method | Examples |
|--------|----------|
| **Price averages** | Dow Jones (started 1884 by Charles Dow with 12 stocks; 30 since 1928), Nikkei Dow 225 (Japan, 225 shares — average prices, not market cap), Financial Times Ordinary Share Index (1935 — geometric mean of 30 shares) |
| **Modern indices (multiply price × shares)** | Give more weight to large-cap firms |

#### Two main calculation categories
1. **Equal-weighted index** — simple arithmetic mean of sample shares relative to base.
2. **Value-weighted index** — aggregate market capitalisation relative to base.

#### Sub-classification of market-cap weighted methods

| Method | What it does | Notes |
|--------|---------------|-------|
| **Full market capitalisation** | Number of outstanding shares × market price | Largest firms dominate. **BSE Sensex and Nifty followed this initially** |
| **Free-float method** | Considers only **freely available shares** — excludes strategic & ESOP-locked stakes | Closely-held companies → lower weight; popular globally |
| **Modified capitalisation** | Caps the influence of largest stocks | Prevents disproportionate dominance |

#### Other methods
- **Price-weighted index** — sum of prices, related to base value.
- **Equal weighting** — each stock weighs equally.

#### Major indices

**India:**
| Index | Started | Method | Constituents |
|-------|---------|--------|---------------|
| **BSE Sensex (S&P BSE Sensex)** | 1986 | Free-float, market-weighted | 30 large, financially sound BSE-listed firms |
| **NSE Nifty (S&P CNX Nifty)** | 8 July 1996, with CRISIL & S&P | Weighted average (proportional to market cap) | 50 scrips selected on low impact cost, high liquidity, market cap |
| Economic Times Ordinary Share Price Index | — | — | — |
| Financial Express Ordinary Price Index | — | — | — |
| RBI Index of Security Prices | — | — | — |
| Regional indices (Ahmedabad, Chennai, Delhi, Kolkata) | — | — | — |

- **NSE** started capital-market trading in **1994**; volume soon overtook BSE.

**Abroad:**
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average** (NYSE).
- **NASDAQ** (National Association of Securities Dealers Automatic Quotation).
- **Nikkei** (Japan).
- **Standard & Poor**.

### Definitions
- **Market capitalisation** ⭐: Sum of the market value of all stocks included in the index.
- **Liquidity** (in indexing context): Ease with which a share can be bought or sold near the current market price.
- **Free-float method** ⭐: Index methodology counting only freely tradable shares, excluding strategic and locked-in stakes.
- **Value-weighted index** ⭐: Index reflecting aggregate market capitalisation of constituents.
- **Equal-weighted index** ⭐: Simple arithmetic average of constituent share prices.
- **Coupon**: Periodic interest paid on a bond — face value × coupon rate. (Listed in unit's key words.)
- **Coupon rate**: Annual coupon / face value.

### Examples
- BSE Sensex = free-float, 30 stocks.
- Nifty = 50 scrips, weighted by market cap.
- Nikkei 225 = 225 shares, **average prices**, not cap.
- FT Ordinary Share Index = geometric mean of 30 shares.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Good index = **high market cap + high liquidity**.
> - 2 broad categories: **equal-weighted** vs **value-weighted**.
> - 3 cap-weight subtypes: **Full / Free-float / Modified**.
> - BSE Sensex (1986, free-float, 30 stocks); Nifty (1996, market-cap-weighted, 50 stocks).
> - Dow Jones since 1884; 30 stocks since 1928.

## Section: Meaning and Characteristics of Bonds 🔴

### Core Idea
A **bond** is a long-term debt instrument in which the issuer commits to pay a fixed or floating interest rate (coupon) and repay the principal after a fixed period. Bonds carry six defining characteristics: long-term tenor, par value, coupon, maturity, issue price (par/premium/discount), and redemption value (par or premium).

> **In Simple Terms:** A bond is a long-term IOU. You lend money now, you collect interest annually, and at maturity you get your principal back. The "rules" of the IOU — face value, interest rate, period — are fixed when the bond is issued.

### Key Concepts

#### Real-life issuance example
- **14 March 2023**: Tata Capital Financial Services Limited (TCFSL) issued ₹2,000 crore bonds, **8.3% p.a. coupon**, **2 years 11 months** maturity.
- TCFSL receives ₹2,000 crore upfront, pays ₹16,600 crore interest annually (note: text says "₹16,600" — this is annual interest in ₹, i.e., ₹16,600 per ₹2 lakh face value; equivalently 8.3% on ₹2,000 cr = ₹166 cr annual; preserving source figure).
- Principal repaid at end of 2 years 11 months.

#### Six characteristics of bonds
1. **Long-term instruments** — long-term debt.
2. **Par (face) value** — stated face value on certificate; **does not change**.
3. **Coupon** — fixed or floating interest, except in zero-coupon bonds; usually annual but can be semi-annual or quarterly; **calculated on face/par value**.
4. **Maturity period** — generally definite, sometimes indefinite (perpetual bonds).
5. **Issue price** — at par / premium (price > face) / discount (price < face).
6. **Redemption** — at par or premium; theoretically possible at discount but not feasible in practice (investor wouldn't accept less than invested).

### Definitions
- **Bond** ⭐: Long-term debt instrument with issuer obliged to pay fixed/floating interest and repay principal after a fixed period.
- **Par value (face value)** ⭐: Stated face value mentioned on the bond certificate; doesn't change over the bond's life.
- **Coupon (rate)** ⭐: Periodic interest, computed on par value.
- **Redemption at premium / par / discount** — based on whether redemption value > / = / < face value.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Issue price relations: **Premium > Par > Discount**.
> - Coupon is calculated on **face value**, not market price.
> - Zero-coupon bonds are the exception — they pay no periodic interest.

## Section: Classification of Bonds 🔴

### Core Idea
Bonds are classified along five axes: **issuer, coupon, maturity, option, convertibility**. Each axis produces standard subtypes investors and issuers use to match risk-return profiles.

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### 1) By issuer

| Subtype | Description | Indian examples |
|---------|-------------|------------------|
| **Government bonds (G-secs)** | Issued by central or state governments; majority of Indian bond market | Fixed-rate bonds, Floating-rate bonds, **Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs)**, Inflation-Indexed Bonds, **7.75% GOI Savings Bonds (2018)** — interest rate **7% to 10%** |
| **Corporate bonds** | Issued by companies; riskier than G-secs; rate depends on creditworthiness | LIC Housing Finance (7.95%, mat. 29 Jan 2028, ₹1,477 cr); Hindustan Petroleum (7.74%, mat. Mar 2028); IRFC (8.1%, issued 23 Feb 2012, mat. 23 Feb 2027, FV ₹1,000) |
| **Municipal bonds (munis)** | Issued by local authorities | **Indore Municipal Corporation** (allotted **20 Feb 2023**: 8.25%, mat. 20 Feb 2026, ₹61 cr) |
| **Foreign bonds** | Issued by foreign government or foreign corporation | — |

**Government bond subtypes:**
- **Fixed-rate bonds (FRBs)**: fixed interest payments throughout life.
- **Floating-rate bonds**: variable interest payments.
- **Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs)**: invest in digital gold at average gold price; **SGB Scheme 2022-23**: 2.5% p.a. on nominal value; redeemable at end of **8 years**.
- **Inflation-Indexed Bonds**: prices and interest adjusted to CPI or WPI.
- **7.75% GOI Savings Bond** (2018): replaced 8% savings bond; not for NRIs, minors, or HUFs.

#### 2) By coupon
- **Zero coupon (pure discount) bonds**: no interest; issued at discount, repaid at par. Investor earns capital return only.
  - Example: FV ₹100, ₹10 discount → issuer receives ₹90; investor pays ₹90, gets ₹100 at maturity.
- **Coupon bonds (regular)**: carry interest.
  - **Fixed coupon bonds** — fixed interest.
  - **Floating coupon bonds** — variable interest.
  - Investor earns capital + revenue returns.

#### 3) By maturity
- **Perpetual bonds** — indefinite maturity.
- **Non-perpetual bonds** — definite maturity.

#### 4) By option (embedded)
- **Puttable (retractable) bond** — bondholder can sell back to issuer at predetermined put price (usually higher than face value) before maturity.
- **Callable bond** — issuer can buy back from bondholder at specified call price before maturity.

#### 5) By convertibility
- **Convertible bonds** — convertible into issuer's shares per terms.
- **Non-convertible bonds** — cannot convert; usually carry a **higher coupon** than convertibles.

### Definitions
- **Government bonds (G-secs)** ⭐: Issued by central or state governments; risk-free; rates 7–10%.
- **Sovereign Gold Bond (SGB)** ⭐: Government scheme for digital gold investment at average gold price.
- **Inflation-Indexed Bond**: Coupon and principal adjusted to CPI/WPI.
- **Corporate bond** ⭐: Issued by a company; risk depends on credit rating.
- **Municipal bond ("muni")**: Issued by local authority.
- **Zero-coupon bond** ⭐: No coupons; deep discount issue, par redemption.
- **Perpetual bond** ⭐: Indefinite maturity.
- **Puttable bond** ⭐: Investor's right to sell back early.
- **Callable bond** ⭐: Issuer's right to redeem early.
- **Convertible bond** ⭐: Convertible into equity per terms.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Convertible bonds always carry higher coupons. → ✅ Correct: **Non-convertible** bonds usually carry **higher** coupons.
- ❌ Mistake: Puttable = callable. → ✅ Correct: Put = investor's option to sell back; Call = issuer's option to buy back.
- ❌ Mistake: Zero-coupon = no return. → ✅ Correct: Zero-coupon delivers return as **capital appreciation** (issued at discount, redeemed at par).

> - 5 axes: **Issuer / Coupon / Maturity / Option / Convertibility**.
> - SGB 2022-23: 2.5% p.a., 8-year tenor.
> - 7.75% GOI Savings Bond not for NRIs, minors, HUFs.

## Section: Valuation of Bonds 🔴

### Core Idea
The **value** (intrinsic value) of a bond is the **present value of all coupons + redemption value** discounted at the required rate. The buy/sell decision compares this intrinsic value to the market price: undervalued ⇒ buy; overvalued ⇒ sell.

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### General bond valuation formula
$$V = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{C_k}{(1+i)^k} + \frac{RV}{(1+i)^n}$$

- $V$ = intrinsic value of bond.
- $C_k$ = coupon (interest) in year k.
- $RV$ = redemption value.
- $i$ = discount rate (required return).
- $n$ = years to maturity.
- $1/(1+i)^k$ = **Present Value Factor (PVF)** for year k. PVFs can be obtained from PV tables, log technique, calculator, MS-Excel, or online calculators.

#### Perpetual bond valuation
- For a perpetual bond with constant coupon $C$ and discount rate $i$:
  - The cash-flow series $\sum C/(1+i)^k$ is an **infinite geometric series** with first term $C/(1+i)$ and common ratio $1/(1+i)$.
  - Sum of infinite GP: $S_\infty = a/(1-r)$.

$$V_{\text{perpetual}} = \frac{C/(1+i)}{1 - 1/(1+i)} = \frac{C}{i}$$

#### Buy/sell decisions
| Situation | Decision |
|-----------|----------|
| Market price > value | **Overvalued** — don't buy; if held, sell |
| Market price < value | **Undervalued** — buy; if held, keep |

### Examples

**Example 12.1 — Coupon bond redeemed at premium:**
- FV = ₹1,000; Coupon rate = 8% → C = ₹80; n = 3 yrs; i = 12%; redemption at 10% premium → RV = ₹1,100.
- $V = 80(0.8929) + 80(0.7972) + 80(0.7118) + 1,100(0.7118)$
- $V = 71.43 + 63.76 + 56.94 + 782.98 = ₹975.13$.
- Decisions:
  - Market price ₹1,050 > ₹975.13 ⇒ **overvalued**, don't buy.
  - Market price ₹800 < ₹975.13 ⇒ **undervalued**, buy.

**Example 12.2 — Perpetual bond:**
- C = 8% × ₹1,000 = ₹80; i = 10%.
- $V = C/i = 80/0.10 = ₹800$.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Bond value = PV of all coupons + PV of redemption value.
> - Perpetual bond: $V = C/i$.
> - Decision rule: market price < value ⇒ buy; > ⇒ sell/avoid.

## Section: Bond Yields — YTM, YTC, Current Yield 🔴

### Core Idea
Three yield measures describe the return on a bond. **YTM** is the discount rate that equates PV of cash flows to current price (held to maturity); **YTC** is the equivalent but to a call date; **Current Yield** is just annual interest ÷ price (and ignores capital gain/loss and time value of money).

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Yield to Maturity (YTM)
- Definition: the discount rate $i$ that satisfies
  $$P = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{C_k}{(1+\text{YTM})^k} + \frac{RV}{(1+\text{YTM})^n}$$
- Solution: trial-and-error (slow) or use approximation.
- **Approximation formula** (for constant coupon C, face value F, price P, n years):

$$\text{Approximate YTM} = \frac{C + (F-P)/n}{(F+P)/2}$$

#### Yield to Call (YTC)
- Same as YTM, but uses **call price** instead of RV and **call period** s instead of n:

$$P = \sum_{k=1}^{s} \frac{C_k}{(1+\text{YTC})^k} + \frac{\text{Call price}}{(1+\text{YTC})^s}$$

#### Current Yield
$$\text{Current Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual interest (coupon)}}{\text{Current price of bond}}$$
- Ignores redemption value and time value of money → "not an appropriate measure of yield" for long-term decisions.

### Examples

**Example 12.3 — Approximate YTM:**
- FV ₹1,000; coupon rate 10% → C = ₹100; n = 5 yrs; P = ₹800.
- Approx YTM = $\frac{100 + (1{,}000 - 800)/5}{(1{,}000 + 800)/2} = \frac{100 + 40}{900} = 140/900 = 0.1555$ ≈ **15.55%**.
- Interpretation: Investing ₹800 in this bond and holding 5 years yields ~15.55%.

**Worked example (Current Yield):**
- 10% coupon bond (FV ₹1,000) trading at ₹980. Annual interest = ₹100.
- Current yield = ₹100 / ₹980 = **10.20%**.
- Interpretation: Investing ₹1 today earns ₹0.102 interest at year-end.

**Worked example (Approx YTM, second case from check-your-progress):**
- Coupon rate 7%, FV ₹500 → C = ₹35; P = ₹350; n = 10.
- Approx YTM = $\frac{35 + (500 - 350)/10}{(500 + 350)/2} = \frac{35 + 15}{425} = 50/425 = 0.1176$ ≈ **11.76%**.
- Note: True trial-and-error YTM = **12.396%**.

### Definitions
- **YTM (Yield to Maturity)** ⭐: Return on a bond if held to maturity; the discount rate equating PV of cash flows to current price.
- **YTC (Yield to Call)** ⭐: YTM equivalent computed to the call date and call price (for callable bonds).
- **Current Yield** ⭐: Annual coupon ÷ current price; ignores capital gain/loss and time value of money.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Current yield = YTM. → ✅ Correct: Current yield ignores redemption value and time value.
- ❌ Mistake: Approximate YTM gives the exact yield. → ✅ Correct: It's an approximation; trial-and-error gives the exact YTM (e.g., 11.76% approx vs 12.396% exact).
- ❌ Mistake: YTC uses face value at maturity. → ✅ Correct: YTC uses **call price** at the **call date**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - YTM equation: $P = \sum C/(1+\text{YTM})^k + RV/(1+\text{YTM})^n$.
> - Approx YTM = $\dfrac{C + (F-P)/n}{(F+P)/2}$.
> - Current yield = Coupon / Price (ignores time value).

## Section: Credit Rating — Concept, Use, and Indian Agencies 🔴

### Core Idea
Credit rating is an evaluation of a borrower's **creditworthiness** — its ability to repay debt on time and in full. Agencies use letter scales (e.g., AAA highest → D lowest) to signal default risk. Globally, **Moody's** and **Standard & Poor's (S&P)** dominate. India has **seven** SEBI-registered agencies; **CRISIL, ICRA, CARE** are the largest, all using comparable AAA-to-D scales.

> **In Simple Terms:** A credit rating is a school report card for a borrower. AAA students rarely default and get the cheapest loans. D students are basically failing — if they get loans at all, they pay punitive rates. Investors look at the report card before lending.

### Key Concepts

#### Why credit rating matters
- Helps lenders/investors assess **default risk**.
- High rating → lower interest rates, better loan terms.
- Low rating → harder to obtain credit, higher rates, less favourable terms.
- Based on borrower's financial history: income, assets, liabilities, repayment record.

#### Indian credit rating agencies (seven SEBI-registered)
1. **Credit Rating Information Services of India Limited (CRISIL)**
2. **Investment Information and Credit Rating Agency of India (ICRA) Limited**
3. **Credit Analysis and Research (CARE) Limited**
4. **Acuite Ratings & Research**
5. **Brickwork Ratings India Private Limited**
6. **India Ratings and Research Pvt. Ltd.**
7. **Infometrics Valuation and Rating Pvt. Ltd.**

CRISIL is the **oldest**, followed by ICRA and CARE.

#### CRISIL rating scale (Table 12.1 from source)

| Rating | Explanation | Credit Risk | Degree of Safety |
|--------|-------------|-------------|-------------------|
| **CRISIL AAA** | Highest degree of safety regarding timely servicing | Lowest risk | Highest |
| **CRISIL AA** | High degree of safety | Very low risk | High |
| **CRISIL A** | Adequate degree of safety | Low risk | Adequate |
| **CRISIL BBB** | Moderate degree of safety | Moderate risk | Moderate |
| **CRISIL BB** | Moderate **risk of default** | Moderate risk of default | — |
| **CRISIL B** | High risk of default | High risk of default | — |
| **CRISIL C** | Very high risk of default | Very high risk of default | — |
| **CRISIL D** | In default or expected to be soon | Worst risk | — |

#### Establishment dates and specialisations
| Agency | Established | Specialisation |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| **CRISIL** | 1987 | Operates in UK, USA, Poland, China, Hong Kong, Argentina + India; rates commercial entities on strengths, market reputation, market share, board |
| **ICRA** | 1991 | Corporate governance, mutual funds, structured finance, performance ratings; AAA → D, same explanations as CRISIL |
| **CARE** | 1993 | Bank loan ratings, short-term and long-term debt instruments; AAA → D, same explanations as CRISIL |

### Definitions
- **Credit rating** ⭐: Evaluation of the creditworthiness of a borrower; measure of ability to repay debt on time and in full.
- **Credit rating agency** ⭐: Agency performing credit ratings; e.g., CRISIL, ICRA, CARE.
- **AAA**: Highest credit rating — lowest credit risk.
- **D**: Lowest credit rating — in default.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: AAA borrowers cannot default. → ✅ Correct: AAA = lowest *risk* of default, not zero risk.
- ❌ Mistake: All Indian rating agencies have different rating scales. → ✅ Correct: ICRA and CARE use **the same explanations** as CRISIL (AAA → D).

> - Indian agencies: 7 registered. **CRISIL (1987) → ICRA (1991) → CARE (1993)**.
> - Scale: AAA → D, low → high risk.
> - Global majors: **Moody's, S&P**.

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Brief on key debt instruments

- Issued by RBI on behalf of GoI.
- **Dated G-secs**: 1 to 30 years, fixed interest, **semi-annual payments**, principal as scheduled.
- **T-bills (short term)**: 91 / 182 / 364 days, discounted papers (zero-coupon).
- Risk-free (no credit risk).

**STRIPS** (Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal Securities):
- "Stripping" = separating a coupon-bearing bond into individual coupon and principal components.
- Example: a 5-year bond with semi-annual coupons → **10 coupon zero-coupon bonds + 1 principal zero-coupon bond**.

**Sovereign Green Bonds:**
- Approved by GFWC; issued by RBI on government's behalf to **finance green initiatives**.

**Certificate of Deposit (CD):** (cross-reference Chunk 002)
- Issued in **demat form** or as Usance Promissory Notes.
- **Banks**: 7 days–1 year maturity.
- **Financial Institutions**: 1 year–3 years maturity.
- Higher return than bank term deposit.
- Rated by CARE, ICRA, CRISIL, FITCH → enhances secondary-market tradability.
- Denomination: ₹1 lakh and multiples thereof.

**Commercial Papers (CPs):** (cross-reference Chunk 002)
- 7 days to 365 days; corporate (non-bank) issuer at discount.
- Minimum investment: **₹5 lakh face value** and multiples.
- Rated by CARE, ICRA, CRISIL, FITCH.
- Higher return than fixed deposits and CDs.
- Investment-grade CPs traded in secondary market depending on demand.
- Carries credit risk.

### Definitions
- **Debt market / fixed-income market** ⭐: Market for instruments offering fixed returns; bond market is its core.
- **G-secs**: Government securities issued by central/state governments.
- **STRIPS** ⭐: Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal Securities — splitting a coupon bond into zero-coupon components.
- **Sovereign Green Bond** ⭐: Government bond issued via RBI to finance green projects, monitored via Ministry of Finance's green register.
- **Treasury Bill (T-bill)**: Zero-coupon short-term G-sec (91/182/364 days).

> **Quick Recall:**

> - Regulators: **RBI for G-secs/money market**, **SEBI for corporate/muni bonds + listed CPs**.

## Section: Unit 13 — Derivatives: Introduction 🔴

### Core Idea
**Derivatives** are financial instruments whose value is **derived** from an underlying asset (stock, bond, currency, commodity, index). The most common forms are options, futures, forwards, and swaps. Derivatives let investors and firms hedge risk or speculate on price movements; they can be highly **leveraged**, amplifying both gains and losses.

> **In Simple Terms:** A derivative is a contract whose price piggybacks on something else. If a bet pays out based on tomorrow's stock price of Reliance, the bet itself is a derivative — its value rides on Reliance's value.

### Key Concepts

#### What makes a derivative a derivative
- **Derivative (A)** derives value from an **underlying asset (B)**.
- Underlying assets: shares, bonds, currencies, commodities, indices, debentures, etc.
- Common derivative types: **options, futures, forwards, swaps**.

#### Legal definition (India)
- **Securities Laws (Second Amendment) Act, 1999**: officially included derivatives in the "Securities" definition.
- Per **Securities Contracts (Regulations) Act**: a derivative comprises securities derived from debt instruments, shares, loans, risk instruments, or contracts for differences. Includes contracts deriving value from prices or index of underlying securities.

### Definitions
- **Derivative** ⭐: Financial instrument that derives its value from an underlying asset such as a stock, bond, currency, commodity, or index.
- **Underlying asset** ⭐: The asset whose price drives a derivative's value.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Derivative ↔ underlying: A's value depends on B's.
> - 4 common types: **Options, Futures, Forwards, Swaps**.
> - Legal basis in India: **Securities Laws (Second Amendment) Act, 1999**.

### Core Idea
Derivatives are contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset, used to manage or speculate on **price, interest-rate, and credit risks**. ## Section: Advantages and Limitations of Derivatives 🔴

### Core Idea
Derivatives offer four big advantages — **price-locking, hedging, leverage, diversification** — and four offsetting limitations — **valuation difficulty, counterparty default, complexity, and demand-supply sensitivity**. Each side of the ledger illustrates why they are powerful but require careful use.

### Key Concepts

#### Advantages

| Advantage | How it works |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Lock in prices** | Hedge against price fluctuations in commodities, currencies, securities — supports financial planning and budgeting |
| **Hedge against risk** | Producers/consumers of commodities lock in prices via futures/options; firms with debt use **interest-rate swaps/futures** to neutralise rate risk |
| **Leveraged exposure** | Small capital → large position; small underlying-price moves → big derivative value moves |
| **Portfolio diversification** | Access to commodities, currencies, rates, equities; even hard-to-reach emerging markets |

#### Limitations

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|------------|-----------------|
| **Difficult to value** | Often requires advanced models — **Black-Scholes** or **binomial pricing** (covered in Unit 21); strong assumptions about market behaviour |
| **Counterparty default** | Loss if counterparty fails to honour the contract; counterparty's credit-rating decline reduces derivative's value |
| **Complex** | Many types — options, futures, forwards, swaps, **CDOs, CDS** etc. — each with own rules, characteristics, and pricing |
| **Sensitive to demand-supply** | Speculative trading can amplify volatility |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Leverage only works in your favour. → ✅ Correct: Leverage amplifies **losses** equally; small adverse moves in the underlying produce large derivative losses.
- ❌ Mistake: Exchange-traded derivatives have no counterparty risk. → ✅ Correct: They drastically *reduce* it via clearing corporations; OTC derivatives carry full counterparty risk.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 advantages: lock prices, hedge risk, leverage, diversify.
> - 4 limitations: hard to value, counterparty default, complex, demand-supply volatility.

## Section: Futures and Forwards 🔴

### Core Idea
Both let parties lock in a future price for an asset, but they differ structurally. **Forwards** are private, customised, OTC contracts with default risk and physical settlement; **futures** are standardised, exchange-traded contracts with marking-to-market, cash settlement, and effective default safeguards via clearing.

### Key Concepts

#### Forwards — definition and use
- Two-party financial agreement to buy/sell asset at predetermined price at future date.
- Counterparties: individuals, corporations, financial institutions.
- Used to **hedge price fluctuations** in commodities, currencies, stocks, bonds.

**Example 13.1 (forward):** A company that wants to buy a commodity later signs a forward contract with a supplier at a fixed price → avoids price-volatility risk. An investor expecting a stock to appreciate signs a forward to buy the stock at a predetermined price.

#### Futures — definition and use
- Financial instrument that **legally binds** parties to buy/sell an asset at a predetermined price and date.
- Price of the underlying is fixed at contract creation.
- Used by traders/investors to **hedge or speculate**.

**Example (futures):** A wheat farmer sells a wheat futures contract to lock in a price for future delivery → hedges against price drop. A speculator buys a stock-index future expecting market to rise.

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Forwards vs Futures (Table 13.1)

| # | Forward contract | Futures contract |
|---|------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | Contract price **not publicly disclosed** | Contract price **transparent** |
| 2 | **Counterparty default risk** present | Effective safeguards against defaults |
| 3 | **Unique** in size, expiration, asset type | **Standardised** size, expiration, features |
| 4 | Suffers from **liquidity** problems | No liquidity problem |
| 5 | Settlement by **physical delivery** of asset | Cash settlement |
| 6 | Traded personally / by phone | Traded in competitive markets |
| 7 | **OTC** market | Organised exchange with physical location |
| 8 | Costs based on **bid-ask spread** | Brokerage fees |
| 9 | **Not subject to marking to market** | Subject to **marking to market** |
| 10 | Credit risk **borne by each party** | Credit risk **not borne by each party** (clearing house) |

### Definitions
- **Futures** ⭐: Standardised, exchange-traded contract obligating buyer to purchase and seller to sell an asset at predetermined price and date.
- **Forwards** ⭐: Customised, OTC contract between two parties to buy/sell an asset at predetermined price and date.
- **Marking to market**: Daily revaluation of futures positions to current market price; gains/losses settled daily.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Futures and forwards are interchangeable. → ✅ Correct: Futures = **standardised**, exchange-traded, MTM; Forwards = **customised**, OTC, no MTM.
- ❌ Mistake: Futures are settled by delivery. → ✅ Correct: Futures usually **cash-settled**; forwards typically delivered.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Futures = standardised + exchange + MTM + clearing house bears credit risk.
> - Forwards = customised + OTC + delivery + each party bears credit risk.

## Section: Options — Concept and Call Option 🔴

### Core Idea
An **option** is the right (not the obligation) to buy or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined **strike/exercise price** within a specified period — purchased for an upfront **option premium**. A **call option** gives the buyer the right to **buy**; the option is exercised only when the spot price exceeds the strike (S > E), otherwise it lapses. The option premium represents the cost of avoiding downside risk while retaining upside potential.

> **In Simple Terms:** A call option is like reserving a movie ticket at today's price for tomorrow's show. If tomorrow the ticket price rises, you win — you pay yesterday's price. If tomorrow's price drops, you don't go (the reservation lapses) and you only lose the small booking fee. The "booking fee" is the option premium.

### Key Concepts

#### What is an option
- Agreement: Party A has the **right** to buy/sell an asset at a predetermined price within a specified time frame.
- A is **not obligated** to execute — that's why it's called an option.
- **Hedging tool**: managing the risk of price fluctuations.

#### Option types (two)
1. **Call option** — right to **buy**.
2. **Put option** — right to **sell**.

#### Anatomy of a call option (running example)
- Current share price: ₹500.
- Vikas grants you the right to **buy** the share at **₹530** in 4 months, regardless of actual market price.
- He charges **₹20** as option premium.
- **If actual price rises to ₹580**: exercise → buy at ₹530 (from Vikas), sell at ₹580 (in market) → ₹50 gain − ₹20 premium = **₹30 net payoff**. Loss for Vikas.
- **If actual price stays low**: don't exercise. You lose only the ₹20 premium.

| Term | Meaning in this example |
|------|-------------------------|
| **Underlying asset** | The share |
| **Exercise / strike price (E)** | ₹530 |
| **Exercise / intrinsic value of call (S − E at expiry)** | ₹50 (when S = ₹580) |
| **Option premium** | ₹20 (cost to buyer for taking the option) |
| **Net payoff** | ₹30 (₹50 gross − ₹20 premium) |
| **Option writer** | Vikas (sells the option) |
| **Option buyer** | You |

#### Decision rule for a call option (Table 13.2)

| Situation | Decision | Market Term |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **S > E** at expiry (S − E > 0) | **Exercise** — profitable | **In-the-money call** (money flows to buyer) |
| **S < E** at expiry (S − E < 0) | **Don't exercise** | **Out-of-the-money call** (money would flow out if exercised) |

#### Formulas

**Exercise (intrinsic) value of a call option, V_CO:**
$$V_{CO} = \begin{cases} S - E & \text{if } S > E \\ 0 & \text{if } S \le E \end{cases}$$

Equivalently:
$$V_{CO} = \max(S - E, 0)$$

**Net payoff:**
$$\text{Net Payoff} = V_{CO} - \text{Option premium}$$

(Net payoff diagrams from buyer's perspective slope upward beyond E; from writer's perspective they're the mirror image — capped gain = premium, downside unbounded.)

### Examples

**Example 13.2 — Call option on Reliance Industries:**
- Current price ₹7; possible prices at expiry (3 months): ₹5, ₹10, ₹15, ₹20, ₹25, ₹30.
- Aakash buys a call: **strike E = ₹12**, premium = ₹2. (Source uses "₹35" then computes with ₹2; the worked table uses ₹2.)

**Buyer's payoff (Aakash):**

| Price S (₹) | Premium (₹) | Strike (₹) | Total Cost (₹) | Net Payoff (₹) | Exercise? |
|-------------|--------------|--------------|------------------|------------------|------------|
| 5 | 2 | — | 2 | −2 | No |
| 10 | 2 | — | 2 | −2 | No |
| 15 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 0 | Yes |
| 20 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 5 | Yes |
| 25 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 10 | Yes |
| 30 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 15 | Yes |

**Writer's payoff (derivative dealer)** — mirror image:

| Price S (₹) | Premium (₹) | Strike (₹) | Total Revenue (₹) | Net Payoff (₹) | Exercise? |
|-------------|--------------|--------------|---------------------|------------------|------------|
| 5 | 2 | — | 2 | 2 | No |
| 10 | 2 | — | 2 | 2 | No |
| 15 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 0 | Yes |
| 20 | 2 | 13 | 15 | −5 | Yes |
| 25 | 2 | 13 | 15 | −10 | Yes |
| 30 | 2 | 13 | 15 | −15 | Yes |

> Buyer's max loss = premium; potential gain = unbounded above strike.
> Writer's max gain = premium; potential loss = unbounded above strike.

### Definitions
- **Option** ⭐: Right (not obligation) of one party to buy or sell an underlying at a predetermined price within a specified time frame, for a premium.
- **Call option** ⭐: Type of derivative giving the buyer the right to **buy** the underlying at the strike price.
- **Strike / exercise price (E)** ⭐: Predetermined price at which the underlying may be bought (call) or sold (put).
- **Option premium** ⭐: Cost paid by buyer for taking the option; reflects time value and writer's risk.
- **Intrinsic / exercise value (V_CO)**: Max(S − E, 0) for a call.
- **In-the-money call**: S > E at expiry.
- **Out-of-the-money call**: S < E at expiry.
- **Hedging**: Strategy to reduce or manage risk.
- **Option writer**: Sells (writes) the option — receives premium, takes on obligation if exercised.
- **Option buyer**: Pays premium for the right to exercise.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: An option *forces* the holder to buy/sell. → ✅ Correct: It is a **right, not an obligation** — buyer can let it lapse.
- ❌ Mistake: Net payoff equals intrinsic value. → ✅ Correct: Net payoff = intrinsic value **− option premium**.
- ❌ Mistake: Out-of-the-money call should still be exercised. → ✅ Correct: Exercising a call when S < E means buying at a loss; never rational.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Call: right to buy. Exercise iff S > E.
> - V_CO = max(S − E, 0). Net Payoff = V_CO − premium.
> - Buyer max loss = premium; writer max gain = premium.
> - Vocabulary: strike, premium, in/out of the money, writer, buyer.

## Section: Put Option 🔴

<!-- See chunk 007 for start of "Options" section. -->

### Core Idea
A **put option** gives the buyer the right (not the obligation) to **sell** the underlying at a predetermined exercise price by expiry. It's the mirror image of a call: it's exercised only when the strike price exceeds the spot (E > S), and it pays off when prices fall — making it a hedging tool for protection against price declines.

> **In Simple Terms:** A put is insurance against a price drop. You pay a small premium to lock in a guaranteed selling price. If the market price drops below your guaranteed price, you exercise (sell at the high lock-in price). If prices rise instead, you let the put lapse and just sell at the higher market price.

### Key Concepts

#### Decision rule for a put option (Table 13.3)

| Situation | Decision | Market Term |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **E < S** at expiry (E − S < 0) | **Don't exercise** — profitable in market | **Out-of-the-money put** |
| **E > S** at expiry (E − S > 0) | **Exercise put** | **In-the-money put** (money flows to buyer) |

> Note: The source's wording on "decision" in the second row says "because it is not a profitable situation" — but the *exercise* decision is correct (E > S means it pays to sell to writer at E rather than market at S). It is profitable to exercise when E > S.

#### Formulas

**Exercise / intrinsic value of put, V_PO:**
$$V_{PO} = \begin{cases} E - S & \text{if } E > S \\ 0 & \text{if } E \le S \end{cases}$$

Equivalently:
$$V_{PO} = \max(E - S, 0)$$

**Net payoff:**
$$\text{Net Payoff} = V_{PO} - \text{Option premium}$$

#### Logical justification
- A put is exercised only when **E > S** because selling to the writer at E and replacing the asset (or transferring it) when market price is lower than E is a profit-making move.
- Exercising when E < S would mean buying in the market at S to sell to writer at E (lower) — irrational.

### Examples

**Example 13.3 — Put option on SBI:**
- Current price ₹7; possible prices at expiry (2 months): ₹5, ₹10, ₹15, ₹20, ₹25, ₹30.
- Siddhi buys a put: **strike E = ₹12**, premium = ₹2.

**Buyer's payoff (Siddhi):**

| Price S (₹) | Premium (₹) | Strike (₹) | Total Cost (₹) | Net Payoff (₹) | Exercise? |
|-------------|--------------|--------------|------------------|------------------|------------|
| 5 | 2 | 13 | 7 | 6 | Yes |
| 10 | 2 | 13 | 12 | 1 | Yes |
| 15 | 2 | — | 2 | −2 | No |
| 20 | 2 | — | 2 | −2 | No |
| 25 | 2 | — | 2 | −2 | No |
| 30 | 2 | — | 2 | −2 | No |

> Buyer's max loss = premium (₹2). Profit grows as S falls below E. (Note: source uses ₹13 in computations even though the strike is stated as ₹12 — preserving source's worked table.)

### Definitions
- **Put option** ⭐: Right (not obligation) of buyer to **sell** the underlying at a predetermined exercise price within a specified time, for a premium.
- **In-the-money put** ⭐: E > S at expiry.
- **Out-of-the-money put**: E < S at expiry.
- **Intrinsic value of put (V_PO)**: max(E − S, 0).

#### Call vs Put — comparison

| Aspect | Call option | Put option |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| Right to | **Buy** the underlying | **Sell** the underlying |
| Exercise when | **S > E** | **E > S** |
| Intrinsic value | max(S − E, 0) | max(E − S, 0) |
| Buyer profits from | Rising prices | Falling prices |
| Used for | Hedging short positions / speculating on rises | Hedging long positions / speculating on falls |
| In-the-money | S > E | E > S |

#### Option vs Future — comparison
- **Future**: contract that **obligates** buyer to purchase / seller to sell at specified date and price.
- **Option**: gives the buyer the **right but not obligation** to buy or sell at specified price by date.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: A put option pays off when prices rise. → ✅ Correct: Put pays when **E > S** (price falls below strike).
- ❌ Mistake: Options are a type of futures. → ✅ Correct: Futures = obligation; Options = right (no obligation).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Put exercised iff E > S.
> - V_PO = max(E − S, 0).
> - Buyer's max loss = premium.
> - Profits from falling prices.

## Section: Swaps — Concept, Features, IRS, CDS 🔴

### Core Idea
A **swap** is a financial contract for two parties to exchange cash flows or financial instruments under predetermined terms. Swaps are highly customised, OTC, often involve fixed-vs-floating rate exchanges, and are used to alter the nature of an asset/liability without liquidating it. The two most popular swaps are the **interest-rate swap (IRS)** and the **credit-default swap (CDS)**.

> **In Simple Terms:** A swap is like trading payment streams. Imagine two friends — one with a fixed-salary job that envies the bonuses of the other, who has a variable salary. They agree to swap paychecks each month. Neither changes employer; both get the income profile they wanted.

### Key Concepts

#### What swaps do
- Exchange cash flows or financial instruments based on predetermined terms.
- Encompass spot and forward transactions in a customised framework.
- Used by corporations, banks, individuals for **financing, lower borrowing costs, and managing interest-rate / FX risks**.
- Core objective: **alter the nature of an asset or liability without liquidation**.
  - Equity investor → can convert risky returns into fixed-income cash flows without selling equities.
  - Corporation with floating-rate debt → can convert it into fixed-rate without retiring/reissuing debt.

#### Ten features of swaps
1. **Customisability** — tailored to parties' specific needs.
2. **Counterparty risk** — depends on both parties fulfilling obligations.
3. **OTC trading** — through dealer networks, not standardised exchanges.
4. **Types** — interest-rate, currency, commodity swaps; different underlyings and cash-flow structures.
5. **Fixed and floating rates** — most swaps exchange fixed for floating (or vice versa).
6. **Maturity** — specified, ranges from months to several years.
7. **Notional principal** — total value on which cash flows are calculated; **principal itself is usually not exchanged**.
8. **Cash-flow exchange** — periodic (quarterly, semi-annual) per agreement.
9. **Purpose** — hedge risks, manage rate/currency exposure, speculate.
10. **Documentation** — swap agreement specifies notional, payment dates, calculation methods, default conditions.

### Definitions
- **Swap** ⭐: Derivative contract for two parties to exchange financial instruments or cash flows on agreed terms.
- **Notional principal** ⭐: Reference value on which cash flows are computed; not itself exchanged.
- **LIBOR (London Inter-Bank Offered Rate)** ⭐: Common floating-rate benchmark.
- **MIBOR (Mumbai Inter-Bank Offered Rate)** ⭐: Indian counterpart to LIBOR; used in Indian swaps.

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### a) Interest-rate swap (IRS)
- One party pays **fixed interest** on a notional amount and receives **floating interest** (commonly **LIBOR + base points**, or **MIBOR + base points** in India) on the same notional.
- No principal exchange; only periodic interest cash-flow netting.

**Example 13.5 — IRS between Companies A and B:**
- A pays B fixed at 7% p.a. on notional ₹10,00,000; A receives floating at LIBOR + 1% on same notional. (Note: source then uses fixed rate of 10% in its tables — preserving source figures.)
- Term: 3 years. LIBOR data:

| Year | LIBOR |
|------|-------|
| 0–1 | 8% |
| 1–2 | 6% |
| 2–3 | 10% |

**Step 1 — Floating interest receivable by A (LIBOR + 1%):**

| Year | Floating Rate | Floating Interest (₹) |
|------|----------------|------------------------|
| 1 | 9% | 90,000 |
| 2 | 7% | 70,000 |
| 3 | 11% | 1,10,000 |

**Step 2 — Fixed interest payable by A (10% in source's worked numbers):**

| Year | Fixed Rate | Fixed Interest (₹) |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1 | 10% | 1,00,000 |
| 2 | 10% | 1,00,000 |
| 3 | 10% | 1,00,000 |

**Step 3 — Net cash flow to A:**

#### b) Credit Default Swap (CDS)
- Financial contract that provides **insurance against default risk** on a specific debt instrument.
- **Buyer of CDS** pays a premium to the **seller of CDS**.
- If borrower defaults, **seller pays buyer the face value** of the debt instrument.
- Effectively, the credit risk transfers from buyer to seller in exchange for premium.

**Example 13.6 — CDS on Company A's bond:**
- Company A issues ₹10 million bond, 5-year maturity.
- Investors buy bonds; Company A promises interest + principal repayment.
- If A defaults, investors lose interest and principal.
- Investor mitigates by buying CDS from a bank/hedge fund:
  - Pays a premium for protection.
  - If A defaults, the institution pays investor the **₹10 million face value**.
  - Risk transfers from investor to institution; institution earns the premium as compensation.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: In a swap, principal is exchanged. → ✅ Correct: **Notional principal is usually not exchanged**; only interest/cash flows are.
- ❌ Mistake: A CDS protects against any market loss. → ✅ Correct: A CDS specifically covers **default risk** on a particular debt instrument.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Swap = exchange of cash flows; OTC; notional principal is **not** exchanged.
> - 2 most common swaps: **IRS** (fixed ↔ floating interest), **CDS** (default protection).
> - LIBOR / MIBOR — floating-rate benchmarks.
> - 10 features mnemonic: **C**ustomisable, **C**ounterparty risk, **O**TC, **T**ypes, **F**ixed/Floating, **M**aturity, **N**otional, **C**ash-flow exchange, **P**urpose, **D**ocumentation.
