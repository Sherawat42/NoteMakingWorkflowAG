## Section: Other Sub-Market Instruments — CDs, CPs, Banker's Acceptance, Repos 🔴

<!-- See chunk 001 for start of "Instruments of Organised Markets" section. Chunk 001 ended mid-section after BRS. -->

### Core Idea
Beyond call money, T-bills and BRS, the organised money market deepens through **Certificates of Deposit (CDs)**, **Commercial Papers (CPs)**, **Banker's Acceptance**, and **Repurchase Options (Repos)**. Each tackles a distinct short-term need: bulk deposit-raising for banks, working-capital finance for corporates, trade-payment guarantee, and collateralised short-term liquidity respectively.

### Key Concepts

#### Certificate of Deposit (CD) 🔴
- Document **of title to a time deposit**, distinguished from a conventional time deposit by its **free negotiability**.
- Issued by **banks** (Commercial banks initially; **RRBs from 2021**) to raise short-term cash.
- **Term**: generally 3 months to 5 years; restricts demand-withdrawal but money can be withdrawn early on penalty.
- **Transferable** by endorsement and delivery, but only **after 45 days** from date of issue.
- Issued **at a discount** (front-end or rear-end); discount rate is **market-determined**.
- Returns are **higher than T-bills** (because higher risk than sovereign).
- Introduced in Indian money market in **March 1989**.
- Important source of **bulk fund mobilisation at low operational cost** for banks.
- **Return method matters**: Annual Percentage Yield (APY, compound) vs Annual Percentage Rate (APR, simple).
  - If interest is paid **once a year**, APY = APR.
  - If interest is paid **more than once a year**, APY > APR → **prefer APY**.

#### Commercial Paper (CP) 🔴
- **Usance promissory note** issued by a **corporate** at a discount, with fixed maturity, evidencing the issuer's short-term obligation.
- **Maturity**: 1 to 270 days (typical), used for accounts receivables, inventories, short-term liabilities.
- Introduced in India in **1989**.
- **Bypasses bank intermediation** → low-cost alternative to bank loans.
- **Unsecured**, not zero-risk; only **firms with high credit ratings** find buyers easily without offering large discounts.
- Yields **higher returns than T-bills** (less secure).
- Issued by corporates and financial institutions; freely transferable in **demat form**; actively traded in **secondary market**.

**Worked example (CP):**
- Company has ₹1 crore receivables, 6-month credit period; needs cash now.
- Issues 6-month CP at **10% discount** on ₹1 crore face value.
- Buyer pays ₹95 lakh now, receives ₹1 crore at maturity → buyer earns **₹5 lakh interest over 6 months**.
- Company immediately liquidates its receivables.

#### Banker's Acceptance 🟡
- Money-market instrument for short-term liquidity.
- Company gives a **bank guarantee** to a seller to pay the agreed amount on a future date.
- If buyer defaults, seller invokes the bank guarantee.
- Typically used to **finance exports and imports**.

#### Repurchase Options (Repo) / Ready Forward (RF) 🔴
- Borrower places acceptable security with the lender against funds received and **agrees to reverse the transaction** on a predetermined future date at an agreed interest cost.
- **No fixed period prescribed**; in practice **min 14 days, max 1 year**.
- Interest is **market-determined**, built into the repo structure.
- Eligible parties: commercial banks, financial institutions, brokers, DFHI.
- Borrower advantage: raises funds against securities **without altering asset mix**.
- Lender advantage: safe, attractive return; predictable reversal date eases fund management.

### Definitions
- **Certificate of Deposit (CD)** ⭐: Negotiable bank-issued title to a time deposit, issued at discount, market-determined rate.
- **Commercial Paper (CP)** ⭐: Unsecured short-term usance promissory note issued by a corporate at a discount.
- **Banker's Acceptance**: Bank guarantee given to a seller on behalf of a buyer, mostly for export-import finance.
- **Repo / Ready Forward**: Sale of securities with agreed repurchase at a future date and agreed price, used for short-term collateralised borrowing.
- **APY (Annual Percentage Yield)**: Compound-interest based annual return.
- **APR (Annual Percentage Rate)**: Simple-interest based annual return.

#### CD vs CP — comparison

| Aspect | Certificate of Deposit (CD) | Commercial Paper (CP) |
|--------|------------------------------|------------------------|
| Issuer | Banks (Commercial; RRBs since 2021) | Corporates / financial institutions |
| Underlying | Title to time deposit | Unsecured promissory note |
| Tenor | 3 months – 5 years | 1 – 270 days |
| Purpose | Bulk fund mobilisation by bank | Working-capital, bypass bank loans |
| Risk | Bank-backed, lower | Corporate, slightly higher |
| Transfer | After 45 days, by endorsement & delivery | Freely transferable, demat |
| Introduced | March 1989 | 1989 |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - CD: bank-issued, ≥ 45 days lock before transfer, APY > APR if compounded.
> - CP: corporate-issued, 1–270 days, only top-rated firms get attractive pricing.
> - Repo: collateralised, min 14 days, max 1 year, market-determined rate.
> - Banker's acceptance: bank's guarantee for trade payment; export/import finance.

## Section: The Unorganised Money Market 🟡

### Core Idea
The unorganised market consists of **indigenous bankers, unregulated non-bank intermediaries (finance companies, chit funds, nidhis), and moneylenders** who lend to retailers, small traders, farmers, artisans and the poor at high rates outside RBI control. Despite RBI's 1991 NBFC directions (Reserve Bank Directions, 1998), regulation remains inadequate and instruments are non-standardised.

### Key Concepts

#### Indigenous Bankers
- Individuals/private firms acting like mini banks (deposits + loans).
- Activities **unregulated** and uninfluenced by the RBI bank rate.
- Footprint shrunk with growth of commercial and cooperative banks; now a few thousand operate, mainly in western & southern India.
- **Four sub-groups**:

| Sub-group | Main location |
|-----------|---------------|
| Gujarati Shroffs | Mumbai, Kolkata, Gujarat industrial/trading cities — **most powerful by volume** |
| Multani / Shikarpuri Shroffs | Mumbai, Chennai |
| Chettiars | Mostly South India |
| Marwari Shroffs | Mumbai, Kolkata, tea gardens of Assam, parts of NE India |

- Mostly mix banking and non-banking business; charge **high interest rates**.

#### Unregulated Non-Bank Financial Intermediaries
- **Finance companies**: collect substantial deposits/borrowings; lend to wholesalers, retailers, artisans, self-employed at **36–48% p.a.**
- **Chit funds**: spread across states, concentrated in **Tamil Nadu and Kerala**.
- **Nidhis**: South-Indian mutual benefit funds, restricted to members.

#### Moneylenders
- Advance loans to small/marginal farmers, agricultural labour, artisans, factory/mine workers, low-paid staff, small traders at very high rates; common malpractices in record-keeping.
- **Three types**:
  1. Professional moneylenders.
  2. Itinerant moneylenders (Kabulis, Pathans).
  3. Non-professional moneylenders.
- Localised, non-uniform methods; **unregulated, unsupervised**, leading to exploitation. A "necessary evil" given inadequate institutional credit access.

### Definitions
- **Indigenous bankers** ⭐: Private individuals/firms taking deposits and lending — operate outside RBI regulation.
- **Nidhi**: South-Indian mutual benefit fund restricted to members.

#### Indigenous Bankers vs Moneylenders — comparison

| Aspect | Indigenous Bankers | Moneylenders |
|--------|---------------------|---------------|
| Activity | Deposits + loans (mini-bank) | Loans only |
| Borrowers | Traders, businesses | Small farmers, labour, poor |
| Subgroups | Gujarati, Multani, Chettiar, Marwari | Professional, Itinerant, Non-professional |
| Rates | High | Very high + malpractices |
| Regulation | Not regulated | Not regulated |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Unorganised market = Indigenous bankers + Non-bank intermediaries + Moneylenders.
> - 4 indigenous-banker groups; **Gujarati Shroffs** = most powerful.
> - Finance companies' rates: **36–48% p.a.**
> - Chit funds concentrated in **Tamil Nadu and Kerala**.

## Section: Regulatory Measures to Streamline the Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
India's money market has been overhauled by reforms initiated through three landmark committees and a series of RBI/SEBI policy measures since the late 1980s — adding institutions (DFHI, SEBI, credit rating agencies), introducing newer instruments (CDs, CPs, MMMFs), deregulating interest rates from **1 May 1989**, and integrating the money market with foreign exchange, capital and gilt markets.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Regulators**: RBI **and** SEBI jointly regulate Indian money markets.

**Three landmark committees:**

| Committee | Year | Mandate |
|-----------|------|---------|
| **Chakravarty Committee** | 1985 | Improvement of the monetary system |
| **Vaghul Working Group** | 1986 | Activate and vitalise the money market |
| **Narasimham Committee** | 1991 | Streamline the financial system |

**Institutional reforms:**
1. **DFHI** established **1988** — broadens and deepens money market; market maker.
2. **SEBI** set up **1992** — apex regulator for security markets.
3. **Credit rating agencies** promoted — **CRISIL (1987), ICRA (1991)**, etc. — to ease fund raising in the money market.

**Instrument & rate reforms:**
- New sub-markets introduced: CDs, call money, collateral loan, commercial paper, MFs (MMMFs).
- **Interest-rate deregulation from 1 May 1989** → market-determined rates.
- Apex agencies: RBI, DFHI, **STCI (Securities Trading Corporation of India)**.
- Operating level: commercial banks, public financial institutions, selected corporates.
- Banks: can both **buy and sell** instruments; PFIs and corporates: typically only **lending** subject to conditions.
- **CRR and SLR** are used as credit-control tools to tone money market operations in the commercial banking sector.

**Repo reforms:**
- **November 1996**: Repos (ready forward) reintroduced — to mop up excess liquidity, reduce volatility in call money and forex markets.
- **21 October 1997**: Repo facility extended to **corporate debt and PSU bonds**.
- RBI introduced **reverse repo** for primary dealers in G-sec at the bank rate, on a discretionary basis.

**Macroeconomic role of money market rates:**
- Higher rates → reduce liquidity → reduce economic activity.
- Lower rates → raise liquidity, lower cost of capital → raise investment.
- Helps RBI control general money supply and **inflation**.

### Definitions
- **CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)**: Share of bank deposits to be held as reserves with the RBI (referenced as a credit-control tool).
- **MMMF (Money Market Mutual Fund)**: Fund pooling investors' money to invest in money-market instruments.
- **Reverse repo**: RBI's facility to absorb liquidity by selling securities with agreement to buy back later.
- **STCI (Securities Trading Corporation of India)**: Apex agency dealing in money market instruments.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three committees: **Chakravarty (1985) → Vaghul (1986) → Narasimham (1991)**.
> - DFHI 1988, SEBI 1992, CRISIL 1987, ICRA 1991.
> - **Interest rates deregulated 1 May 1989**.
> - Repo reintroduced **Nov 1996**; extended to corporate debt/PSU bonds **21 Oct 1997**.

## Section: Limitations of the Indian Money Market 🟡

### Core Idea
Despite reforms, the Indian money market remains **less developed than world standards** — characterised by inadequate short-term assets, dichotomy between organised and unorganised sectors, lack of intermediaries, weak rural banking penetration, and disparate interest rates.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Key drawbacks:
1. **Inadequate / discontinuous supply of short-term assets** (T-bills, bills of exchange, short-term G-secs).
2. **No dedicated short-term assets dealers** acting as intermediaries between government and the banking system.
3. **Underdeveloped bill market** despite RBI efforts.
4. **Lack of coordination** among constituents → fails to attract **foreign funds**.
5. **Dichotomy** between organised and unorganised sectors makes uniform monetary-policy implementation difficult.
6. **Wasteful competition** within and between sectors.
7. **Inadequate banking institutions** relative to country size; rural areas under-served.
8. **Disparities in interest rates** disrupt smooth functioning.

### Examples
- DFHI and MMMFs cited as recent improvements that have advanced the Indian money market.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Organised vs unorganised dichotomy = **biggest structural weakness**.
> - Bill market still **underdeveloped** despite RBI push.
> - Reform progress = DFHI + MMMFs + interest deregulation.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Indian money market is fully integrated. → ✅ Correct: It still has a **dichotomic** structure with weak coordination.
- ❌ Mistake: Money market interest rates are administered. → ✅ Correct: Deregulated and **market-determined since 1 May 1989**.

## Section: Summary, Key Words, and Wrap-up 🟢

### Core Idea
The money market is the high-liquidity, ≤1-year debt segment of the financial system. It enables governments, banks and large institutions to fund short-term cash flows; lets individuals invest small amounts in low-risk assets; and gives the central bank a venue to align market rates with the policy rate. Capital markets (long-term) sit alongside it.

### Key Concepts

#### Key Words
- **Bill of Exchange**: Negotiable instrument with three parties — drawer, drawee, payee. A commercial bank cheque is an example (a draft on the bank by the depositor payable to a designated payee).
- **Credit Controls**: Regulatory restrictions on bank lending.
- **Financial Integration**: How financial markets are tied together geographically (domestically and internationally).
- **Market Maker**: Dealer who buys/sells from inventory ensuring continuous market for investors.
- **Monetary Policy**: Management of money supply and its links to prices, interest rates, and other economic variables.
- **Open Market Operations (OMO)**: Purchase and sale of securities by the central bank in financial markets.
- **Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)**: Established 1975 under the **Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976**, to develop the rural economy.
- **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: Share of total deposits a commercial bank must keep in government or government-approved securities.
- **Underwriters**: Intermediaries who subscribe to the unsubscribed portion of a company's issued securities.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - SLR is the part kept in **G-secs** (vs CRR which is cash with RBI).
> - RRBs: 1975, statutory framework via Regional Rural Banks Act 1976.
> - Bill of exchange: drawer / drawee / payee.
