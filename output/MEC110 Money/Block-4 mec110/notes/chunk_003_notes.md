## Section: Purpose and Uses of Capital Markets 🔴

### Core Idea
Capital markets are the mechanism that brings together buyers and sellers of long-term financial assets so prices are formed for them. They link investors (postponing consumption for higher returns) with borrowers (firms, governments, individuals) who need financing — through debt instruments (bonds, debentures) or equity (shares). They make information transparent, enable quick valuation, lower transaction/search costs, and integrate sectors of the economy.

> **In Simple Terms:** If money markets are the parking lot for cash, capital markets are the housing market — buyers and sellers meet to exchange long-term claims on businesses and governments. The price you pay for a share or bond reflects what the market thinks that future cash stream is worth.

### Key Concepts

#### Capital market structure
- **Money market** — markets for short-term funds (≤1 year).
- **Capital market** — markets for long-term funds (> 1 year).
- Capital markets split into:
  - **Debt markets** — long-term debt contracts (bonds, debentures).
  - **Equity markets** — financial claims on a firm's profits (shares).
- Each splits into:
  - **Primary market** — where new issues are first sold.
  - **Secondary market** — where existing issues are traded.

#### Borrowers and lenders
- **Borrowers**: individuals (mortgages, durables, education), companies (cash-flow + growth), governments (national debt), public-sector firms, foreign agencies.
- **Lenders**: individuals, financial institutions, companies (with idle cash, in money markets).
- **Securities** are receipts (financial claims) issued by borrowers to lenders. They state amount owed, repayment date, and interest rate.

#### Five functions of capital markets
1. Mechanism for matching buyers and sellers of financial assets and **determining their prices**.
2. Make **information processing and dissemination** easier — better investment/sale decisions.
3. Enable **quick valuation** of debt and equity instruments.
4. Provide **operational efficiency** by simplifying transactions and lowering search/transaction costs.
5. Develop **integration**: real ↔ financial sector, debt ↔ equity, short-term ↔ long-term, domestic ↔ foreign funds; channel funds into efficient uses.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Capital market = long-term funds; Money market = short-term funds.
> - Primary = new issues; Secondary = trading of existing issues.
> - 5 functions: matching, information, valuation, efficiency, integration.

## Section: Debt vs Equity as Means of Raising Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
A firm financing its assets must choose how much to raise via **debt** (borrowing) and how much via **equity** (issuing shares). This **capital-structure** decision is informed by financial-ratio analysis across four families: **liquidity, activity, financial leverage, profitability**. Each family answers a different stakeholder concern (creditor, shareholder, manager).

### Key Concepts

#### Capital structure
- If the firm is entirely **equity-financed**, all cash flows go to shareholders.
- If the firm uses **debt + equity**, lenders get risk-free claims first; shareholders get the residual.
- The **capital structure** = mix of debt and equity used.

#### Stakeholders and their concerns
| Stakeholder | Primary concern |
|-------------|------------------|
| Stockholders, bondholders | Long-term profitability |
| Creditors | Whether firm pays on time (short-term solvency) |
| Management | Long-, short-, and day-to-day performance |

#### Four families of ratios

| Family | What it measures |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Liquidity** | Ability of current assets to meet current liabilities |
| **Activity** | Efficiency of resource use |
| **Financial leverage** | Effectiveness of debt management |
| **Profitability** | Ability to generate income relative to sales / assets / equity |

### Mechanisms / Processes

#### Liquidity ratios
- **Current ratio** = Current Assets / Current Liabilities.
- **Quick (acid-test) ratio** = (Current Assets − Inventories) / Current Liabilities.
  - Excludes inventories because they are the **least liquid** current asset.

#### Activity ratios
- **Accounts receivable turnover** = Net Credit Sales / Receivables.
- **Average collection period** = (365 × Receivables) / Net Credit Sales.
- **Total asset turnover** = Net Sales / Total Assets.

#### Financial leverage ratios (a.k.a. "gearing" in Britain)
- More debt-financed operations → more risk.
- **Debt ratio** = Total Liabilities / Total Assets.
- **Debt-equity ratio** = Total Liabilities / Equities.
- **Identity** (since Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Equity):
  - **Debt-equity ratio = Debt ratio / (1 − Debt ratio)**.
- **Cash flow coverage** (from income statement) =
  (EBIT + Lease/Rental Payments + Depreciation) / [Interest + Lease/Rental Payments + Principal Repayment / (1 − T)]
  - EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes; T = tax rate.

#### Profitability ratios
- **Gross profit margin** = Gross Profits / Net Sales.
- **Net profit margin** = Net Income / Net Sales.
- **Operating profit margin** = EBIT / Net Sales.
- **Return on Assets (ROA / ROI)** = Net Income / Total Assets.
- **Return on Equity (ROE)** = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity.

### Definitions
- **Capital structure** ⭐: Mix of debt and equity used to finance the firm's assets.
- **Working capital**: Current Assets − Current Liabilities.
- **Leverage / Gearing**: Firm's ability to service its debt; the more debt-financed, the higher the leverage and risk.
- **EBIT**: Earnings Before Interest and Taxes.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Quick ratio includes inventories. → ✅ Correct: Quick ratio **excludes** inventories.
- ❌ Mistake: Debt-equity ratio uses only long-term debt. → ✅ Correct: It uses **total liabilities** (current + long-term).
- ❌ Mistake: A high ROE always means a healthy firm. → ✅ Correct: ROE can be inflated by **high leverage**, which raises risk.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 ratio families: **Liquidity, Activity, Leverage, Profitability**.
> - Quick = (CA − Inv) / CL.
> - D/E = D / (1 − D).
> - ROE > ROA ⇒ leverage benefit.

## Section: Debt Market Instruments and Their Pricing 🔴

### Core Idea
The main long-term debt instruments are **bonds** — fixed-income securities paying periodic interest and principal at maturity. **Debentures** are bonds with a clause to convert into equity. Beyond plain-vanilla bonds, modern markets have evolved zero-coupon, floating-rate, embedded-option, and commodity-linked bonds. Pricing rests on the **present value of expected cash flows**, with three yield measures (current yield, yield to maturity, yield to call).

### Key Concepts

#### Bond types

| Type | Distinguishing feature |
|------|-------------------------|
| Plain vanilla / straight | Fixed coupon over life, principal at maturity |
| Zero-coupon | No coupon; issued at large discount, redeemed at face value (sometimes with call/put options) |
| Floating-rate | Coupon linked to a benchmark (e.g., T-bill rate) |
| Embedded options | Convertible (debenture), callable (issuer can redeem early), puttable (investor can sell back early) |
| Commodity-linked | Return tied to specified commodity prices |

- Issuers: Government of India (largest borrower; sells short/medium-term bonds via RBI), state governments, government agencies (with guarantee), public-sector firms, financial institutions, private-sector firms.

#### Bond valuation (assuming risk-free, fixed coupon, annual payments, par redemption)

**Bond value formula:**

$$A = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{X}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{M}{(1+r)^T}$$

- A = bond value (₹)
- T = number of years
- X = annual coupon payment (₹)
- r = required return (discount rate)
- M = maturity (face) value
- t = period when cash flow received

#### Price–yield and price–time relationships
- **Price ↔ yield: inverse**. As yield rises, present value falls, so price falls.
- **Price ↔ time: convergence to par**. Bond price must equal par at maturity (assuming no default risk).
  - **Premium bond** — current price (before redemption) > par.
  - **Discount bond** — current price (before redemption) < par.

#### Bond yield measures

| Measure | Formula / definition | Limitation |
|---------|----------------------|-------------|
| **Current Yield** | Annual Interest / Price | Ignores capital gain/loss and time value of money |
| **Yield to Maturity (YTM)** | Discount rate r solving $P = \sum X/(1+r)^t + M/(1+r)^T$ | Often needs trial-and-error |
| **Yield to Call (YTC)** | Same as YTM but uses call price (instead of M) and call date (instead of T) | Only relevant for callable bonds |

### Definitions
- **Bond** ⭐: Long-term debt instrument paying periodic interest and principal at maturity.
- **Debenture** ⭐: Bond with a clause to convert into equity on pre-specified terms.
- **Plain vanilla / straight bond**: Bond with fixed coupon and principal repayment at maturity.
- **Zero-coupon bond**: Issued at deep discount, redeemed at face value, no coupons.
- **Floating-rate bond**: Coupon tied to a benchmark interest rate.
- **Callable bond**: Issuer can redeem early.
- **Puttable bond**: Investor can sell back early.
- **Yield to Maturity (YTM)** ⭐: Discount rate that equates a bond's PV of cash flows to its current price.
- **Yield to Call**: Yield computed assuming bond is held until the call date.
- **Current Yield**: Annual coupon / market price; ignores capital gain and time value.

### Examples
- In India, the **Government of India** is the largest borrower; bonds are sold through RBI.
- State governments and government agencies (with guarantee) also issue bonds.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Bond price = PV of all future cash flows.
> - **Price ↔ Yield: inverse**.
> - At maturity, bond price = par (if no default).
> - 3 yields: **Current Yield, YTM, YTC**.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Current yield includes capital gains. → ✅ Correct: It uses **only annual interest / price**.
- ❌ Mistake: Higher coupon always means higher YTM. → ✅ Correct: YTM depends on **price + cash flows**, not coupon alone.

## Section: Equity Markets — Introduction 🟡

### Core Idea
A stock exchange regulates company listings, forms prices, supervises trading, authorises members, settles transactions, and publishes trade data and prices. Equity markets are split into **primary** (new issues) and **secondary** (existing issues). A handful of capital concepts — authorised, issued, paid-up, par value, equity premium, book value, market value — describe how shareholders' capital and its valuation work.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine a company as a pizza. The "authorised capital" is the maximum number of slices you're allowed to bake; "issued capital" is the slices you actually brought to the table; "paid-up capital" is what people have already paid for. The price someone is willing to pay you for a slice today is its **market value**; the company's accounting basis for one slice is its **book value**.

### Key Concepts

#### Capital concepts (equity)

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Authorised capital** | Maximum capital a company can issue |
| **Issued capital** | Capital the company offers to investors |
| **Paid-up capital** | Capital subscribed and paid by investors (assuming no calls outstanding) |
| **Par / face value** | Equity capital ÷ number of equity shares |
| **Equity premium** | Issue price − Par value (when issue price > par) |
| **Book / intrinsic value** | (Paid-up capital + Reserves & Surplus) / Number of shares = Net Assets / Number of shares |
| **Market value** | Price at which the share is traded in the market |

#### Primary vs secondary equity markets

| Aspect | Primary | Secondary |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| What is sold | New issues | Already-issued shares |
| Where funds go | Issuer receives | Original buyer receives |
| Function | Capital raising | Liquidity and price discovery |

#### Valuation ratios
- **Price-Earnings (P/E) ratio** = Market price per share / Earnings per share.
  - **Earnings per share (EPS)** = (After-tax profit − Preference dividend) / Number of equity shares.
  - Reflects risk, sentiment, liquidity, and growth prospects.
  - Higher P/E → higher earning per share → may increase share demand.
- **Yield (total return)** = (Dividend + Price change) / Initial price.
  - Decomposable as **Dividend yield + Capital-gains/loss yield**.
  - Low-growth firms → high dividend yield, low capital gains; opposite for high-growth firms.
- **Market Value to Book Value ratio** = MV per share / BV per share.
  - > 1 ⇒ wealth has been generated.
  - = 1 ⇒ Earnings/Price ratio (= 1/PE), Yield, and Return on Equity all coincide.

### Definitions
- **Authorised capital** ⭐: Maximum capital a company is allowed to issue.
- **Issued capital** ⭐: Capital offered to investors.
- **Paid-up capital** ⭐: Subscribed capital actually paid (assuming no outstanding calls).
- **Par value (face value)** ⭐: Equity capital ÷ number of equity shares.
- **Equity premium** ⭐: Issue price − par value when issue price > par.
- **Book value (intrinsic value) of a share** ⭐: (Paid-up capital + Reserves & Surplus) / Number of equity shares.
- **Market value**: Trading price.
- **EPS**: After-tax profit − preference dividend, divided by number of equity shares.
- **P/E ratio**: Market price per share / EPS.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Authorised ≥ Issued ≥ Paid-up.
> - Equity premium triggered only when **issue price > par**.
> - Book value uses paid-up + reserves; market value is what the market pays.
> - Total yield = dividend yield + capital-gains yield.
