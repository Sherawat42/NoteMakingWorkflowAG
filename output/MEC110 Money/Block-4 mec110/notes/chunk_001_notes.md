## Section: Concept and Features of the Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The money market is the segment of the financial market that handles short-term (up to one year in India) financial assets which are close substitutes for money. It functions as an **over-the-phone / online wholesale market** that equates short-term surpluses of lenders with the short-term cash needs of borrowers, and it is the first point at which the central bank's monetary policy is transmitted to the rest of the financial system.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of the money market as a giant overnight parking lot for cash. Banks, companies and the government with extra cash for a few days drive in and lend it; those who need cash for a few days drive in and borrow it. The "rent" is the short-term interest rate — and the RBI is the parking attendant who can change rates to influence the whole economy.

### Key Concepts

#### Money market vs capital market
The financial market splits into (i) the **money market** for short-term funds (≤ 1 year) and (ii) the **capital market** for long-term funds. Both connect surplus sectors (households, institutions) with deficit sectors (corporates, government), but the money market emphasises **liquidity and safety**, while the capital market emphasises **return and growth**.

| Aspect | Money Market | Capital Market |
|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Maturity | ≤ 1 year (overnight to 364 days) | > 1 year (long term) |
| Instruments | T-bills, CPs, CDs, call money, repo | Shares, bonds, debentures |
| Returns | Lower, but high liquidity & safety | Higher, with more risk |
| Function | Parks short-term surplus / meets short-term deficit | Mobilises long-term capital |
| Influence | Sets short-term interest rates; transmits monetary policy | Funds investment, growth |

#### Distinctive features (six)
1. **Negotiable instruments** — used by companies to raise funds; safe due to high liquidity.
2. **Short-term + low cost of turnover** — equilibrates short-term lender surpluses with borrower needs.
3. **A market of sub-markets** — call/notice/term money, repos, T-bills, commercial bills, CDs, CPs, inter-bank participation certificates, swaps, futures, options. Free flow of funds across sub-markets is a key requirement.
4. **Geographical concentration** — activities cluster in centres (London, New York globally; Mumbai in India). With screen-based trading, multiple centres rapidly integrate into one national market.
5. **Impersonal character** — no shops or counters; competition is relatively pure.
6. **Wholesale trading** — volumes traded are very large.

### Definitions
- **Money market**: Market for short-term money and financial assets that are close substitutes for money; in India, "short-term" = up to one year. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **RBI definition**: "The centre for dealings, mainly of short-term character, in money assets. It meets the short-term requirements of borrowers and provides liquidity or cash to the lenders." ⭐
- **Vaghul Committee definition**: "The money market is a market for short-term financial assets that are close substitutes for money. The important feature of money market instruments is that it is liquid and can be turned over quickly at low cost, and it provides an avenue for equilibrating the short-term surplus funds of lenders and the requirements of borrowers." ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Money market = short-term, liquid, low-cost, wholesale, over-the-phone.
> - Term "short-term" in India = up to 1 year.
> - Mumbai is the principal Indian money market centre.

## Section: Objectives and Functions of the Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The money market exists to (i) absorb surplus short-term funds, (ii) meet short-term deficits, (iii) give cheap quick access to short-term finance, and (iv) provide the central bank a venue to influence liquidity. It performs critical economy-wide functions — financing trade, transmitting monetary policy, supporting business working capital, and giving banks self-sufficiency.

### Key Concepts

#### Four objectives
1. A parking place to employ short-term surplus funds.
2. Room for overcoming short-term deficits.
3. Reasonable access to short-term funds quickly, adequately, and at reasonable cost.
4. The Central Bank's avenue to influence and regulate liquidity through intervention.

#### Five functions in the economy
1. **Financing trade** — discounts bills of exchange; finances local and international trade; supplies funds to agriculture and small-scale industries.
2. **Implementing central bank policies** — provides the operational base for monetary policy. An integrated money market lets RBI influence sub-markets and align liquidity & short-term rates with policy.
3. **Growth of business and industries** — corporates with surplus cash invest in short-term securities; corporates with shortfalls borrow. Capital market interest rates **benchmark off** money market rates.
4. **Self-sufficiency to commercial banks** — banks invest excess reserves at interest while keeping liquidity, and can borrow from the money market (often cheaper than the central bank) when liquidity-strapped.
5. **Other functions** — promotes savings/investment, supplies non-inflationary finance to the government via T-bills (issuing T-bills does not raise prices the way printing money would).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Compact list: equilibration mechanism, focal point for central-bank intervention, reasonable access at realistic interest rates.
> - Money market rates → benchmark for capital market rates.
> - T-bills = non-inflationary government finance.

## Section: Requisites of a Good Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
A "developed" money market satisfies seven structural conditions covering institutional depth, central-bank strength, mobility of funds, instrument variety, sub-market integration, settlement systems, and supervised participants. London and New York best embody these; Paris, Zurich, Frankfurt, Amsterdam and Vienna substantially do.

### Mechanisms / Processes
The seven requisites:
1. **Institutional development** — political stability + a well-organised commercial banking and financial system.
2. **Strong central bank** — credibility, supervision, ability to implement monetary policy via Open Market Operations.
3. **Financial mobility** — easy inter-sectoral fund flows.
4. **Continuous supply of negotiable securities** — bills of exchange, T-bills, short-term government bonds, with **varied maturity and risk profiles** to match different appetites. Multiple instruments add depth.
5. **Multiple integrated sub-markets** — each specialising in a particular short-term asset; funds must flow between segments to exploit arbitrage; integration ensures uniform interest rates and channels savings into productive working-capital uses.
6. **Integration with the rest of the financial system** — efficient payment & settlement (EFT, Depository System, Delivery-versus-Payment, High-Value Inter-bank Payment System).
7. **Integrity & supervision** — because deals are concluded over the phone with later written confirmation, integrity is *sine qua non*; participants must be licensed and supervised.

### Examples
- **Best examples** of developed money markets: London, New York.
- **Substantially developed**: Paris, Zurich, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, Vienna.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 7 requisites mnemonic: **Institutions, Central bank, Mobility, Variety, Sub-markets, Integration, Integrity**.
> - "Sine qua non" trait of money market: **integrity** (because trading is over the phone).

## Section: Money Market in India — Structure 🟡

### Core Idea
The Indian money market has a **dichotomic structure**: a tightly RBI-regulated organised sector coexists with a largely unregulated unorganised sector of indigenous bankers, moneylenders, finance companies, chit funds, and nidhis. The organised market is dominated by a small number of large players, with Mumbai as its leading centre.

### Key Concepts

#### Organised sector
- Fully under RBI control with standardised rules and high institutionalisation/instrumentalisation.
- Transactions are large-volume, dominated by few large players.
- Still **underdeveloped** by international standards.
- A **conglomeration of sub-markets**: call money market, bill market, and other sub-markets.
- **Leading centres**: Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, Delhi, Bangalore, Ahmedabad. Mumbai houses RBI HQ, two leading stock exchanges, the bullion exchange, and an organised G-sec market — putting it on par with London and New York.

### Definitions
- **Dichotomic structure**: Simultaneous existence of organised and unorganised money markets in India. ⭐

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Indian money market = Organised (RBI-regulated) + Unorganised (indigenous).
> - Mumbai → at par with London & New York for Indian money market activity.

## Section: Instruments of the Organised Money Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The organised money market is a portfolio of instruments — call/notice/term money, T-bills, commercial bills (BRS), CDs, CPs, banker's acceptance, repos. Common features: **short duration, large volume, deregulated interest rates, high liquidity, high safety**. Each instrument plays a specific role in liquidity management.

### Key Concepts

#### Call / Notice / Term money market 🔴
- **Unsecured** segment for very short liquidity management.
- **Maturity tiers**:
  - Call money: overnight (up to 1 day).
  - Notice money: > 2 days, up to 14 days.
  - Term money: 15 days to 1 year.
- **Inter-bank market** — banks with surplus lend to needy banks; commonly used to meet **statutory reserve requirements** with the RBI.
- **No collateral, no brokers**; settlement via current accounts at RBI.
- Low documentation + same-day settlement → strong incentive for intra-day funding.
- Interest rates are **highly volatile** and considered the **barometer of the money market** — rates in other instruments are pegged to call rates.
- **Seasonality**: April, May, October → high rates and high volatility; January, August → lower and steady.
- **DFHI** (Discount and Finance House of India), set up 1988, was meant to broaden the call market and act as **market maker**, but has limited resources.
- Mumbai handles **85–90%** of call business; other centres: Kolkata, Delhi, Chennai, Bengaluru, Ahmedabad.
- **Major players**: commercial banks + DFHI (both borrow and lend); UTI, LIC, IDBI, NABARD permitted **as lenders only**.

#### Treasury bills (T-bills) 🔴
- Short-term **government** security; promissory notes; sold by RBI on behalf of Government of India.
- Three current maturities: **91 days, 182 days, 364 days**.
- **Zero-coupon**: pay no interest. Issued at a discount, redeemed at face value at maturity.
- **No default risk** — government paper.
- **Approved SLR assets** for banks.
- Sold by **auction to highest bidder**; **price-based, multiple-price** auctions.
- DFHI is the market maker.
- Best instrument for **hedging against call-loan-rate volatility**.

**Worked example (T-bill discount):**
- 91-day T-bill, face value ₹100, issued at ₹98.20.
- Discount = ₹100 − ₹98.20 = ₹1.80.
- Investor's return = redemption value − issue price = ₹100 − ₹98.20 = ₹1.80 over 91 days.

#### Bills Rediscounting Scheme (BRS) — commercial bill market 🟡
- Operative since **1970**.
- Commercial banks issue **derivative usance promissory notes** (≤ 90 days maturity) **backed by genuine trade bills** of similar tenor.
- These can be **rediscounted** by the holder via endorsement and delivery.
- **Stamp duty waived** on usance promissory notes since **1989**.
- Eligible issuers: all commercial banks; eligible discounters: commercial banks, select financial institutions, urban cooperative banks.
- DFHI provides daily bid rates → assures liquidity.
- **Major source of funds for banks**: borrowing under BRS is **not counted as a liability** when calculating Net Demand and Time Liability (NDTL).
- Attractive for investors due to high liquidity, safety, and returns.

### Definitions
- **Call money** ⭐: Unsecured, overnight (up to 1 day) inter-bank lending for very short-term liquidity management.
- **Notice money** ⭐: 2–14 day money.
- **Term money** ⭐: 15 days to 1 year money.
- **Treasury bill (T-bill)** ⭐: Short-term zero-coupon government security (91/182/364 days), issued at discount and redeemed at face value.
- **DFHI**: Discount and Finance House of India (1988) — market maker in call and T-bill markets. ⭐
- **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)**: Portion of total deposits a commercial bank must keep in government or government-approved securities.
- **NDTL (Net Demand and Time Liability)**: Bank's net liability base used by RBI for reserve calculations.
- **Usance promissory note**: A promissory note payable at a fixed period after sight or after a specified date (used as the legal vehicle in BRS).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treasury bills carry coupon interest. → ✅ Correct: T-bills are **zero-coupon**; the return is the discount on issue price.
- ❌ Mistake: Call money is collateralised. → ✅ Correct: It is **unsecured**, no collateral, no brokers.
- ❌ Mistake: Notice money = overnight money. → ✅ Correct: Notice money = > 2 days up to 14 days; **call** = overnight.
- ❌ Mistake: All RBI-issued money market instruments pay interest. → ✅ Correct: T-bills pay no interest — they are issued at a discount.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Call < Notice < Term: ≤1 day | 2–14 days | 15 d–1 yr.
> - T-bill maturities: **91 / 182 / 364 days** (zero-coupon, multiple-price auction).
> - BRS bills ≤ 90 days; backing: genuine trade bills; stamp duty waived 1989.
> - Call rate = barometer of money market; Mumbai ≈ 85–90% of call business.
> - DFHI established 1988 as market maker.
