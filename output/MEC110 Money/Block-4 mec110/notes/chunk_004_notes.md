## Section: Tobin's q & Functions of Secondary Markets 🟡

<!-- See chunk 003 for start of "Equity: Markets and Volatility". This continues that section. -->

### Core Idea
Beyond simple Market-to-Book, **Tobin's q** values a firm by relating market value of *all* claims (equity + liabilities) to the *replacement cost* of its assets. Secondary markets serve issuers (price feedback) and investors (liquidity, escape valve), and they reduce search and transaction costs.

### Key Concepts

#### Tobin's q ratio (named after Nobel laureate James Tobin)
$$q = \frac{\text{Market value of equities and liabilities}}{\text{Estimated replacement cost of assets}}$$

| Difference vs Market/Book | Tobin's q |
|---------------------------|-----------|
| Numerator includes equity only | Includes equity **+ liabilities** (all wealth) |
| Denominator uses **book value** | Uses **replacement cost** of assets |

- **Interpretation:** q > 1 ⇒ market values the firm's claims above what it would cost to rebuild it ⇒ wealth generated; q < 1 ⇒ the opposite.

#### Functions of secondary markets

**To the issuer:**
- **Price information**: latest prices reveal what the market thinks of the firm; helps the issuer evaluate the use of past primary-market funds and gauge appetite for new issues.

**To investors:**
- **Escape clause**: original IPO buyers can sell for cash if the share underperforms — or to lock in capital gains.
- Without secondary-market liquidity, investors would demand higher returns/dividends → harder for firms to raise capital.

**General economic functions:**
- Provide **fair price information** and **liquidity**.
- Reduce **search costs** by matching buyers and sellers.
- Reduce **transaction costs**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Tobin's q numerator = equity + liabilities; denominator = **replacement** cost (not book).
> - Secondary markets: price info + liquidity + lower search/transaction costs.

## Section: Stock-Market Health Indicators — Volatility, Liquidity, Size, Cost 🔴

### Core Idea
A stock market's developmental maturity is gauged across four parameters: **volatility** (price fluctuation frequency), **liquidity** (trade volume without large price impact), **size** (market capitalisation as % of GDP), and **transaction cost** (explicit + implicit). High volatility deters investors; high liquidity, large size, low costs all signal a developed market.

### Key Concepts

#### Volatility
- Measure of how frequently a stock's price changes over time.
- High volatility → investors avoid; **affects outside fund flows** → macroeconomic dimension.
- Drivers: speculation, trading & settlement systems, government policies, interest rates, inflation, regulatory framework.

#### Liquidity (two measures)
| Measure | Formula | Captures |
|---------|---------|----------|
| **Turnover ratio** | Total value of domestic shares traded / Total market capitalisation | Trading activity relative to market size |
| **Value-traded ratio** | Total value of domestic shares traded on major exchanges / GDP | Macro-level liquidity (organised trading as share of GDP) |

- **High turnover** often signals **low transaction costs**.
- A large market with low trading volume can still have low turnover.

#### Size of the market
- **Market capitalisation ratio** = Market capitalisation / GDP.
  - Market capitalisation = market price × number of equity shares (for all listed firms).
- Assumption: market size positively correlates with capital-mobilising and risk-diversifying ability.

#### Transaction costs
- **Explicit**: brokerage, stamp duty, commission.
- **Implicit**: clearing & settlement costs, bad delivery, paperwork.
- Low costs → higher trading volume → higher value-traded ratio (a positive feedback loop).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 parameters: **Volatility, Liquidity, Size, Cost**.
> - 2 liquidity measures: **Turnover ratio** (vs market cap) and **Value-traded ratio** (vs GDP).
> - Size measure: **Market capitalisation ratio** (cap / GDP).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: A larger market always means high liquidity. → ✅ Correct: Size and liquidity are different — a large market with low trading is illiquid.
- ❌ Mistake: Tobin's q = Market/Book. → ✅ Correct: q's numerator includes **liabilities**, and the denominator uses **replacement cost**.

## Section: Indices of Share Prices 🔴

### Core Idea
A stock-price index uses a representative basket of stocks to track market behaviour. Good indices use **high market capitalisation + high liquidity** stocks. Indices can be **price averages** (Dow Jones, Nikkei 225 — equal weighting / arithmetic or geometric mean) or **price indices weighted by market cap** (BSE Sensex, NSE Nifty). Within market-cap weighting, refinements include **full**, **free-float**, and **modified** capitalisation methods.

### Key Concepts

#### Characteristics of a good index
1. Stocks have **high market capitalisation**.
2. Stocks are **highly liquid**.

Both ensure the index reflects movements in shares that are actively traded and economically significant.

#### How indices are calculated
- For a given day, the index = **percentage of aggregate market value of selected stocks today** to **average market value of the same stocks during the base period**.
- Example: BSE Sensex = weighted average of prices of **30 select stocks**.
- Top constituents change as share prices change → index composition is dynamic.

#### Price averages vs price indices
| Method | Examples |
|--------|----------|
| **Price averages** | Dow Jones (started 1884 by Charles Dow with 12 stocks; 30 since 1928), Nikkei Dow 225 (Japan, 225 shares — average prices, not market cap), Financial Times Ordinary Share Index (1935 — geometric mean of 30 shares) |
| **Modern indices (multiply price × shares)** | Give more weight to large-cap firms |

#### Two main calculation categories
1. **Equal-weighted index** — simple arithmetic mean of sample shares relative to base.
2. **Value-weighted index** — aggregate market capitalisation relative to base.

#### Sub-classification of market-cap weighted methods

| Method | What it does | Notes |
|--------|---------------|-------|
| **Full market capitalisation** | Number of outstanding shares × market price | Largest firms dominate. **BSE Sensex and Nifty followed this initially** |
| **Free-float method** | Considers only **freely available shares** — excludes strategic & ESOP-locked stakes | Closely-held companies → lower weight; popular globally |
| **Modified capitalisation** | Caps the influence of largest stocks | Prevents disproportionate dominance |

#### Other methods
- **Price-weighted index** — sum of prices, related to base value.
- **Equal weighting** — each stock weighs equally.

#### Major indices

**India:**
| Index | Started | Method | Constituents |
|-------|---------|--------|---------------|
| **BSE Sensex (S&P BSE Sensex)** | 1986 | Free-float, market-weighted | 30 large, financially sound BSE-listed firms |
| **NSE Nifty (S&P CNX Nifty)** | 8 July 1996, with CRISIL & S&P | Weighted average (proportional to market cap) | 50 scrips selected on low impact cost, high liquidity, market cap |
| Economic Times Ordinary Share Price Index | — | — | — |
| Financial Express Ordinary Price Index | — | — | — |
| RBI Index of Security Prices | — | — | — |
| Regional indices (Ahmedabad, Chennai, Delhi, Kolkata) | — | — | — |

- **NSE** started capital-market trading in **1994**; volume soon overtook BSE.

**Abroad:**
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average** (NYSE).
- **NASDAQ** (National Association of Securities Dealers Automatic Quotation).
- **Nikkei** (Japan).
- **Standard & Poor**.

### Definitions
- **Market capitalisation** ⭐: Sum of the market value of all stocks included in the index.
- **Liquidity** (in indexing context): Ease with which a share can be bought or sold near the current market price.
- **Free-float method** ⭐: Index methodology counting only freely tradable shares, excluding strategic and locked-in stakes.
- **Value-weighted index** ⭐: Index reflecting aggregate market capitalisation of constituents.
- **Equal-weighted index** ⭐: Simple arithmetic average of constituent share prices.
- **Coupon**: Periodic interest paid on a bond — face value × coupon rate. (Listed in unit's key words.)
- **Coupon rate**: Annual coupon / face value.

### Examples
- BSE Sensex = free-float, 30 stocks.
- Nifty = 50 scrips, weighted by market cap.
- Nikkei 225 = 225 shares, **average prices**, not cap.
- FT Ordinary Share Index = geometric mean of 30 shares.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Good index = **high market cap + high liquidity**.
> - 2 broad categories: **equal-weighted** vs **value-weighted**.
> - 3 cap-weight subtypes: **Full / Free-float / Modified**.
> - BSE Sensex (1986, free-float, 30 stocks); Nifty (1996, market-cap-weighted, 50 stocks).
> - Dow Jones since 1884; 30 stocks since 1928.

## Section: Unit 12 — Bond Markets: Introduction & Definition 🔴

### Core Idea
Bond markets are where companies and governments raise long-term loans by issuing **bonds** — loan-taking certificates. The bond market (a.k.a. **debt market, fixed-income market, credit market**) splits into a **primary market** (new issues) and **secondary market** (trading). It sets interest rates for other financial products like mortgages and loans, influencing borrowing costs across the economy.

> **In Simple Terms:** Instead of begging a bank for a ₹500-crore loan, a company can issue thousands of "loan tickets" (bonds) directly to investors. Investors give cash now in exchange for a promise of regular interest payments and the principal back at the end. The bond market is the place where these tickets are first sold and later traded.

### Key Concepts

#### Bond market structure

| Segment | What it does |
|---------|---------------|
| **Primary** | New bonds first sold; transactions directly between issuer and buyer. "New issues market." |
| **Secondary** | Existing bonds bought and sold; provides liquidity (sell before maturity); broker-mediated; can be packaged into pension funds, mutual funds, life insurance. |

#### Why the bond market matters
- Sets interest rates for mortgages, loans, and other products.
- Determines **borrowing cost** for corporations and governments → affects investment and spending.

### Definitions
- **Bond market** ⭐: Collective name for all trades and issues of debt securities; also called debt market, fixed-income market, or credit market.
- **Bond** (preview of full Chunk 005 definition): A loan-taking certificate — investors lend money in exchange for fixed/floating interest payments over a defined period.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Bond market = primary (new issues) + secondary (existing bonds).
> - Aliases: **debt market, fixed-income market, credit market**.
> - Bond markets influence rates on mortgages, loans → affect borrowing costs across the economy.
