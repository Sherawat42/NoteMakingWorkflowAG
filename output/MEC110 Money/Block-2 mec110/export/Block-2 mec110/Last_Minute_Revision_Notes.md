### Nature and Functions of Money 🔴

**Defining Money**

**Why Money Exists — The Barter Problem**

**Five Criteria for an Effective Money Commodity**
1. Easily standardized — value is simple to ascertain.
2. Widely accepted.
3. Divisible — to make change easy.
4. Easy to carry.
5. Does not deteriorate quickly.

**Near Money**
- Money market funds
- Bank and Post Office deposits
- Government treasury securities
- Bonds near their redemption date
- Foreign currencies (USD, EUR, GBP, JPY)
- Equities/shares of the corporate sector

**The Four Functions of Money**
| Function | What it means | Why it matters |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Medium of Exchange** | Used to pay for goods/services in any transaction | Eliminates the double-coincidence-of-wants problem; enables specialization and division of labour |
| **Unit of Account** | Common yardstick for measuring value (like pounds for weight, miles for distance) | Reduces the number of relative prices needed — N goods need only N prices in money, not N(N−1)/2 barter ratios |
| **Store of Value** | Holds purchasing power over time so wealth can be carried into the future | Ensures wealth is liquid, divisible, and broadly accepted; effectiveness reduced by inflation |
| **Standard of Deferred Payment** | Allows transactions and their settlement to be separated in time | Source of credit and debt in the economy |

**Unit of Account — why it matters with many goods**

**Store of Value — limitations**
- **Money**: Anything that functions as a means of payment (medium of exchange), unit of account, and store of value. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Barter**: A system where goods and services are exchanged for other goods and services. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Near Money**: Highly liquid non-cash assets that are easily convertible into cash. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Double Coincidence of Wants**: The barter requirement that two traders must each want what the other has.
- **Transaction Cost**: Time/effort spent exchanging goods or services.
- **Legal Tender**: Money officially recognized for the settlement of debts.

### Measures of Money Supply (M0–M4) 🔴

**Monetary Aggregates — From Narrowest to Broadest**
| Aggregate | Composition | Liquidity | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| **M0** (monetary base / base money / **high-powered money** / central bank money) | Currency (coins + notes) in circulation **+** demand deposits of commercial banks at the central bank | Most liquid claims on central bank | Directly controlled by the central bank via **open-market operations** (buying/selling short-term government bonds) |
| **M1** | Currency in circulation **+** demand deposits held by the **non-bank** public in commercial banks | Highly liquid | **Excludes** currency held by commercial banks and demand deposits held by commercial banks at the central bank. Includes most of M0 and is generally larger than M0. |
| **M2** | M1 **+** savings deposits with unrestricted access (demand deposits) | Near-liquid | Not instantly liquid, but close. |
| **M3** | M1 **+** time deposits | Broader | Captures broader liquidity. |
| **M4** | M3 **+** other forms of broad money (longer-term deposits, post office deposits, etc.) | Broadest | Includes less liquid financial assets. |

**Narrow vs Broad Money**
| Aspect | Narrow Money | Broad Money |
|--------|--------------|-------------|
| Liquidity | Instantly usable, no restriction | Includes claims requiring notice/conversion |
| Examples | Currency, demand deposits | Time deposits, money-market mutual funds |
| Measures | M0, M1 | M2, M3, M4 |
- **Money Supply**: Total amount of money available in an economy at a particular point in time (a **stock**, not a flow). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Monetary Base / High-Powered Money / M0**: Fully liquid claims on the central bank held by the private sector — currency in circulation plus demand deposits of commercial banks at the central bank. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Open-Market Operations**: Central bank trading (usually short-term) government bonds with the private sector to change the monetary base.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- ❌ Mistake: Including currency held by commercial banks in M1 → ✅ Correct: M1 excludes currency held by commercial banks and excludes their demand deposits at the central bank.
- ❌ Mistake: Calling money supply a flow → ✅ Correct: Money supply is a **stock** measured at a point in time.

**Quick Recall:**

- M1 = currency + demand deposits of non-bank public.
- M2 = M1 + savings deposits.
- M3 = M1 + time deposits.
- M4 = M3 + other broad money (e.g., post office deposits).
- M0 is directly controllable by the central bank via OMO; broader aggregates are not.

### Money, Credit and the Macroeconomy 🔴

**The Money Demand → Aggregate Demand Mechanism**
1. ↑ Money demand → ↑ interest rates → ↓ investment + ↓ consumer spending → ↓ aggregate demand → possible recession.
2. **Central bank response**: Buy government securities → ↑ commercial-bank reserves → ↑ lending capacity → ↓ interest rates → ↑ borrowing/spending → ↑ aggregate demand.
3. **Caveat**: Near full capacity, ↑ aggregate demand → ↑ price level (inflation) rather than ↑ output.

**Timing Matters**

**Quick Recall:**

- Central bank counter-tool: open market operations (buy securities → ↑ bank reserves → ↑ lending).
- Near full capacity, expansion = inflation, not output.

### The Credit Channel of Monetary Policy 🔴

**Why a Credit Channel Exists — Asymmetric Information**

**Two Sub-Channels — Comparison**
| Aspect | Bank Lending Channel | Balance Sheet Channel |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Focus | Banks' **ability** to lend | Borrowers' **financial health** |
| Mechanism | Monetary policy alters bank reserves → loan supply | Monetary policy alters asset prices → net worth → collateral → borrowing capacity |
| Expansion path | ↑ Reserves → ↑ Loans at ↓ rates → ↑ Investment & consumption → ↑ AD | ↑ Asset prices → ↑ Net worth → ↑ Collateral value → ↓ Borrowing costs → ↑ Investment & spending → ↑ AD |
| Contraction path | ↑ Rates → ↓ Reserves → tighter lending → ↓ AD | Falling asset prices → ↓ Net worth → ↓ Collateral → restricted borrowing → ↓ AD |
| Seminal paper | Bernanke & Blinder (1988) | Bernanke & Gertler (1995) |

**1) The Bank Lending Channel — Mechanism**

**2) The Balance Sheet Channel — Mechanism**

**Cyclical Variation in Effectiveness**
- **Downturns**: Even with expansionary policy, banks may be **hesitant to lend** due to higher perceived risk → channel weakened.
- **Booms**: Easier credit can **exacerbate** expansions.
- **2008 crisis**: Ivashina & Scharfstein (2010) found the **impaired credit channel significantly contributed to the depth and duration** of the recession.
- **Credit Channel**: The transmission mechanism by which monetary policy affects the economy through the **availability and terms of credit**, alongside the interest-rate channel. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Bank Lending Channel**: Sub-channel through which monetary policy affects banks' ability to lend (via reserves and cost of funds). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Balance Sheet Channel**: Sub-channel through which monetary policy affects borrowers' financial position (via asset prices, net worth, collateral). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Asymmetric Information**: Lenders (banks) have more information about borrower creditworthiness than external investors.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- ❌ Mistake: Assuming the credit channel works equally in booms and busts → ✅ Correct: In downturns, banks may not lend even when policy is expansionary — channel weakens.

**Quick Recall:**

- Bank Lending → bank's **ability** to lend; Balance Sheet → borrower's **financial health**.

### Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher) 🔴

**Transaction Version**
| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **M** | Quantity of money (money supply) |
| **V** | **Transaction velocity** of money — number of times a rupee changes hands per period |
| **P** | Price per transaction |
| **T** | Number of transactions per period |

**Income Version**
|--------|---------|
| **M** | Quantity of money |
| **V** | **Income velocity** of money (replaces transaction velocity) |
| **P** | Price level |
| **Y** | Real output |

**Deriving Money Demand from the Quantity Theory (Laidler, 1977)**

**Criticisms of the Quantity Theory**
| Critic | Critique |
|--------|----------|
| **J.M. Keynes** (*General Theory*) | Inflation is driven by **aggregate demand**, not money supply. At full employment, AD > AS creates an inflationary gap **independent of money supply changes**. |
| **Paul Krugman** (Nobel laureate) | Theory **fails in a liquidity trap** — when interest rates hit minimum, further fall is unexpected, and monetary policy is ineffective. |
| **Ludwig von Mises** (Austrian-American) | The theory does not focus on the **money demand side** — only equilibrium derivation; not applicable in disequilibrium. |
| Various economists | The quantity theory is dismissed by some as a **tautology** rather than a theory. |
- **Quantity Theory of Money**: MV = PT — relationship between money supply, velocity, price, and transactions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Velocity of Money**: Number of times a unit of money is exchanged in a period; transaction velocity (V_T) or income velocity (V_Y).
- **Liquidity Trap**: Situation when interest rate hits its minimum and further fall is not expected — monetary policy becomes ineffective. ⭐ (exam-important)

**Quick Recall:**
- **MV = PT** (transactions); **MV = PY** (income).
- Theory is about supply, but yields Md = kY in equilibrium.
- 4 critiques: Keynes (AD-driven inflation), Krugman (liquidity trap), Mises (no demand side), tautology charge.

### Liquidity Preference Theory (Keynes) 🔴

**The Three Motives — Comparison**
| Motive | Formula | Driver | Interest sensitivity |
|--------|---------|--------|----------------------|
| **Transaction** (M_d^T) | M_d^T = α(PY) | Income/transactions | Generally inelastic (some economists: elastic above i₃ when opportunity cost is high) |
| **Precautionary** (M_d^p) | M_d^p = β(PY) | Income/uncertainty about future | Generally inelastic (similar caveat as above) |
| **Speculative** (M_d^sp) | M_d^sp = (P×W)·f(i) | Interest rate / bond price expectations | Strongly interest-elastic |

**1) Transaction Motive**
- Nominal: $M_d^T = \alpha(PY)$
- Real: $M_d^T / P = \alpha Y$
- α = fraction of nominal income demanded for transactions.

**2) Precautionary Motive**
- Nominal: $M_d^p = \beta(PY)$
- Real: $M_d^p / P = \beta Y$
- β = fraction of nominal income demanded for precaution.

**3) Speculative Motive — The Bond Market Logic**

**Keynes' Bullish/Bearish Logic**
- There is a **normal interest rate**.
- If i > normal → individual expects i to **fall** → expects ↑ bond prices → **bearish on cash** = capital gain + interest → holds **bonds**.
- If i < normal → individual expects i to **rise** → expects ↓ bond prices → **bullish on cash** = capital loss but still earns coupon → holds **bonds only if** coupon > capital loss; otherwise holds **all cash**.
- At aggregate level, individuals' weights smooth out → **aggregate speculative Md = smooth function of i**.

**Total Money Demand (Keynes)**

**Mankiw's Compact Form**
- Nominal: $M_d = P \cdot L(i, Y)$
- Real: $M_d / P = L(i, Y)$

**Criticisms of Liquidity Preference Theory**
1. **Single purse problem**: People don't keep separate purses for each motive; in reality one common balance serves all needs.
2. **Empirical weakness**: Fails to fully explain observed fluctuations in interest rates and money demand. Other factors (inflation expectations, growth, central-bank policy) also drive these.
3. **Homogeneous expectations**: Assumes all individuals share the same interest-rate and income expectations; in reality expectations are heterogeneous.
- **Transaction Motive**: Demand for money to bridge gap between receipts and regular planned payments. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Precautionary Motive**: Demand for money as a buffer for unforeseen future expenses. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Speculative Motive**: Demand for money tied to expectations about future interest rates / bond prices. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Capital Gain**: Profit due to asset price appreciation (e.g., bond price rise).
- **Bullish Expectation**: Expectation of price increase / interest rate fall.
- **Bearish Expectation**: Expectation of price decrease / interest rate rise.
- **Perpetual Bond**: A bond with infinite maturity; V = C/i.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Saying transaction & precautionary demand depend on i → ✅ Correct: Both are interest-**inelastic** in Keynes (some say elastic above a threshold).
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing bullish/bearish with rate movements directly → ✅ Correct: Bullish on **bonds** = expects bond **prices** to rise = expects **interest rates to fall**.
- ❌ Mistake: Applying V = C/i to all bonds → ✅ Correct: This formula is for **perpetual** bonds (infinite maturity).

**Quick Recall:**
- Md = M_d^T + M_d^p + M_d^sp.
- Transaction & Precautionary = f(Y); Speculative = f(i).
- Md = αY + βY + (P×W)·f(i).
- Bond value (perpetual): V = C/i — inverse of interest rate.
- 3 criticisms: single purse, empirical weakness, homogeneous expectations.
- Builds on: Cambridge Approach (transaction k → α here).

### Baumol-Tobin Model of Transaction Demand 🔴

**Setup**
- Annual spending: ₹Y, withdrawn from a bank account paying interest **i**.
- Withdrawals are made in equal lumps; cash is spent at a **constant rate**.
- Each bank visit incurs **transaction cost b** (e.g., travel cost).

**Average Cash Holding for Q Visits**
- 1 visit/year: opens with Y, ends with 0 → average = **Y/2**.
- 2 visits/year: half-year cycles → average = **Y/4**.
- Q visits/year: average = **Y/(2Q)**.

**Costs**
- **Transaction cost** of Q visits: $bQ$.
- **Opportunity cost** (interest forgone on average holding): $\frac{iY}{2Q}$.

**Average Money Holding (Optimum)**

**Worked Example (from text)**
- i = 5% = 0.05/year
- b = ₹4 per visit
- $Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{0.05 \times 1{,}00{,}000}{2 \times 4}} = \sqrt{\frac{5000}{8}} = \sqrt{625} = 25$ visits.
- $A = \sqrt{\frac{4 \times 1{,}00{,}000}{2 \times 0.05}} = \sqrt{\frac{4{,}00{,}000}{0.1}} = \sqrt{40{,}00{,}000} = ₹2000$.

**Comparative Statics**
- ↑ i → ↑ Q* (more visits) → ↓ A (less cash held). Higher rates make holding cash costly.
- ↑ Y → ↑ Q* and ↑ A but **square-root relation** (so A grows slower than Y — economies of scale in cash management).
- ↑ b → ↓ Q* and ↑ A. Higher transaction cost ⇒ fewer visits, more cash per visit.

**Criticisms of Baumol-Tobin**
1. Assumes spending happens at **constant rate**, but real spending is uneven and uncertain.
2. **Hard to quantify** transaction costs in practice.
- **Baumol-Tobin Model / Cash Management Model**: Optimal cash-holding model that minimizes total transaction + opportunity cost; A = √(bY/2i). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **EOQ Analogy**: Cash-management problem mirrors inventory-management problem with purchase, order, and carrying costs.

- ❌ Mistake: Saying Md grows linearly in Y → ✅ Correct: Square-root relation — economies of scale.

**Quick Recall:**
- **Q* = √(iY/2b)**, **A = √(bY/2i)**.
- At optimum, transaction cost = opportunity cost.
- Square-root law → Md grows slower than income.
- 2 criticisms: constant-rate spending, hard to measure b.
- Builds on: Keynes' transaction motive (previous section, this chunk).
- Connects to: Friedman's modern theory (later this chunk) — both treat money like an asset.

### Tobin's Theory of Speculative Demand 🔴

**Mathematical Setup**
- V = value of bonds; r = rate of return; θ = rate of risk.
- **Total return**: $R = rV$
- **Total risk**: $\delta = \theta V$
- Combining: $R = \frac{r}{\theta} \delta$
- Let $\beta = r/\theta$: $R = \beta \delta$ → **linear positive relation between R and δ** (the line **OZ**).

**Geometric Construction (Figure 5.3 in text)**
- **Panel (a)**: Risk (horizontal), Return (vertical).
- IC₁, IC₂ = indifference curves over (risk, return) — higher utility on IC₂.
- Line **OZ** = opportunity set; investor cannot move off it.
- **Optimum at point E** where OZ is tangent to highest reachable IC.
- **Panel (b)**: Wealth axis (vertical) shows the bonds–cash split.
  - At E, bond holding = OD; cash = WD (where W = total wealth).
  - Line **OM** maps each portfolio combination back to a (risk, return) point on OZ.

**Effect of an Interest-Rate Change**

**Tobin vs Keynes — Key Difference**
| Aspect | Keynes (Liquidity Preference) | Tobin (Portfolio Theory) |
|--------|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Portfolio choice | All cash OR all bonds (corner solution) | Mix of cash AND bonds (interior solution) |
| Driver | Expectation about future interest rates | Risk-return optimization (uncertainty) |
| Result | Discontinuous demand at individual level | Smooth demand at individual level |
- **Tobin's Theory of Speculative Demand**: Portfolio-choice model where investors hold a mix of cash and bonds; speculative Md is driven by uncertainty (risk preferences), not expectations alone. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Indifference Curve (over risk-return)**: Combinations of risk and return that yield equal utility; higher curve = higher utility.

**Quick Recall:**
- R = βδ, β = r/θ.
- Optimum where OZ is tangent to highest IC.
- ↓ i → OZ rotates clockwise → ↓ bonds, ↑ cash.
- **Tobin: cash + bonds (mix). Keynes: all cash OR all bonds.**
- Builds on: Keynes' speculative motive — Tobin generalizes it to portfolio theory.
- Connects to: Modern portfolio theory and the term "liquidity preference as behaviour towards risk".

### Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory of Money 🔴

**Friedman's 1970 Money Demand Function**

**Variables Explained**
| Symbol | Meaning | Sign of relation with Md |
|--------|---------|---------------------------|
| **P** | Price level | + (↑ P → ↓ real balance → people demand more nominal money) |
| **Y_p** | **Permanent income** — weighted average of past & present incomes; proxy for human wealth (labour earnings) | + (↑ Y_p → ↑ Md) |
| **W** | Non-human wealth (land, machines) | + (more wealth → more money held) |
| **i_m** | Return on money itself | + (↑ i_m → people willing to hold more money) |
| **i_b** | Return on bonds | − (↑ i_b → capital loss / better alternative → ↓ Md) |
| **i_e** | Return on equities | − (↑ i_e → ↑ dividends attract investors → ↓ Md) |
| **π^e** | Expected inflation rate | − (↑ π^e → ↓ purchasing power → spend/invest now → ↓ Md) |
| **u** | All other factors (tastes & preferences) | Either direction |

**Expected Inflation Formula**

**Why "Modern" / "Restatement"**
- Classical QTM: about money supply.
- Friedman: same equation reframed as **demand for an asset**.
- **Money is just one asset in a wealth portfolio** — same logic as demanding bonds, stocks, land.

**Friedman vs Keynes**
| Aspect | Friedman | Keynes |
|--------|----------|--------|
| View of money | Asset like any other | Special — three motives (T, P, sp) |
| Wealth concept | Permanent income (Y_p) + non-human wealth (W) | Current income (Y) + real wealth (W) |
| Determinants | Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u | i and Y |
| Stability of velocity | Stable (predictable Md) | Unstable (depends on speculative motive) |
- **Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory**: Md = f(Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u) × P — money demand as demand for an asset. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Permanent Income (Y_p)**: Weighted average of past and present incomes; serves as proxy for human wealth in money-demand functions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Non-human Wealth (W)**: Tangible/financial wealth excluding human capital (e.g., land, machines, financial assets).
- **Expected Inflation (π^e)**: Anticipated rate of change of price level.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating Friedman's theory as identical to Fisher's → ✅ Correct: Friedman **restates** it as demand-for-asset; Fisher's is supply-side mechanical.
- ❌ Mistake: Using current income Y in Friedman's function → ✅ Correct: Use **permanent income Y_p**.
- ❌ Mistake: Saying ↑ i_b → ↑ Md → ✅ Correct: ↑ i_b → ↓ Md (bond is more attractive than money).

**Quick Recall:**
- Md = f(Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u) × P.
- Permanent income Y_p replaces current Y.
- +Md drivers: P, Y_p, W, i_m. −Md drivers: i_b, i_e, π^e.
- Treats money as an asset → "modern quantity theory".
- Builds on: Quantity Theory (Fisher) and Cambridge approach.
- Compares with: Keynes' Liquidity Preference Theory.
| Theory | Core equation/idea | Key contribution |
|--------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Fisher's Quantity Theory** | MV = PT | Direct link between money and prices; theory of supply with derivable demand |
| **Cambridge** | Md = k(PY) | Models money demand directly as fraction of nominal income |
| **Keynes' Liquidity Preference** | Md = f(Y, i) via three motives | Speculative motive introduces interest-rate elasticity |
| **Baumol-Tobin** | A = √(bY/2i) | Optimum cash-holding from EOQ-style cost minimization |
| **Tobin's Speculative Demand** | Portfolio of cash + bonds | Negative i ↔ Md, positive i ↔ bond demand; mix vs corner solution |
| **Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory** | Md = f(Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u)P | Money as just another asset |
- **Capital Gain**: Profit from sale of an asset (stocks, real estate, bonds) above purchase price. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Transactive Motive for Money Demand**: Need for money to facilitate day-to-day transactions (buying goods/services). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Precautionary Motive for Money Demand**: Holding money as a buffer/reserve for unexpected future expenses or emergencies. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Speculative Motive for Money Demand**: Holding money to seize favourable market/investment opportunities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Velocity of Money**: Rate at which money is exchanged in an economy — number of times money is spent in a given period; high velocity = rapid circulation. ⭐ (exam-important)

**Quick Recall:**
- Md drivers: i (−), Y (+), P (+), risk on alts (+), payment tech (−).
- 6 theories: Fisher, Cambridge, Keynes (3 motives), Baumol-Tobin, Tobin, Friedman.
- Velocity = number of times money changes hands per period.

- **Open Market Operations**: Central bank's buying/selling of government securities to influence reserves.
- **Discount Rate**: Interest rate at which commercial banks borrow from the central bank.

### Money Multiplier Process — Full Mechanism 🔴

**Five-Step Mechanism**
1. **Initial Injection of Base Money**
   - Central bank injects base money via open market operations, **discount window lending**, or other tools.
2. **Reserve Requirement**
   - Banks must hold a percentage of their deposits as reserves (vault cash or central-bank deposits).
   - This **constrains** the amount of money banks can create through lending.
3. **Deposit Creation through Lending**
   - When banks receive additional reserves (from central-bank injections or deposit inflows), they expand lending.
   - **Loans create new deposits**, increasing the money supply **beyond the initial injection**.
4. **Multiplier Effect**
   - Newly created deposits become part of the broader money supply.
   - These deposits get **re-deposited or spent** → further rounds of lending and deposit creation.
5. **Limitations and Leakages**
   - **Excess reserve holdings** by banks dampen the chain.
   - **Currency drain** — public withdrawing cash — reduces the deposit base.
   - **Non-bank financial intermediaries** outside reserve requirements weaken the link.

**Determinants of the Money Multiplier — Quick View**
| Determinant | Effect on Multiplier |
|-------------|----------------------|
| **Reserve ratio (rr)** ↓ | Multiplier ↑ (banks can lend more) |
| **Currency drain (c_d)** ↑ | Multiplier ↓ (less deposit base) |
| **Excess reserves (er)** ↑ | Multiplier ↓ (banks hoard) |

**Implications for Monetary Policy**
- **Money Multiplier Process**: Mechanism by which initial base-money injection leads to multiple expansion of money supply via bank lending and deposit creation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Currency Drain**: Public's preference to hold currency outside banks, reducing the deposit base on which banks can create loans.
- **Discount Window Lending**: Central-bank lending facility through which commercial banks borrow at the discount rate.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing the money supply expands without limit → ✅ Correct: It's bounded by **rr, er, and c_d** through the multiplier formula.
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking only the central bank determines money supply → ✅ Correct: **Public** (via c_d) and **banks** (via er) jointly affect supply.

**Quick Recall:**
- 5 steps: **Inject → Reserve constraint → Lending creates deposits → Multiplier effect → Leakages**.
- Multiplier ↓ when rr ↑, c_d ↑, or er ↑.
- Three policy tools: rr, OMO, discount rate.

### Factors Affecting the Money Multiplier and High-Powered Money 🔴

**Factors Affecting the Money Multiplier**
| Factor | Effect | Why |
|--------|--------|-----|

| **2. Currency Drain (c_d)** | ↑ c_d → ↓ m | Cash held by public doesn't create deposits |

### Theory of Endogenous Money Supply 🔴

**Five Pillars of Endogenous Money Supply**
1. **Credit Creation by Banks**
   - When banks lend, they **simultaneously create new deposits** in borrowers' accounts.
   - These deposits function as money → ↑ overall money supply.
2. **Loanable Funds vs Money Supply**
   - **Loanable funds theory**: banks intermediate **pre-existing** deposits.
   - **Endogenous theory**: banks **create new money** when they lend.
   - Money supply is **not limited by pre-existing funds** — it's determined by **credit demand** and bank **lending capacity**.
3. **Reserves and Central Bank Influence (Indirect)**
   - Central bank still affects money supply via the monetary base.
   - But influence is **indirect**: through interest rates and OMO that affect borrowing cost and banks' willingness to lend.
4. **Creditworthiness and Economic Conditions**
   - Bank credit-creation capacity depends on **borrower creditworthiness** and prevailing economic conditions.
   - **Boom**: banks willing to lend → ↑ Ms.
   - **Downturn / crisis**: banks cautious → contraction in credit and Ms.
5. **Liquidity Preference and Monetary Policy Transmission**
   - Liquidity preference and interest-rate expectations shape how policy reaches the economy.
   - Changes in central-bank policy rates → cost of borrowing → demand for credit → Ms.

**Exogenous vs Endogenous Money Supply Curves (Figure 6.1)**
| Aspect | Exogenous | Endogenous |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Shape | **Vertical** (interest rate has no effect on Ms) | **Upward sloping** (positive i → Ms relation) |
| Determinants of position | MB, rr, er, c_d (all exogenous) | MB, rr exogenous; **er and c_d depend on i** |
| Shift drivers | ↓ MB, ↑ er, ↑ c_d, ↑ rr → leftward shift | ↑ MB, ↑ rr → rightward shift |
| Underlying logic | Money supply set by central bank only | Money supply responds to credit demand and bank decisions |

**Why the Endogenous Curve Slopes Up**
- ↑ interest rate → commercial banks make more loans → ↑ money supply (positive relation).
- ↑ interest rate → customers prefer deposits over cash → c_d ↓ → more loanable funds → ↑ Ms.
- Therefore er and c_d **respond to** interest rates → upward-sloping Ms curve.

**Money Supply Equation (Reminder from Chunk 004)**

**Three Implications**
1. **Monetary Policy Effectiveness**: Depends on responsiveness of banks/borrowers to policy changes — not just on monetary base.
2. **Financial Stability Considerations**: Bank capital adequacy and systemic risk management are essential for the endogenous mechanism to work smoothly.
3. **Role of Central Banks**: Still significant, but **understanding bank/borrower behaviour is essential**.
- **Endogenous Money Supply Theory**: Theory that money supply is determined by the demand for credit and the lending capacity of banks, with central-bank actions only indirectly influencing it through interest rates. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Exogenous (in money-supply context)**: Determined externally — i.e., set by the central bank rather than by market forces.
- **Loanable Funds Theory**: Banks act as intermediaries lending out pre-existing deposits — contrasts with endogenous money supply theory.
- **Liquidity Preference**: Desire of individuals/businesses to hold assets in liquid form (cash, demand deposits) rather than illiquid forms (physical assets, long-term investments).

- ❌ Mistake: Drawing exogenous Ms curve as upward-sloping → ✅ Correct: Exogenous = **vertical**; endogenous = **upward-sloping**.
