## Section: Nature and Functions of Money 🔴
<!-- Reason: foundational, has Check Your Progress question, exam objective "Define money" -->

### Core Idea
Money is anything generally acceptable as a means of payment for transactions, including the settlement of debt. Its acceptability rests on collective belief, not on intrinsic worth. By drastically lowering transaction costs it enables specialization, division of labour, and rapid economic growth — outcomes impossible in a barter economy.

> **In Simple Terms:** Money is whatever everyone trusts to take as payment. We don't want it for itself (you can't eat a ₹500 note), but it lets us turn what we have into what we want without trying to find someone who wants exactly what we have.

### Key Concepts

#### Defining Money
Anything that functions as a (i) means of payment / medium of exchange, (ii) unit of account, and (iii) store of value. Today, money also acts as **legal tender**, which enhances public trust in it.

#### Why Money Exists — The Barter Problem
In a barter economy, exchange requires a "**double coincidence of wants**" — you must find someone who has what you want AND wants what you have. The time spent searching for trading partners is the **transaction cost**, and this cost is high in barter. Money solves this by being universally accepted, so any seller will take it. This frees people to specialize in what they do best instead of being self-sufficient like in primitive/tribal societies.

#### Five Criteria for an Effective Money Commodity
A commodity must satisfy all five to function as money:
1. Easily standardized — value is simple to ascertain.
2. Widely accepted.
3. Divisible — to make change easy.
4. Easy to carry.
5. Does not deteriorate quickly.

#### Near Money
**Highly liquid assets that are not cash but can easily be converted into cash.** Common examples:
- Money market funds
- Bank and Post Office deposits
- Government treasury securities
- Bonds near their redemption date
- Foreign currencies (USD, EUR, GBP, JPY)
- Equities/shares of the corporate sector

> What makes money distinct from near-monies is its function as a **medium of exchange** — stocks, bonds, and houses do not perform this role.

#### The Four Functions of Money

| Function | What it means | Why it matters |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Medium of Exchange** | Used to pay for goods/services in any transaction | Eliminates the double-coincidence-of-wants problem; enables specialization and division of labour |
| **Unit of Account** | Common yardstick for measuring value (like pounds for weight, miles for distance) | Reduces the number of relative prices needed — N goods need only N prices in money, not N(N−1)/2 barter ratios |
| **Store of Value** | Holds purchasing power over time so wealth can be carried into the future | Ensures wealth is liquid, divisible, and broadly accepted; effectiveness reduced by inflation |
| **Standard of Deferred Payment** | Allows transactions and their settlement to be separated in time | Source of credit and debt in the economy |

> The medium-of-exchange function is what **distinguishes money from other assets** like stocks, bonds, and houses — those serve as stores of value but cannot be directly used in transactions.

##### Unit of Account — why it matters with many goods
For 3 goods (X, Y, Z) we need 3 relative prices. As N grows, the number of barter prices explodes [N(N−1)/2]. Quoting all prices in money collapses this to just N prices — a huge transaction-cost saving as economies grow more complex.

##### Store of Value — limitations
Effectiveness fluctuates with **inflation**, economic stability, and the availability of alternative investments. High inflation erodes purchasing power, pushing people to alternative stores of value (real estate, precious metals, stocks, bonds). Yet money remains the most convenient store because of its **liquidity**, divisibility, and broad acceptance.

### Definitions
- **Money**: Anything that functions as a means of payment (medium of exchange), unit of account, and store of value. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Barter**: A system where goods and services are exchanged for other goods and services. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Near Money**: Highly liquid non-cash assets that are easily convertible into cash. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Double Coincidence of Wants**: The barter requirement that two traders must each want what the other has.
- **Transaction Cost**: Time/effort spent exchanging goods or services.
- **Legal Tender**: Money officially recognized for the settlement of debts.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking money is wanted for its own sake → ✅ Correct: Money is wanted only because it has purchasing power and is universally acceptable.
- ❌ Mistake: Equating money with all financial assets → ✅ Correct: Stocks/bonds are stores of value but lack the medium-of-exchange function — only money has all four functions.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Money = anything universally acceptable in exchange/debt settlement.
> - 4 functions: **M**edium of exchange, **U**nit of account, **S**tore of value, **S**tandard of deferred payment ("MUSS").
> - Near money = liquid non-cash assets (T-bills, money market funds, foreign currency).
> - 5 criteria: standardizable, accepted, divisible, portable, durable.

## Section: Measures of Money Supply (M0–M4) 🔴
<!-- Reason: named monetary aggregates, syllabus objective "Explain measures of money supply", direct exam content -->

### Core Idea
The **money supply** is the total stock of money in an economy at a point in time. Because not all financial assets are equally liquid, economists use multiple aggregates — narrow vs. broad money. **Narrow money** = claims usable instantly without restriction (highly liquid). **Broad money** = adds claims that aren't instantly liquid (time deposits may need notice, MMMFs may set minimum cheque sizes).

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine your wallet, savings account, fixed deposit, and post-office certificate as concentric rings. M0 is the innermost (cash + central-bank reserves); M4 is the outermost (everything moderately liquid).

### Key Concepts

#### Monetary Aggregates — From Narrowest to Broadest

| Aggregate | Composition | Liquidity | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| **M0** (monetary base / base money / **high-powered money** / central bank money) | Currency (coins + notes) in circulation **+** demand deposits of commercial banks at the central bank | Most liquid claims on central bank | Directly controlled by the central bank via **open-market operations** (buying/selling short-term government bonds) |
| **M1** | Currency in circulation **+** demand deposits held by the **non-bank** public in commercial banks | Highly liquid | **Excludes** currency held by commercial banks and demand deposits held by commercial banks at the central bank. Includes most of M0 and is generally larger than M0. |
| **M2** | M1 **+** savings deposits with unrestricted access (demand deposits) | Near-liquid | Not instantly liquid, but close. |
| **M3** | M1 **+** time deposits | Broader | Captures broader liquidity. |
| **M4** | M3 **+** other forms of broad money (longer-term deposits, post office deposits, etc.) | Broadest | Includes less liquid financial assets. |

#### Narrow vs Broad Money

| Aspect | Narrow Money | Broad Money |
|--------|--------------|-------------|
| Liquidity | Instantly usable, no restriction | Includes claims requiring notice/conversion |
| Examples | Currency, demand deposits | Time deposits, money-market mutual funds |
| Measures | M0, M1 | M2, M3, M4 |

### Definitions
- **Money Supply**: Total amount of money available in an economy at a particular point in time (a **stock**, not a flow). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Monetary Base / High-Powered Money / M0**: Fully liquid claims on the central bank held by the private sector — currency in circulation plus demand deposits of commercial banks at the central bank. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Open-Market Operations**: Central bank trading (usually short-term) government bonds with the private sector to change the monetary base.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating M0 as the largest aggregate → ✅ Correct: M0 is the narrowest; M1 ⊂ M2 ⊂ M3 ⊂ M4 in scope.
- ❌ Mistake: Including currency held by commercial banks in M1 → ✅ Correct: M1 excludes currency held by commercial banks and excludes their demand deposits at the central bank.
- ❌ Mistake: Calling money supply a flow → ✅ Correct: Money supply is a **stock** measured at a point in time.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **M0 ⊂ M1 ⊂ M2 ⊂ M3 ⊂ M4** (broadens with each step).
> - M0 = currency in circulation + bank deposits at central bank (high-powered money).
> - M1 = currency + demand deposits of non-bank public.
> - M2 = M1 + savings deposits.
> - M3 = M1 + time deposits.
> - M4 = M3 + other broad money (e.g., post office deposits).
> - M0 is directly controllable by the central bank via OMO; broader aggregates are not.

## Section: Money and the Payments System — Evolution 🟡
<!-- Reason: historical/contextual narrative; supports CYP question on evolution -->

### Core Idea
The form of money has evolved through five broad phases — barter → metal coins → paper currency → digital payments → cryptocurrency/CBDC. Each step reduced transaction costs and extended the geographic and temporal reach of trade.

> **In Simple Terms:** Money has been on a long journey from "I'll trade you 3 chickens for a sack of rice" to "I just sent you ₹500 by scanning a QR code" — every step was about making payment faster and more trusted.

### Key Concepts

#### Evolutionary Stages of Money
1. **Barter** → goods directly exchanged; limited by double coincidence of wants.
2. **Metal coins** (bronze, copper → silver, gold) → payments by **count** rather than by **weight**; simplified transactions.
3. **Financial instruments** (claims on goods, **bills of exchange**) → introduced delayed payment and trust-based exchange.
4. **Paper currency** → lighter, easier to transport than metal; enabled long-distance trade and modern banking.
5. **Digital payments / cryptocurrency** → near-instant person-to-person retail payments.

#### Cryptocurrency
Digital or virtual currencies that use **cryptography** for security and operate on **decentralized networks** based on **blockchain technology** (e.g., Bitcoin, Ethereum). Criticisms:
- Extreme price volatility
- Regulatory uncertainty
- Security risks
- Scalability issues
- High energy consumption
- Limited acceptance
- Technical complexity
- Legal/tax implications
- Market manipulation
- Lack of consumer protection

### Definitions
- **Cryptocurrency**: Digital/virtual currencies that use cryptography for security and operate on decentralized blockchain networks. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Bills of Exchange**: Financial instruments providing a promise of payment or transfer of goods at a later date.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Sequence: Barter → Metal coins → Paper money → Digital payments → Crypto/CBDC.
> - Coins enabled payment by count, not weight.
> - Paper currency lowered transport cost — foundation of modern banking.

## Section: Indian Payment System — RTGS, NEFT, IMPS, UPI, BBPS, NETC 🔴
<!-- Reason: India-specific case study with named systems; CYP topic; high-yield exam content -->

### Core Idea
India has built a robust 24×7×365 electronic payment ecosystem governed by a separate **Payment and Settlement Systems** law. Modern systems support economic development, financial stability, and financial inclusion. The **RBI** has driven this innovation since the mid-1980s. Over **26 crore digital transactions are processed daily**, with **UPI alone accounting for more than two-thirds**.

> **In Simple Terms:** India's payment system is the plumbing that lets a chai-stall in Bihar accept a UPI payment from a customer in Mumbai instantly — and it now moves more digital transactions per day than most other countries combined.

### Key Concepts

#### Major Indian Payment Systems

| System | Full Form | Function |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **RTGS** | Real Time Gross Settlement | Real-time, gross-basis transfer of money/securities bank-to-bank to avoid settlement risk |
| **NEFT** | National Electronic Funds Transfer | One-to-one electronic fund transfers anywhere in the country (beneficiary needs a bank account) |
| **IMPS** | Immediate Payment Service | Instant payment settlement |
| **UPI** | Unified Payments Interface | Real-time, free, mobile-based bank-to-bank transfer |
| **BBPS** | Bharat Bill Payment System | Mobile-based bill payments |
| **NETC** | National Electronic Toll Collection | Electronic toll payments |

#### Drivers of Adoption
- 24×7 availability
- Affordability and accessibility
- Convenience, safety, security
- Involvement of **non-bank FinTech firms** as PPI issuers, **BBPOUs**, and third-party UPI app providers
- International recognition

### Definitions
- **RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement)**: Specialist funds transfer system where transfer of money/securities takes place bank-to-bank on a real-time, gross basis to avoid settlement risk. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **NEFT**: Electronic payment system for one-to-one direct payments anywhere in India (beneficiary requires a bank account). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **UPI (Unified Payments Interface)**: Real-time payment system in India enabling free, instant bank-to-bank transfers via mobile device. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **PPI**: Prepaid Payment Instrument (issued by non-bank FinTech firms).
- **BBPOU**: Bharat Bill Payment Operating Unit.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Real-time, gross, bank-to-bank → **RTGS**.
> - One-to-one electronic transfer, country-wide → **NEFT**.
> - Instant settlement → **IMPS**.
> - Free, mobile-based, real-time → **UPI**.
> - Bills → **BBPS**; Tolls → **NETC**.
> - >26 crore digital transactions/day in India; UPI ≈ 2/3 of these.

## Section: Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — Digital Rupee (e₹) 🔴
<!-- Reason: CYP question target, current policy topic, high-yield -->

### Core Idea
**CBDC** is the legal tender issued by a central bank in digital form — akin to sovereign paper currency but in a different form. It is exchangeable at par with existing currency, accepted as a medium of payment, legal tender, and a safe store of value. The **RBI is rolling out the Digital Rupee (e₹)** through a phased pilot strategy. Unlike volatile cryptocurrencies, CBDCs are sovereign-backed and appear as a **liability on the central bank's balance sheet**.

> **In Simple Terms:** A CBDC is the digital twin of a ₹100 note — same value, same trust, same legal-tender status, just stored digitally instead of in your wallet.

### Key Concepts

#### Why CBDCs?
Cryptocurrencies are volatile, risky, and unregulated. Central banks worldwide explore CBDCs to:
- Maintain continuum with payment innovations.
- Provide a safe digital alternative to private crypto.
- Promote financial inclusion.
- Make monetary/payment systems more efficient.

#### BIS Foundational Principles (must hold for CBDC issuance)
- Issuance must **not compromise monetary or financial stability**.
- A CBDC must **coexist with and complement** existing forms of money.
- It should **promote innovation and efficiency**.

#### CBDC vs Cryptocurrency

| Aspect | CBDC | Cryptocurrency |
|--------|------|----------------|
| Issuer | Central bank | Decentralized network |
| Legal tender | Yes | No |
| Value stability | Pegged to fiat at par | Highly volatile |
| Backing | Sovereign liability | None (algorithmic) |
| Regulation | Fully regulated | Largely unregulated |

### Definitions
- **CBDC (Central Bank Digital Currency)**: Legal tender issued by a central bank in digital form, exchangeable at par with existing currency, appearing as a liability on the central bank's balance sheet. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Digital Rupee (e₹)**: India's CBDC being rolled out by the RBI in phased pilots.
- **Digital Currency**: Any currency, money, or money-like asset primarily managed, stored, or exchanged on digital computer systems.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating CBDC and cryptocurrency as the same → ✅ Correct: CBDC is sovereign-issued legal tender; cryptocurrency is private, decentralized, and not legal tender.
- ❌ Mistake: Saying CBDC replaces existing currency → ✅ Correct: BIS principle — CBDC must coexist and complement existing forms of money.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - CBDC = central bank's digital legal tender; appears as **central bank liability**.
> - Digital Rupee = e₹.
> - BIS foundational principles: stability + coexistence + innovation/efficiency.
> - CBDC ≠ crypto: sovereign vs private; stable vs volatile; legal tender vs not.

## Section: Money, Credit and the Macroeconomy — Introduction 🟡

### Core Idea
Understanding the dynamics between money, credit, and the macroeconomy is essential for tracing how monetary policy reaches aggregate demand, output, and prices. **Money demand** — the desire to hold cash/liquid assets vs. less liquid investments — is influenced by income levels, interest rates, and economic uncertainty. A rise in money demand, if not met by an increase in money supply, lowers aggregate demand.

> **In Simple Terms:** When people suddenly want to hoard cash (rising money demand), the rest of the economy slows down — unless the central bank prints/creates more money in time.

### Key Concepts
- **Money demand drivers**: income (↑ income → ↑ transaction demand), interest rates (↑ rates → ↓ demand because holding money becomes costly), economic uncertainty.
- **Aggregate demand link**: ↑ money demand without matching supply response → ↓ aggregate demand → potential slowdown/recession.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Higher income → higher money demand.
> - Higher interest rate → lower money demand (opportunity cost rises).
> - Money demand ↑ unmatched by supply ↑ → aggregate demand ↓.

## Section: Money, Credit and the Macroeconomy 🔴
<!-- See chunk 001 for start of this section -->
<!-- Reason: Check Your Progress 3 question, links money to monetary policy transmission -->

### Core Idea
A rise in money demand, unless matched promptly by a rise in money supply, raises interest rates and dampens investment and consumer spending — reducing aggregate demand and potentially triggering recession. The central bank's tool is to **inject more money** (e.g., via **open market operations**) so that lower interest rates restore borrowing, spending, and aggregate demand. If this happens near full capacity, the extra demand spills into prices (inflation) instead of output.

> **In Simple Terms:** When people want to hold more cash, lending dries up and the economy cools. The central bank's job is to spot this fast and print/create enough new money to keep the engine running — but not so much that prices catch fire.

### Key Concepts

#### The Money Demand → Aggregate Demand Mechanism
1. ↑ Money demand → ↑ interest rates → ↓ investment + ↓ consumer spending → ↓ aggregate demand → possible recession.
2. **Central bank response**: Buy government securities → ↑ commercial-bank reserves → ↑ lending capacity → ↓ interest rates → ↑ borrowing/spending → ↑ aggregate demand.
3. **Caveat**: Near full capacity, ↑ aggregate demand → ↑ price level (inflation) rather than ↑ output.

#### Timing Matters
If the monetary authority **fails to recognise and respond promptly** to money-demand shifts, slower growth or contraction follows. Aggregate demand rises only when **money supply grows faster than money demand**, with a concomitant rise in output or price level.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Money demand ↑ unmatched by supply → AD ↓.
> - Central bank counter-tool: open market operations (buy securities → ↑ bank reserves → ↑ lending).
> - Near full capacity, expansion = inflation, not output.

## Section: The Credit Channel of Monetary Policy 🔴
<!-- Reason: named theory (Bernanke), exam-eligible CYP topic, central transmission mechanism -->

### Core Idea
Ben Bernanke's "**Credit in the Macroeconomy**" (1993) shows that monetary policy reaches the economy not only through the traditional **interest-rate channel** but also through a **credit channel** — the availability and terms of credit. The credit channel is especially powerful when credit markets tighten (e.g., 2008 financial crisis) and operates through **two sub-channels**: the **Bank Lending Channel** and the **Balance Sheet Channel**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Lower interest rates aren't the only way the RBI reaches the economy. It also works through whether banks are willing to lend and whether borrowers look healthy enough to be lent to.

### Key Concepts

#### Why a Credit Channel Exists — Asymmetric Information
Banks have **more information** about borrower creditworthiness than external investors do. This makes **bank lending a crucial source of finance** for many firms and households (Bernanke, 1993). Hence credit conditions matter independently of the headline interest rate.

#### Two Sub-Channels — Comparison

| Aspect | Bank Lending Channel | Balance Sheet Channel |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Focus | Banks' **ability** to lend | Borrowers' **financial health** |
| Mechanism | Monetary policy alters bank reserves → loan supply | Monetary policy alters asset prices → net worth → collateral → borrowing capacity |
| Expansion path | ↑ Reserves → ↑ Loans at ↓ rates → ↑ Investment & consumption → ↑ AD | ↑ Asset prices → ↑ Net worth → ↑ Collateral value → ↓ Borrowing costs → ↑ Investment & spending → ↑ AD |
| Contraction path | ↑ Rates → ↓ Reserves → tighter lending → ↓ AD | Falling asset prices → ↓ Net worth → ↓ Collateral → restricted borrowing → ↓ AD |
| Seminal paper | Bernanke & Blinder (1988) | Bernanke & Gertler (1995) |

#### 1) The Bank Lending Channel — Mechanism
**Expansionary policy** (↓ interest rate or central bank buys securities) → ↑ commercial-bank reserves → ↑ funds available for lending → ↓ cost of funds → more loans at lower rates → ↑ borrowing for investment/consumption → ↑ aggregate demand → economic growth. Contractionary policy reverses the chain.

#### 2) The Balance Sheet Channel — Mechanism
**Expansionary policy** → higher asset prices (stocks, real estate) → ↑ borrower net worth → ↑ collateral value → easier and cheaper borrowing → ↑ investment and spending → ↑ aggregate demand. Contractionary scenario: falling asset prices → ↓ net worth → ↓ collateral → restricted borrowing → ↓ investment/consumption.

#### Cyclical Variation in Effectiveness
- **Downturns**: Even with expansionary policy, banks may be **hesitant to lend** due to higher perceived risk → channel weakened.
- **Booms**: Easier credit can **exacerbate** expansions.
- **2008 crisis**: Ivashina & Scharfstein (2010) found the **impaired credit channel significantly contributed to the depth and duration** of the recession.

### Definitions
- **Credit Channel**: The transmission mechanism by which monetary policy affects the economy through the **availability and terms of credit**, alongside the interest-rate channel. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Bank Lending Channel**: Sub-channel through which monetary policy affects banks' ability to lend (via reserves and cost of funds). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Balance Sheet Channel**: Sub-channel through which monetary policy affects borrowers' financial position (via asset prices, net worth, collateral). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Asymmetric Information**: Lenders (banks) have more information about borrower creditworthiness than external investors.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating the credit channel as the same as the interest-rate channel → ✅ Correct: It operates **alongside** it via credit availability and terms.
- ❌ Mistake: Assuming the credit channel works equally in booms and busts → ✅ Correct: In downturns, banks may not lend even when policy is expansionary — channel weakens.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Credit Channel = Bank Lending Channel + Balance Sheet Channel.
> - Bernanke (1993); Bernanke & Blinder (1988); Bernanke & Gertler (1995).
> - Bank Lending → bank's **ability** to lend; Balance Sheet → borrower's **financial health**.
> - 2008 crisis: impaired credit channel deepened recession (Ivashina & Scharfstein, 2010).

## Section: Unit 5 — Demand for Money: Introduction & Curve 🔴
<!-- Reason: unit objective; sets up theories that follow; CYP question on money demand curve -->

### Core Idea
Money demand is the quantity of cash households and firms wish to hold rather than placing in alternative assets like bonds. It plays a central role in monetary policy and financial-market behaviour. The **money demand curve slopes downward** because the interest rate is the **opportunity cost** of holding money — higher rates make holding money more expensive relative to interest-bearing assets.

> **In Simple Terms:** Holding ₹500 in your wallet earns you nothing, but if interest rates are high, you lose more by not putting it in a bank. So the higher rates climb, the less cash you want to hold.

### Key Concepts

#### Unit 5 Objectives
After this unit, you will be able to:
1. Define money demand.
2. Identify factors affecting money demand.
3. Understand the major theories of money demand.

#### Money Demand Curve
- **Vertical axis**: interest rate (i).
- **Horizontal axis**: quantity of money demanded.
- **Slope**: downward (negative) — like the demand for any good.
- **Reasoning**: Holding money loses the interest that would be earned on bonds → interest rate = opportunity cost of holding money.

#### Theories Covered (preview)
1. **Quantity Theory of Money** (Fisher).
2. **Cambridge / Neoclassical** (Marshall, Pigou, Robertson, Keynes).
3. **Liquidity Preference** (Keynes) — three motives of holding money.
4. **Baumol-Tobin** — transaction demand, opportunity cost lens.
5. **Tobin's Speculative Demand** — portfolio of cash & bonds.
6. **Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory** — money as an asset.

### Definitions
- **Money Demand**: Quantity of money (cash) which households and firms want to hold with themselves rather than in other forms like bonds. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Opportunity Cost of Holding Money**: Interest income foregone by holding cash instead of bonds. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Money demand curve slopes **downward**: ↑ i → ↓ Md.
> - Interest rate = opportunity cost of holding money.
> - 6 major theories of money demand will follow.

## Section: Factors Affecting the Demand for Money 🔴
<!-- Reason: unit objective, CYP-eligible material -->
<!-- Continues in chunk 003 -->

### Core Idea
Six main factors shape money demand: **interest rate, price level, income (GDP), risk of alternative assets, liquidity of alternative assets, and payments/receipts technology**. Some raise money demand (price level, income, risk on alternatives, low liquidity of alternatives); others lower it (high interest rates, payment-tech advancements like UPI).

> **In Simple Terms:** Whether you hold more or less cash depends on six things — how much you earn, how high prices are, how risky/illiquid the alternatives are, how high interest rates are, and how easy digital payments make life.

### Key Concepts

#### 1) Interest Rate (Negative Relation)
Money demand depends on **expected returns** of money vs. alternative assets.
- ↑ expected return on **money** → ↑ money demand.
- ↑ expected return on **alternatives** (e.g., stocks) → ↓ money demand (wealth shifts to alternatives).

**Worked example:** Investor faces 5% on both money and stocks initially. If stock returns rise to 8%, investor reallocates from money → stocks. Conversely, if money's return rises to 7% (stocks stay 5%), investor reallocates from stocks → money. **Portfolio-allocation theory** in action.

#### 2) Price Level (Positive Relation)
↑ prices → people need more currency for the same transactions → **nominal money demand** increases.
**Example:** On a small island, fruits initially cost a few island dollars. Decades later, due to inflation, the same basket costs many more dollars. Islanders must hold more cash for daily transactions — **proportional relation between price level and nominal money demand**.

#### 3) Income / GDP (Positive Relation)
Higher income → higher transaction volume → higher money demand at any given interest rate. ↑ Real GDP → ↑ Md.

#### 4) Risk of Alternative Assets (Positive Relation)
If risk on stocks/real estate ↑, investors shift to safer holdings (cash, government bonds, savings accounts) → ↑ money demand. Example: stock market crash plus uncertain real estate → shift to safe assets including cash.

#### 5) Liquidity of Alternative Assets (Negative Relation)
↑ liquidity of alternatives → ↓ need to hold money. Conversely, illiquid alternatives → ↑ money demand.

#### 6) Payments and Receipts Technology (Negative Relation)
**UPI platforms (PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm) and net banking** dramatically reduce demand for physical cash:
- Instant, secure smartphone-based bank transfers.
- Reach into remote areas with limited banking.
- Strong security features build trust.
- Cashback incentives push digital adoption.
- Wide merchant integration normalizes digital payments.
**Result**: India's payment landscape is largely digital, sharply reducing physical-currency reliance.

#### Summary Table — Factors and Their Effect on Money Demand

| Factor | Direction | Reasoning |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Interest rate | ↓ Md | Higher opportunity cost of holding money |
| Price level | ↑ Md | More cash needed for same transactions |
| Income / GDP | ↑ Md | More transactions need more money |
| Risk of alternatives | ↑ Md | Flight to safety — money is safer |
| Liquidity of alternatives | ↓ Md | Less need to hold money if alternatives are liquid |
| Payment technology | ↓ Md | UPI/digital transfer reduces need for physical cash |

### Definitions
- **Portfolio Allocation Theory**: Wealth allocation between money and alternatives is driven by **expected returns on each**.
- **Nominal Money Demand**: Quantity of money demanded measured in current-price currency units.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing real and nominal money demand for the price-level effect → ✅ Correct: A rise in price level raises **nominal** money demand proportionally; **real** demand may stay the same.
- ❌ Mistake: Assuming digital payments increase money demand → ✅ Correct: They **reduce** demand for physical cash because of better alternatives.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 6 factors: **Interest rate, Price level, Income, Risk of alts, Liquidity of alts, Payments tech**.
> - + drivers of Md: ↑P, ↑Y, ↑risk on alternatives, ↓liquidity of alternatives.
> - − drivers of Md: ↑i, ↑liquidity of alternatives, ↑payment tech.
> - UPI ↓ physical money demand in India.

## Section: Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher) 🔴
<!-- See chunk 002 for unit introduction -->
<!-- Reason: foundational named theory, explicit CYP question, derivation included -->

### Core Idea
The **Quantity Theory of Money**, attributed to David Hume and algebraically formalized by **Irving Fisher**, is fundamentally about **money supply**, not demand — but in equilibrium it can be re-arranged to derive money demand. The transactions version states **MV = PT**; the income version (more practical) replaces T with Y: **MV = PY**.

> **In Simple Terms:** It says how much money exists × how fast it changes hands = how much stuff costs × how much stuff gets sold. Once you assume the economy is in equilibrium, you can flip it around to see how much money people want.

### Key Concepts

#### Transaction Version
$$M \times V = P \times T$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **M** | Quantity of money (money supply) |
| **V** | **Transaction velocity** of money — number of times a rupee changes hands per period |
| **P** | Price per transaction |
| **T** | Number of transactions per period |

**Worked example:** M = ₹25, T = 50 transactions/year, P = ₹5 per transaction → 25V = 5 × 50 = 250 → V = 10. (₹25 must change hands 10 times to support ₹250 of transactions.)

**Practical limitation**: T is impossible to measure in real life → use the income version.

#### Income Version
$$M \times V = P \times Y$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **M** | Quantity of money |
| **V** | **Income velocity** of money (replaces transaction velocity) |
| **P** | Price level |
| **Y** | Real output |

**Logic of substitution**: T and Y are positively related and proportional, so Y is a workable proxy for T.

#### Deriving Money Demand from the Quantity Theory (Laidler, 1977)
Per Laidler, in equilibrium **money supply = money demand**, so M = Md:
$$M_d \times V = P \times T$$
With V and T constant (V̄, T̄):
$$M_d = P \times \frac{1}{\bar{V}} \times \bar{T}$$
Letting **k = 1/V̄**:
$$M_d = k \times (P \times \bar{T}) = k \times \text{volume of transactions}$$
In the classical framework, T = Y, so:
$$\boxed{M_d = k \times \text{Income}}$$

#### Criticisms of the Quantity Theory

| Critic | Critique |
|--------|----------|
| **J.M. Keynes** (*General Theory*) | Inflation is driven by **aggregate demand**, not money supply. At full employment, AD > AS creates an inflationary gap **independent of money supply changes**. |
| **Paul Krugman** (Nobel laureate) | Theory **fails in a liquidity trap** — when interest rates hit minimum, further fall is unexpected, and monetary policy is ineffective. |
| **Ludwig von Mises** (Austrian-American) | The theory does not focus on the **money demand side** — only equilibrium derivation; not applicable in disequilibrium. |
| Various economists | The quantity theory is dismissed by some as a **tautology** rather than a theory. |

### Definitions
- **Quantity Theory of Money**: MV = PT — relationship between money supply, velocity, price, and transactions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Velocity of Money**: Number of times a unit of money is exchanged in a period; transaction velocity (V_T) or income velocity (V_Y).
- **Liquidity Trap**: Situation when interest rate hits its minimum and further fall is not expected — monetary policy becomes ineffective. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **MV = PT** (transactions); **MV = PY** (income).
> - Theory is about supply, but yields Md = kY in equilibrium.
> - 4 critiques: Keynes (AD-driven inflation), Krugman (liquidity trap), Mises (no demand side), tautology charge.

### Connections
- Continues into: Cambridge Approach (next section) — the "demand-side" version.

## Section: Cambridge / Neoclassical Approach 🔴
<!-- Reason: named theory, exam-eligible, paired with Fisher in syllabus -->

### Core Idea
The **Cambridge approach** (Marshall, Pigou, Robertson, Keynes) is an improvement over Fisher's quantity theory because it **explicitly models money demand**. Cambridge economists argue that people don't hold all income as cash (cash earns no interest); only a fraction **k** is held for transactions. Hence: **M_d = k(PY)**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Cambridge said, "of every ₹100 you earn, you'll keep some fraction k as cash for transactions and put the rest in interest-bearing assets." That fraction k is the heart of money demand.

### Key Concepts

#### Cambridge Equation
$$M_d = k \times (P \times Y)$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **M_d** | Money demand |
| **k** | **Cambridge k** — fraction of income held as cash for transactions |
| **P** | Price level |
| **Y** | Real income |

**Cambridge k** is the **reciprocal of k** as defined in the quantity theory's V (where k = 1/V̄). Money supply is determined in equilibrium by setting Md = Ms.

#### Cambridge vs Fisher

| Aspect | Fisher (Quantity Theory) | Cambridge / Neoclassical |
|--------|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Primary focus | Money supply | Money demand |
| Equation | MV = PT (or PY) | Md = k(PY) |
| Role of k | k = 1/V̄ (derived) | k is a structural constant of demand |
| Money demand | Derived only in equilibrium | Modelled directly |

### Definitions
- **Cambridge k**: Fraction of nominal income (PY) people choose to hold as cash for transactions; reciprocal of velocity (1/V̄). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cambridge / Neoclassical Theory of Money Demand**: Md = k(PY). ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Md = k(PY).
> - Cambridge k = 1/V̄.
> - Cambridge models demand directly; Fisher derives it only in equilibrium.

## Section: Liquidity Preference Theory (Keynes) 🔴
<!-- Reason: cornerstone Keynesian theory, CYP question on three motives, derivation -->

### Core Idea
Keynes' **Liquidity Preference Theory** decomposes money demand into **three motives**: **Transaction (M_d^T), Precautionary (M_d^p), and Speculative (M_d^sp)**. The first two depend on **income**; the third depends on the **interest rate** and operates through bond price expectations. Total Md = αY + βY + (P×W)·f(i).

> **In Simple Terms:** Keynes said you hold cash for three reasons: (1) to pay your bills, (2) for "what if" emergencies, and (3) to wait for a better deal on bonds. Income drives the first two; the interest rate drives the third.

### Key Concepts

#### The Three Motives — Comparison

| Motive | Formula | Driver | Interest sensitivity |
|--------|---------|--------|----------------------|
| **Transaction** (M_d^T) | M_d^T = α(PY) | Income/transactions | Generally inelastic (some economists: elastic above i₃ when opportunity cost is high) |
| **Precautionary** (M_d^p) | M_d^p = β(PY) | Income/uncertainty about future | Generally inelastic (similar caveat as above) |
| **Speculative** (M_d^sp) | M_d^sp = (P×W)·f(i) | Interest rate / bond price expectations | Strongly interest-elastic |

#### 1) Transaction Motive
Need for cash to bridge the gap between **receipts** and **planned regular payments** (school fees, electricity, salaries, rent, loan repayments, etc.). Higher income → higher transaction volume → higher Md.
- Nominal: $M_d^T = \alpha(PY)$
- Real: $M_d^T / P = \alpha Y$
- α = fraction of nominal income demanded for transactions.

#### 2) Precautionary Motive
Holding cash for **unforeseen events** (e.g., death of family member, illness). People save part of income for odd circumstances.
- Nominal: $M_d^p = \beta(PY)$
- Real: $M_d^p / P = \beta Y$
- β = fraction of nominal income demanded for precaution.

#### 3) Speculative Motive — The Bond Market Logic
**Speculation** = profiting from price fluctuations (e.g., buy bond at ₹1000, price rises to ₹1200 in a year → ₹200 capital gain + interest/coupon). For a **perpetual bond**:
$$V = \frac{C}{i}$$
where V = bond value, C = coupon, i = market interest rate.

**Inverse i ↔ V relationship**: ↑ i → ↓ bond price; ↓ i → ↑ bond price.

##### Keynes' Bullish/Bearish Logic
- There is a **normal interest rate**.
- If i > normal → individual expects i to **fall** → expects ↑ bond prices → **bearish on cash** = capital gain + interest → holds **bonds**.
- If i < normal → individual expects i to **rise** → expects ↓ bond prices → **bullish on cash** = capital loss but still earns coupon → holds **bonds only if** coupon > capital loss; otherwise holds **all cash**.
- At aggregate level, individuals' weights smooth out → **aggregate speculative Md = smooth function of i**.

$$M_d^{sp} = (P \times W) \times f(i)$$
where W = real wealth.

#### Total Money Demand (Keynes)
$$M_d = \alpha Y + \beta Y + (P \times W) \times f(i)$$
or:
$$M_d = (\alpha + \beta)Y + (P \times W) \times f(i)$$
**Md is a function of Y, W, and i.**

#### Mankiw's Compact Form
- Nominal: $M_d = P \cdot L(i, Y)$
- Real: $M_d / P = L(i, Y)$

where L(·) captures the liquidity-preference function.

#### Criticisms of Liquidity Preference Theory
1. **Single purse problem**: People don't keep separate purses for each motive; in reality one common balance serves all needs.
2. **Empirical weakness**: Fails to fully explain observed fluctuations in interest rates and money demand. Other factors (inflation expectations, growth, central-bank policy) also drive these.
3. **Homogeneous expectations**: Assumes all individuals share the same interest-rate and income expectations; in reality expectations are heterogeneous.

### Definitions
- **Transaction Motive**: Demand for money to bridge gap between receipts and regular planned payments. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Precautionary Motive**: Demand for money as a buffer for unforeseen future expenses. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Speculative Motive**: Demand for money tied to expectations about future interest rates / bond prices. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Capital Gain**: Profit due to asset price appreciation (e.g., bond price rise).
- **Bullish Expectation**: Expectation of price increase / interest rate fall.
- **Bearish Expectation**: Expectation of price decrease / interest rate rise.
- **Perpetual Bond**: A bond with infinite maturity; V = C/i.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Saying transaction & precautionary demand depend on i → ✅ Correct: Both are interest-**inelastic** in Keynes (some say elastic above a threshold).
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing bullish/bearish with rate movements directly → ✅ Correct: Bullish on **bonds** = expects bond **prices** to rise = expects **interest rates to fall**.
- ❌ Mistake: Applying V = C/i to all bonds → ✅ Correct: This formula is for **perpetual** bonds (infinite maturity).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Md = M_d^T + M_d^p + M_d^sp.
> - Transaction & Precautionary = f(Y); Speculative = f(i).
> - Md = αY + βY + (P×W)·f(i).
> - Bond value (perpetual): V = C/i — inverse of interest rate.
> - 3 criticisms: single purse, empirical weakness, homogeneous expectations.

### Connections
- Builds on: Cambridge Approach (transaction k → α here).
- Continues into: Baumol-Tobin (transaction motive made rigorous) and Tobin's speculative model (speculative motive made rigorous).

## Section: Baumol-Tobin Model of Transaction Demand 🔴
<!-- Reason: named model, derivation, worked numerical, exam-favourite -->

### Core Idea
Independently developed by **William J. Baumol (1952)** and **James Tobin (1956)**, this model treats cash management like inventory management — analogous to **Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)**. The optimal cash holding minimizes **total cost = transaction cost + opportunity cost of holding money** (forgone interest). The result: optimum number of bank visits Q* = √(iY/2b); average cash holding A = √(bY/2i).

> **In Simple Terms:** Like deciding how often to refill your bike's petrol tank, Baumol-Tobin tells you how often to visit the bank — frequent small withdrawals save interest but cost time/transaction fees, infrequent big withdrawals lose interest. There's a sweet spot.

### Key Concepts

#### Setup
- Annual spending: ₹Y, withdrawn from a bank account paying interest **i**.
- Withdrawals are made in equal lumps; cash is spent at a **constant rate**.
- Each bank visit incurs **transaction cost b** (e.g., travel cost).

#### Average Cash Holding for Q Visits
- 1 visit/year: opens with Y, ends with 0 → average = **Y/2**.
- 2 visits/year: half-year cycles → average = **Y/4**.
- Q visits/year: average = **Y/(2Q)**.

#### Costs
- **Transaction cost** of Q visits: $bQ$.
- **Opportunity cost** (interest forgone on average holding): $\frac{iY}{2Q}$.
- **Total cost**:
$$TC = \frac{iY}{2Q} + bQ$$

#### First-Order Condition (Cost Minimization)
$$\frac{d(TC)}{dQ} = -\frac{iY}{2Q^2} + b = 0$$
$$Q^{*2} = \frac{iY}{2b}$$
$$\boxed{Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{iY}{2b}}}$$

#### Average Money Holding (Optimum)
$$A = \frac{Y}{2Q^*} = \frac{Y}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2b}{iY}}$$
$$\boxed{A = \sqrt{\frac{bY}{2i}}}$$

#### Worked Example (from text)
**Mr. M** wants to spend ₹1,00,000 from bank balance.
- i = 5% = 0.05/year
- b = ₹4 per visit
- $Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{0.05 \times 1{,}00{,}000}{2 \times 4}} = \sqrt{\frac{5000}{8}} = \sqrt{625} = 25$ visits.
- $A = \sqrt{\frac{4 \times 1{,}00{,}000}{2 \times 0.05}} = \sqrt{\frac{4{,}00{,}000}{0.1}} = \sqrt{40{,}00{,}000} = ₹2000$.

#### Verification Table — TC Minimum at Q = 25

| Q | A = Y/2Q | Visit cost (Q×4) | Opportunity cost (A × 0.05) | Total cost |
|---|----------|-------------------|------------------------------|------------|
| 5 | 10000 | 20 | 500.00 | 520.00 |
| 10 | 5000 | 40 | 250.00 | 290.00 |
| 16 | 3125 | 64 | 156.25 | 220.25 |
| 22 | 2273 | 88 | 113.64 | 201.64 |
| **25** | **2000** | **100** | **100.00** | **200.00** ← minimum |
| 28 | 1786 | 112 | 89.29 | 201.29 |
| 34 | 1471 | 136 | 73.53 | 209.53 |
| 49 | 1020 | 196 | 51.02 | 247.02 |

At optimum (Q = 25), **visit cost = opportunity cost = ₹100** — TC curve is at its trough.

#### Comparative Statics
- ↑ i → ↑ Q* (more visits) → ↓ A (less cash held). Higher rates make holding cash costly.
- ↑ Y → ↑ Q* and ↑ A but **square-root relation** (so A grows slower than Y — economies of scale in cash management).
- ↑ b → ↓ Q* and ↑ A. Higher transaction cost ⇒ fewer visits, more cash per visit.

#### Criticisms of Baumol-Tobin
1. Assumes spending happens at **constant rate**, but real spending is uneven and uncertain.
2. **Hard to quantify** transaction costs in practice.

### Definitions
- **Baumol-Tobin Model / Cash Management Model**: Optimal cash-holding model that minimizes total transaction + opportunity cost; A = √(bY/2i). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **EOQ Analogy**: Cash-management problem mirrors inventory-management problem with purchase, order, and carrying costs.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Mixing up the Q* and A formulas → ✅ Correct: Q* = √(iY/2b), A = √(bY/2i). At optimum, **visit cost = opportunity cost**.
- ❌ Mistake: Saying Md grows linearly in Y → ✅ Correct: Square-root relation — economies of scale.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Q* = √(iY/2b)**, **A = √(bY/2i)**.
> - At optimum, transaction cost = opportunity cost.
> - Square-root law → Md grows slower than income.
> - 2 criticisms: constant-rate spending, hard to measure b.

### Connections
- Builds on: Keynes' transaction motive (previous section, this chunk).
- Connects to: Friedman's modern theory (later this chunk) — both treat money like an asset.

## Section: Tobin's Theory of Speculative Demand 🔴
<!-- Reason: named theory, portfolio-based, contrasts with Keynes -->

### Core Idea
Tobin (1958, *"Liquidity Preference as Behavior Towards Risk"*) reframed speculative demand as a problem of **portfolio choice under uncertainty**, not interest-rate expectations. Investors hold a **mix of cash and bonds** rather than 100% one or the other (as Keynes assumed). Optimization happens where an indifference curve over (risk, return) is tangent to the linear opportunity locus R = βδ.

> **In Simple Terms:** Tobin disagreed with Keynes' "all-cash or all-bonds" view. He said most people split their portfolio — some cash, some bonds — to balance risk and return like a normal investor.

### Key Concepts

#### Mathematical Setup
- V = value of bonds; r = rate of return; θ = rate of risk.
- **Total return**: $R = rV$
- **Total risk**: $\delta = \theta V$
- Combining: $R = \frac{r}{\theta} \delta$
- Let $\beta = r/\theta$: $R = \beta \delta$ → **linear positive relation between R and δ** (the line **OZ**).

#### Geometric Construction (Figure 5.3 in text)
- **Panel (a)**: Risk (horizontal), Return (vertical).
- IC₁, IC₂ = indifference curves over (risk, return) — higher utility on IC₂.
- Line **OZ** = opportunity set; investor cannot move off it.
- **Optimum at point E** where OZ is tangent to highest reachable IC.
- **Panel (b)**: Wealth axis (vertical) shows the bonds–cash split.
  - At E, bond holding = OD; cash = WD (where W = total wealth).
  - Line **OM** maps each portfolio combination back to a (risk, return) point on OZ.

#### Effect of an Interest-Rate Change
If interest rate **falls** → OZ rotates **clockwise** to OZ' → new tangency at point F (lower IC₁) → bond holding falls to OS, cash rises to WS.

**Result**: ↓ interest rate → ↓ bond holding (OS < OD) → ↑ cash holding → confirms negative relation between interest rate and money demand.

#### Tobin vs Keynes — Key Difference

| Aspect | Keynes (Liquidity Preference) | Tobin (Portfolio Theory) |
|--------|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Portfolio choice | All cash OR all bonds (corner solution) | Mix of cash AND bonds (interior solution) |
| Driver | Expectation about future interest rates | Risk-return optimization (uncertainty) |
| Result | Discontinuous demand at individual level | Smooth demand at individual level |

### Definitions
- **Tobin's Theory of Speculative Demand**: Portfolio-choice model where investors hold a mix of cash and bonds; speculative Md is driven by uncertainty (risk preferences), not expectations alone. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Indifference Curve (over risk-return)**: Combinations of risk and return that yield equal utility; higher curve = higher utility.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - R = βδ, β = r/θ.
> - Optimum where OZ is tangent to highest IC.
> - ↓ i → OZ rotates clockwise → ↓ bonds, ↑ cash.
> - **Tobin: cash + bonds (mix). Keynes: all cash OR all bonds.**

### Connections
- Builds on: Keynes' speculative motive — Tobin generalizes it to portfolio theory.
- Connects to: Modern portfolio theory and the term "liquidity preference as behaviour towards risk".

## Section: Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory of Money 🔴
<!-- Reason: named theory, exam target, restatement of QTM -->

### Core Idea
**Milton Friedman** (1976 Nobel laureate) developed a "**modern quantity theory**" — a restatement of the classical theory in **money-demand terms**. He treats money like any other asset and models money demand as a function of permanent income, non-human wealth, returns on multiple alternatives, expected inflation, and tastes.

> **In Simple Terms:** Friedman said: don't treat money as something special — treat it like a stock, a bond, a piece of land. People will hold more of it when its return rises and less when other assets' returns rise.

### Key Concepts

#### Friedman's 1970 Money Demand Function
$$M_d = f(Y_p,\ W,\ i_m,\ i_b,\ i_e,\ \pi^e,\ u) \times P$$

#### Variables Explained

| Symbol | Meaning | Sign of relation with Md |
|--------|---------|---------------------------|
| **P** | Price level | + (↑ P → ↓ real balance → people demand more nominal money) |
| **Y_p** | **Permanent income** — weighted average of past & present incomes; proxy for human wealth (labour earnings) | + (↑ Y_p → ↑ Md) |
| **W** | Non-human wealth (land, machines) | + (more wealth → more money held) |
| **i_m** | Return on money itself | + (↑ i_m → people willing to hold more money) |
| **i_b** | Return on bonds | − (↑ i_b → capital loss / better alternative → ↓ Md) |
| **i_e** | Return on equities | − (↑ i_e → ↑ dividends attract investors → ↓ Md) |
| **π^e** | Expected inflation rate | − (↑ π^e → ↓ purchasing power → spend/invest now → ↓ Md) |
| **u** | All other factors (tastes & preferences) | Either direction |

#### Expected Inflation Formula
$$\pi^e = \frac{1}{P} \cdot \frac{dP^e}{dt}$$

#### Why "Modern" / "Restatement"
- Classical QTM: about money supply.
- Friedman: same equation reframed as **demand for an asset**.
- **Money is just one asset in a wealth portfolio** — same logic as demanding bonds, stocks, land.

#### Friedman vs Keynes

| Aspect | Friedman | Keynes |
|--------|----------|--------|
| View of money | Asset like any other | Special — three motives (T, P, sp) |
| Wealth concept | Permanent income (Y_p) + non-human wealth (W) | Current income (Y) + real wealth (W) |
| Determinants | Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u | i and Y |
| Stability of velocity | Stable (predictable Md) | Unstable (depends on speculative motive) |

### Definitions
- **Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory**: Md = f(Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u) × P — money demand as demand for an asset. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Permanent Income (Y_p)**: Weighted average of past and present incomes; serves as proxy for human wealth in money-demand functions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Non-human Wealth (W)**: Tangible/financial wealth excluding human capital (e.g., land, machines, financial assets).
- **Expected Inflation (π^e)**: Anticipated rate of change of price level.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating Friedman's theory as identical to Fisher's → ✅ Correct: Friedman **restates** it as demand-for-asset; Fisher's is supply-side mechanical.
- ❌ Mistake: Using current income Y in Friedman's function → ✅ Correct: Use **permanent income Y_p**.
- ❌ Mistake: Saying ↑ i_b → ↑ Md → ✅ Correct: ↑ i_b → ↓ Md (bond is more attractive than money).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Md = f(Y_p, W, i_m, i_b, i_e, π^e, u) × P.
> - Permanent income Y_p replaces current Y.
> - +Md drivers: P, Y_p, W, i_m. −Md drivers: i_b, i_e, π^e.
> - Treats money as an asset → "modern quantity theory".

### Connections
- Builds on: Quantity Theory (Fisher) and Cambridge approach.
- Compares with: Keynes' Liquidity Preference Theory.

## Section: Unit 5 — Summary & Key Words 🟢
<!-- Reason: recap content; not new theory but consolidation -->

### Core Idea
Money demand is the desire to hold cash and liquid assets for transaction, precautionary, and speculative purposes. It is shaped by interest rate, income, price level, risk and liquidity of alternatives, and payment technology. Six theories — Fisher's Quantity Theory, Cambridge, Keynes' Liquidity Preference, Baumol-Tobin, Tobin's Speculative Demand, and Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory — each model these forces differently.

### Key Concepts

#### Recap — Factors Affecting Money Demand
- **Interest rate**: ↑ i → ↓ Md (opportunity cost of holding money).
- **Income / GDP**: ↑ Y → ↑ Md (more transactions).
- **Price level**: ↑ P → ↑ nominal Md.
- **Risk on alternatives**: ↑ risk → ↑ Md (flight to safety).
- **Payment technology** (Paytm, UPI, etc.): ↑ digital ease → ↓ physical-cash demand.

#### Recap — Six Theories at a Glance

| Theory | Core equation/idea | Key contribution |
|--------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Fisher's Quantity Theory** | MV = PT | Direct link between money and prices; theory of supply with derivable demand |
| **Cambridge** | Md = k(PY) | Models money demand directly as fraction of nominal income |
| **Keynes' Liquidity Preference** | Md = f(Y, i) via three motives | Speculative motive introduces interest-rate elasticity |

## Section: High-Powered Money (H) and the Money Supply Equation 🔴
<!-- Reason: core derivation, named formula, exam-eligible CYP -->

### Core Idea
**High-powered money H** (= **monetary base**) is the total currency in circulation **plus reserves held by the central bank**. Through the **money multiplier mechanism**, every rupee of H supports a multiple expansion of the broader money supply M. The relationship is M = m × H, where the multiplier m depends on the **currency-to-deposit ratio (c_d)**, **required reserve ratio (rr)**, and **excess reserves ratio (er)**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of high-powered money as fertilizer the central bank pours into the soil. The money multiplier is how many plants grow from each scoop — depending on how much banks lend (rr, er) and how much cash people prefer to hold (c_d).

### Key Concepts

#### Foundational Equations

| # | Equation | Meaning |
|---|----------|---------|
| (1) | **M = C + D** | Money supply = Currency + Demand Deposits |
| (2) | **H = C + R** | High-powered money = Currency + Reserves |
| (3) | H/MB = C + (RR + ER) | Monetary base decomposed into currency + required + excess reserves |
| (4) | c_d = C/D, so C = c_d · D | Currency-to-deposit ratio |
| (5) | rr = RR/D, so RR = rr · D | Required-reserve-to-deposit ratio |
| (6) | er = ER/D, so ER = er · D | Excess-reserves-to-deposit ratio |
| (7) | **M = m · H**, so m = M/H | Money multiplier definition |

#### Variable Glossary (Unit 6)

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| M | Money supply |
| H / MB | High-powered money / Monetary Base |
| C | Currency |
| D | Demand Deposit |
| R | Reserves (= RR + ER) |
| RR | Required Reserves |
| ER | Excess Reserves |
| c_d | Currency-to-deposit ratio (C/D) |
| rr | Required reserve ratio (RR/D) |
| er | Excess reserves ratio (ER/D) |
| m | Money multiplier |

#### Deriving the Money Multiplier
$$m = \frac{C + D}{C + (RR + ER)}$$
Dividing numerator and denominator by D:
$$m = \frac{C/D + D/D}{C/D + RR/D + ER/D} = \frac{c_d + 1}{c_d + rr + er}$$

$$\boxed{m = \frac{c_d + 1}{c_d + rr + er}}$$

Since m = M/H:
$$\frac{M}{H} = \frac{c_d + 1}{c_d + rr + er}$$

#### Special Cases of the Multiplier

| Case | Multiplier |
|------|-----------|
| If **c_d = 0** | m = 1 / (rr + er) |
| If **c_d = 0 and er = 0** | m = **1/rr** (simple deposit multiplier) |

#### Components of High-Powered Money
1. **Currency in Circulation**: All physical currency (notes + coins) circulating among individuals, businesses, and banks.
2. **Reserves Held by the Central Bank**: Deposits that commercial banks hold at the central bank — mandatory; serve as buffer against crises and liquidity shortages.
   - **Required Reserves (RR)**: Mandated fraction of deposits banks must hold (as vault cash or central-bank deposits).
   - **Excess Reserves (ER)**: Reserves above the required amount; held for precaution, withdrawal demand, or compliance.

#### Implications for Money Supply

| Tool | Action | Effect on Reserves | Effect on Money Supply |
|------|--------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| **Reserve requirements (rr)** | Lower | ↑ Excess reserves → more lending capacity | ↑ Money supply |
| Reserve requirements (rr) | Raise | ↓ Excess reserves → less lending capacity | ↓ Money supply |
| **Open Market Operations** | Buy government securities | ↑ Bank reserves | ↑ Money supply |
| Open Market Operations | Sell government securities | ↓ Bank reserves | ↓ Money supply |
| **Discount Rate** | Lower | Cheaper borrowing → more lending | ↑ Money supply |
| Discount Rate | Raise | Costlier borrowing → less lending | ↓ Money supply |

### Definitions
- **High-Powered Money (H)**: Total currency in circulation plus reserves held by the central bank; foundation for broader money supply. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Money Multiplier (m)**: Ratio of change in money supply to change in monetary base; m = (c_d + 1)/(c_d + rr + er). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Required Reserves (RR)**: Mandated fraction of deposits banks must hold. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Excess Reserves (ER)**: Reserves held by banks above the required amount.
- **Currency-to-Deposit Ratio (c_d)**: C/D — public's preference for currency vs deposits.
- **Open Market Operations**: Central bank's buying/selling of government securities to influence reserves.
- **Discount Rate**: Interest rate at which commercial banks borrow from the central bank.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating "monetary base" and "high-powered money" as different things → ✅ Correct: They are **synonyms**.
- ❌ Mistake: Forgetting to add 1 in the numerator of m → ✅ Correct: **m = (c_d + 1)/(c_d + rr + er)** — the +1 represents the "D/D" term from deposits.
- ❌ Mistake: Saying "M0 = M1" → ✅ Correct: M0 (= H) excludes most demand deposits of the public; M1 includes them.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **M = C + D**, **H = C + R**, **R = RR + ER**.
> - **m = (c_d + 1)/(c_d + rr + er)**; simple deposit multiplier (c_d = er = 0): **m = 1/rr**.
> - **M = m × H**.
> - 3 tools to control money supply: rr, OMO, discount rate.
> - H components: Currency in circulation + Reserves (Required + Excess).

## Section: The Money Multiplier Process — Mechanism Setup 🔴
<!-- Reason: full process derivation; CYP question; exam-favourite -->
<!-- Continues in chunk 005 -->

### Core Idea
The money-multiplier process explains how an initial injection of base money (high-powered money) **multiplies** through the banking system to expand the broader money supply. The text further decomposes the multiplier into a **complete deposit multiplier** and a **complete currency multiplier**.

> **In Simple Terms:** When the central bank releases ₹100 of base money into a bank, the bank lends most of it, the borrower deposits it elsewhere, that bank lends most of *that*, and so on — turning ₹100 into many times more deposits.

### Key Concepts

#### Complete Deposit Multiplier
From equation (3): H/MB = C + (RR + ER), so:
$$MB = c_d D + rr \cdot D + er \cdot D = D(c_d + rr + er)$$
$$D = \frac{MB}{c_d + rr + er}$$

Therefore, the **complete deposit multiplier** is:
$$\boxed{\frac{1}{c_d + rr + er}}$$

If **c_d = 0 and er = 0** → **simple deposit multiplier = 1/rr**.

#### Complete Currency Multiplier
From c_d = C/D and D = MB/(c_d + rr + er):
$$C = c_d \cdot D = c_d \cdot \frac{MB}{c_d + rr + er} = \left[\frac{c_d}{c_d + rr + er}\right] \cdot MB$$

Therefore, the **complete currency multiplier** is:
$$\boxed{\frac{c_d}{c_d + rr + er}}$$

#### Multiplier Definitions Summary

| Multiplier | Formula | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------|----------------|
| **Money multiplier (m)** | (c_d + 1) / (c_d + rr + er) | M / H |
| **Complete deposit multiplier** | 1 / (c_d + rr + er) | D / MB |
| **Simple deposit multiplier** | 1 / rr | D / MB when c_d = er = 0 |
| **Complete currency multiplier** | c_d / (c_d + rr + er) | C / MB |

### Definitions
- **Complete Deposit Multiplier**: D/MB = 1/(c_d + rr + er). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Simple Deposit Multiplier**: 1/rr (when public holds no currency and banks hold no excess reserves). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Complete Currency Multiplier**: C/MB = c_d / (c_d + rr + er).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Deposit multiplier = 1/(c_d + rr + er).
> - Simple deposit multiplier = 1/rr.
> - Currency multiplier = c_d / (c_d + rr + er).
> - Sum check: Deposit multiplier × (1) + Currency multiplier = (c_d + 1)/(c_d + rr + er) = m ✓.

## Section: Money Multiplier Process — Full Mechanism 🔴
<!-- See chunk 004 for start of this section -->
<!-- Reason: 5-step mechanism, CYP-eligible, exam high-yield -->

### Core Idea
The money-multiplier process unfolds in **five steps**: (1) initial injection of base money by the central bank, (2) reserve requirement constrains how much banks can lend, (3) lending creates new deposits, (4) deposits get re-deposited and re-lent, triggering the multiplier effect, and (5) leakages (excess reserves, currency drain, non-bank intermediaries) dampen the expansion.

> **In Simple Terms:** It's like a chain of dominoes — the central bank tips the first one (puts in fresh money), each bank passes most of it on as a loan, and the chain creates several rupees of money for every rupee originally injected. But some dominoes get stuck (excess reserves) or fall sideways (cash held outside banks), which shortens the chain.

### Key Concepts

#### Five-Step Mechanism

1. **Initial Injection of Base Money**
   - Central bank injects base money via open market operations, **discount window lending**, or other tools.

2. **Reserve Requirement**
   - Banks must hold a percentage of their deposits as reserves (vault cash or central-bank deposits).
   - This **constrains** the amount of money banks can create through lending.

3. **Deposit Creation through Lending**
   - When banks receive additional reserves (from central-bank injections or deposit inflows), they expand lending.
   - **Loans create new deposits**, increasing the money supply **beyond the initial injection**.

4. **Multiplier Effect**
   - Newly created deposits become part of the broader money supply.
   - These deposits get **re-deposited or spent** → further rounds of lending and deposit creation.

5. **Limitations and Leakages**
   - **Excess reserve holdings** by banks dampen the chain.
   - **Currency drain** — public withdrawing cash — reduces the deposit base.
   - **Non-bank financial intermediaries** outside reserve requirements weaken the link.

#### Determinants of the Money Multiplier — Quick View

| Determinant | Effect on Multiplier |
|-------------|----------------------|
| **Reserve ratio (rr)** ↓ | Multiplier ↑ (banks can lend more) |
| **Currency drain (c_d)** ↑ | Multiplier ↓ (less deposit base) |
| **Excess reserves (er)** ↑ | Multiplier ↓ (banks hoard) |

#### Implications for Monetary Policy
By adjusting the **monetary base** through OMO, **discount rate** changes, or **reserve requirement** adjustments, central banks influence money supply → regulate economic activity, inflation, and interest rates.

### Definitions
- **Money Multiplier Process**: Mechanism by which initial base-money injection leads to multiple expansion of money supply via bank lending and deposit creation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Currency Drain**: Public's preference to hold currency outside banks, reducing the deposit base on which banks can create loans.
- **Discount Window Lending**: Central-bank lending facility through which commercial banks borrow at the discount rate.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing the money supply expands without limit → ✅ Correct: It's bounded by **rr, er, and c_d** through the multiplier formula.
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking only the central bank determines money supply → ✅ Correct: **Public** (via c_d) and **banks** (via er) jointly affect supply.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 steps: **Inject → Reserve constraint → Lending creates deposits → Multiplier effect → Leakages**.
> - Multiplier ↓ when rr ↑, c_d ↑, or er ↑.
> - Three policy tools: rr, OMO, discount rate.

## Section: Factors Affecting the Money Multiplier and High-Powered Money 🔴
<!-- Reason: itemized factor lists; direct CYP source; exam-eligible -->

### Core Idea
The money supply is shaped by two distinct sets of factors: those that affect the **multiplier** (mostly bank and public behaviour) and those that affect **high-powered money** (mostly central-bank and government action). Both must be understood to manage monetary policy effectively.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of money supply as a recipe: H is the ingredient amount the central bank puts in, and m is how the cooks (banks) and diners (public) use that ingredient.

### Key Concepts

#### Factors Affecting the Money Multiplier

| Factor | Effect | Why |
|--------|--------|-----|
| **1. Reserve Requirements (rr)** | ↓ rr → ↑ m | Lower required reserves let banks lend more deposits |
| **2. Currency Drain (c_d)** | ↑ c_d → ↓ m | Cash held by public doesn't create deposits |
| **3. Banking Behaviour** | Cautious banks (↑ er) → ↓ m; Aggressive lenders → ↑ m | Excess reserves hoarded reduce multiplier |
| **4. Central Bank Policy** | OMO purchases ↑ reserves → can ↑ m | Reserve injections support more lending |
| **5. Deposit Leakage** | ↑ leakage → ↓ m | Excess reserves, cash withdrawals, asset purchases all leak deposits out of the system |

#### Factors Affecting High-Powered Money

| Factor | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| **1. Central Bank Operations** | Primary driver — controls H via OMO, reserve requirements, lending facilities |
| **2. Government Transactions** | Fiscal-policy decisions affecting government spending or taxation; e.g., expenditures financed by central bank ↑ H |
| **3. Foreign Exchange Operations** | Central-bank purchases/sales of foreign currency reserves change H, especially under fixed/managed exchange-rate regimes |
| **4. Financial Crises** | Unconventional tools (e.g., **quantitative easing** = large-scale asset purchases) ↑ H |
| **5. Banking Sector Dynamics** | Bank consolidation or shifts in reserve composition can affect H |

### Definitions
- **Quantitative Easing (QE)**: Unconventional monetary policy involving large-scale purchases of financial assets to expand the monetary base, especially during financial crises. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Currency Drain**: Public's preference to hold cash rather than deposits, lowering the multiplier.
- **Deposit Leakage**: Movement of deposits out of the banking system (excess reserves, currency withdrawals, off-banking purchases) that dampens multiplier effects.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 multiplier factors: rr, c_d, banking behaviour (er), central-bank policy, deposit leakage.
> - 5 H factors: CB ops, govt transactions, FX ops, financial crises (QE), banking dynamics.
> - QE = unconventional ↑ H during crises.

## Section: Theory of Endogenous Money Supply 🔴
<!-- Reason: named theory, contrast with traditional/exogenous view, CYP target -->

### Core Idea
The traditional **exogenous** view says the central bank controls money supply directly. The **endogenous money supply theory** challenges this: money supply is largely **determined by the demand for credit** from borrowers and the **lending capacity of banks**. Banks **create new money when they make loans** — they don't just intermediate pre-existing funds. The central bank's influence becomes **indirect** through interest rates and reserve operations that affect credit demand.

> **In Simple Terms:** The traditional view says the central bank pours water (money) into a tank. The endogenous view says banks have a magic tap — they create water whenever a borrower asks for a loan and meets credit standards. The central bank can only nudge the temperature of the tap.

### Key Concepts

#### Five Pillars of Endogenous Money Supply

1. **Credit Creation by Banks**
   - When banks lend, they **simultaneously create new deposits** in borrowers' accounts.
   - These deposits function as money → ↑ overall money supply.

2. **Loanable Funds vs Money Supply**
   - **Loanable funds theory**: banks intermediate **pre-existing** deposits.
   - **Endogenous theory**: banks **create new money** when they lend.
   - Money supply is **not limited by pre-existing funds** — it's determined by **credit demand** and bank **lending capacity**.

3. **Reserves and Central Bank Influence (Indirect)**
   - Central bank still affects money supply via the monetary base.
   - But influence is **indirect**: through interest rates and OMO that affect borrowing cost and banks' willingness to lend.

4. **Creditworthiness and Economic Conditions**
   - Bank credit-creation capacity depends on **borrower creditworthiness** and prevailing economic conditions.
   - **Boom**: banks willing to lend → ↑ Ms.
   - **Downturn / crisis**: banks cautious → contraction in credit and Ms.

5. **Liquidity Preference and Monetary Policy Transmission**
   - Liquidity preference and interest-rate expectations shape how policy reaches the economy.
   - Changes in central-bank policy rates → cost of borrowing → demand for credit → Ms.

#### Exogenous vs Endogenous Money Supply Curves (Figure 6.1)

| Aspect | Exogenous | Endogenous |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Shape | **Vertical** (interest rate has no effect on Ms) | **Upward sloping** (positive i → Ms relation) |
| Determinants of position | MB, rr, er, c_d (all exogenous) | MB, rr exogenous; **er and c_d depend on i** |
| Shift drivers | ↓ MB, ↑ er, ↑ c_d, ↑ rr → leftward shift | ↑ MB, ↑ rr → rightward shift |
| Underlying logic | Money supply set by central bank only | Money supply responds to credit demand and bank decisions |

#### Why the Endogenous Curve Slopes Up
- ↑ interest rate → commercial banks make more loans → ↑ money supply (positive relation).
- ↑ interest rate → customers prefer deposits over cash → c_d ↓ → more loanable funds → ↑ Ms.
- Therefore er and c_d **respond to** interest rates → upward-sloping Ms curve.

#### Money Supply Equation (Reminder from Chunk 004)
$$\text{Money supply} = \frac{c_d + 1}{c_d + rr + er} \times MB$$

In the **exogenous** view, MB and rr are central-bank set; er and c_d are interest-rate-independent.
In the **endogenous** view, **er and c_d depend on the interest rate**.

#### Three Implications

1. **Monetary Policy Effectiveness**: Depends on responsiveness of banks/borrowers to policy changes — not just on monetary base.
2. **Financial Stability Considerations**: Bank capital adequacy and systemic risk management are essential for the endogenous mechanism to work smoothly.
3. **Role of Central Banks**: Still significant, but **understanding bank/borrower behaviour is essential**.

### Definitions
- **Endogenous Money Supply Theory**: Theory that money supply is determined by the demand for credit and the lending capacity of banks, with central-bank actions only indirectly influencing it through interest rates. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Exogenous (in money-supply context)**: Determined externally — i.e., set by the central bank rather than by market forces.
- **Loanable Funds Theory**: Banks act as intermediaries lending out pre-existing deposits — contrasts with endogenous money supply theory.
- **Liquidity Preference**: Desire of individuals/businesses to hold assets in liquid form (cash, demand deposits) rather than illiquid forms (physical assets, long-term investments).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Saying endogenous theory means "central bank has no role" → ✅ Correct: It says the role is **indirect** — through rates affecting credit demand.
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing loanable-funds with endogenous theory → ✅ Correct: Loanable funds = banks **intermediate**; endogenous = banks **create** money via lending.
- ❌ Mistake: Drawing exogenous Ms curve as upward-sloping → ✅ Correct: Exogenous = **vertical**; endogenous = **upward-sloping**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Endogenous: Ms determined by credit demand + bank lending capacity.
> - Banks create deposits when they lend (not just intermediate).
> - Exogenous Ms = vertical; Endogenous Ms = upward-sloping.
> - In endogenous view: er, c_d depend on i.
> - Central bank's role becomes **indirect**.
