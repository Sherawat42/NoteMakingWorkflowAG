Instruments of Monetary Policy 
There are several direct and indirect instruments that are used for 
implementing monetary policy. 
1) Repo Rate:  The interest rate at which the Reserve Bank provides 
liquidity under the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF) to all LAF 
participants against the collateral of government and other approved 
securities. 
2) Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) Rate:  The rate at which the Reserve 
Bank accepts uncollateralised deposits, on an overnight basis, from all 
LAF participants. The SDF is also a financial stability tool in addition 
to its role in liquidity management. The SDF rate is placed at 25 basis 
points below the policy repo rate. With introduction of SDF in April 
2022, the SDF rate replaced the fixed reverse repo rate as the floor of 
the LAF corridor. 
3) Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) Rate:  The penal rate at which 
banks can borrow, on an overnight basis, from the Reserve Bank by 
dipping into their Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) portfolio up to a 
predefined limit (2 per cent). This provides a safety valve against 
unanticipated liquidity shocks to the banking system. The MSF rate is 
placed at 25 basis points above the policy repo rate. 
4) Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF):  The LAF refers to the Reserve 
Bank's operations through which it injects/absorbs liquidity into/from 
the banking system. It consists of overnight as well as term repo/reverse 
repos (fixed as well as variable rates), SDF and MSF. Apart from LAF, 
instruments of liquidity management include outright open market

5) LAF Corridor:  The LAF corridor has the marginal standing facility 
(MSF) rate as its upper bound (ceiling) and the standing deposit facility 
(SDF) rate as the lower bound (floor), with the policy repo rate in the 
middle of the corridor. 
6) Main Liquidity Management Tool:  A 14-day term repo/reverse repo 
auction operation at a variable rate conducted to coincide with the cash 
reserve ratio (CRR) maintenance cycle is the main liquidity 
management tool for managing frictional liquidity requirements. 
7) Fine Tuning Operations: The main liquidity operation is supported by 
fine-tuning operations, overnight and/or longer tenor, to tide over any 
unanticipated liquidity changes during the reserve maintenance period. 
In addition, the Reserve Bank conducts, if needed, longer-term variable 
rate repo/reverse repo auctions of more than 14 days. 
8) Reverse Repo Rate:  The interest rate at which the Reserve Bank 
absorbs liquidity from banks against the collateral of eligible 
government securities under the LAF. Following the introduction of 
SDF, the fixed rate reverse repo operations will be at the discretion of 
the RBI for purposes specified from time to time. 
9) Bank Rate:  The rate at which the Reserve Bank is ready to buy or 
rediscount bills of exchange or other commercial papers. The Bank 
Rate acts as the penal rate charged on banks for shortfalls in meeting 
their reserve requirements (cash reserve ratio and statutory liquidity 
ratio). The Bank Rate is published under Section 49 of the RBI Act, 
1934. This rate has been aligned with the MSF rate and, changes 
automatically as and when the MSF rate changes alongside policy repo 
rate changes. 
10) Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR):  The average daily balance that a bank is 
required to maintain with the Reserve Bank as a per cent of its net 
demand and time liabilities (NDTL) as on the last Friday of the second 
preceding fortnight that the Reserve Bank may notify from time to time 
in the Official Gazette. 
11) Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR):  Every bank shall maintain in India 
assets, the value of which shall not be less than such percentage of the 
total of its demand and time liabilities in India as on the last Friday of 
the second preceding fortnight, as the Reserve Bank may, by 
notification in the Official Gazette, specify from time to time and such 
assets shall be maintained as may be specified in such notification 
(typically in unencumbered government securities, cash and gold). 
12) Open Market Operations (OMOs):  These include outright 
purchase/sale of government securities by the Reserve Bank for 
injection/absorption of durable liquidity in the banking system.

9.2.2  Role of Financial Intermediaries 
As we have discussed in Unit 2 of this course, financial intermediaries serve 
as the linchpin of the financial system, facilitating the efficient allocation of 
capital between surplus and deficit units. They bridge the gap between savers 
and borrowers by pooling funds from individuals and institutions and 
deploying them into various investment avenues. By engaging in 
intermediation activities such as deposit-taking, lending, and risk 
management, these entities enhance liquidity, mitigate risk, and foster 
economic growth. 
At the heart of their function lies the transformation of financial assets and 
liabilities, converting short-term liabilities (deposits) into long-term assets 
(loans and investments). This process not only ensures the efficient utilization 
of resources but also promotes stability within the financial system. 
Moreover, financial intermediaries play a pivotal role in reducing information 
asymmetry between borrowers and lenders, thereby fostering trust and 
confidence in the financial markets.

Monetary Policy 9.2.3  Channels of Transmission 
The transmission of financial activities occurs through a myriad of channels, 
each playing a distinct role in shaping economic outcomes. These channels 
encompass various mechanisms through which changes in financial 
conditions reverberate throughout the economy, influencing consumption, 
investment, and overall economic activity. 
One such channel is the interest rate channel, wherein changes in monetary 
policy rates affect borrowing costs, investment decisions, and aggregate 
demand. Additionally, the credit channel operates through alterations in the 
availability of credit, impacting the spending behaviour of households and 
businesses. Moreover, the asset price channel operates via changes in asset 
prices, influencing wealth effects and consumer spending patterns. 
Furthermore, the exchange rate channel comes into play through fluctuations 
in currency values, affecting trade balances, inflation dynamics, and 
monetary policy effectiveness. Finally, the confidence channel operates 
through changes in investor sentiment and perceptions of risk, influencing 
asset prices and financial market stability. 
Understanding these channels of transmission is essential for policymakers 
and market participants alike, as they navigate the intricacies of the financial 
system and anticipate the impact of various shocks and policy measures on 
economic outcomes. 
In conclusion, the role of financial intermediaries and the channels of 
transmission are integral components of the financial system, shaping the 
allocation of resources, the dynamics of economic activity, and the stability 
of markets. By comprehensively exploring these concepts, this unit aims to 
provide readers with a deeper insight into the mechanisms that drive financial 
intermediation and the transmission of financial activities.

9.3 FINANCIAL INTERMEDIARIES AND 
MONETARY POLICY TRANSMISSION 
Financial intermediaries play a pivotal role in the transmission of monetary 
policy, acting as conduits through which central bank actions influence 
economic variables such as interest rates, credit availability, and investment 
decisions. This section delves into the mechanisms through which different 
types of financial intermediaries facilitate the transmission of monetary 
policy, including commercial banks, non-banking financial institutions 
(NBFIs), and capital markets.  
9.3.1  Commercial Banks 
Commercial banks are the cornerstone of the financial system, serving as 
primary intermediaries between depositors and borrowers. In the context of 
monetary policy transmission, commercial banks play a critical role in 
channelling central bank actions to the broader economy. When central banks 
adjust policy rates, such as the discount rate or the federal funds rate (the RBI 
repo and reverse repo rates in India), commercial banks respond by adjusting 
their own lending and deposit rates accordingly. These changes in interest 
rates influence the cost of borrowing for businesses and consumers, thereby 
impacting investment and consumption decisions. Moreover, changes in 
central bank policy can affect the availability of credit, as commercial banks 
adjust their lending standards and loan portfolios in response to changes in 
monetary conditions. We will discuss more about Commercial banks in Unit 15. 
9.3.2  Non-Banking Financial Institutions 
Non-banking financial institutions (NBFIs), including insurance companies, 
pension funds, and mutual funds, complement the role of commercial banks 
in the financial system. While they do not engage in traditional deposit-taking 
activities, NBFIs play a crucial role in intermediating funds between savers 
and borrowers. In the context of monetary policy transmission, NBFIs 
influence the availability and cost of credit through their participation in 
financial markets. For instance, changes in central bank policy rates can 
impact the returns on fixed-income securities held by NBFIs, influencing 
their investment decisions and portfolio allocations. Additionally, NBFIs may 
engage in lending activities or provide liquidity through money market 
instruments, thereby transmitting monetary policy impulses to the broader 
economy. Non-banking financial intermediaries will be discussed in greater 
length in Unit 16.  
9.3.3  Capital Markets 
Capital markets serve as a vital channel for the transmission of monetary 
policy, providing avenues for the exchange of long-term securities such as 
stocks and bonds. Central bank actions can influence capital market 
conditions through changes in interest rates, liquidity provision, and 
signalling effects. When central banks adjust policy rates, they affect the

9.4.1  Mechanisms of Credit Provision 
The credit channel operates through various mechanisms by which changes 
in monetary policy conditions affect the provision of credit by financial 
intermediaries. When central banks adjust policy rates,  commercial banks 
respond by recalibrating their lending rates and credit standards. Lowering 
policy rates stimulates borrowing activity by reducing the cost of funds for 
banks, which in turn lowers interest rates for borrowers. Conversely, 
tightening monetary policy leads to higher borrowing costs as banks adjust 
their lending rates upwards to maintain profitability and manage risk. 
Additionally, changes in central bank liquidity provision and regulatory 
policies influence the overall availability of credit in the economy. For 
instance, central bank interventions in money markets and open market 
operations affect bank reserves and liquidity conditions, thereby influencing 
banks’ ability to extend credit to households and businesses. Moreover, 
regulatory measures such as capital adequacy requirements and loan-to-value 
ratios impact banks’ lending behaviour and the availability of credit across 
different sectors of the economy. 
9.4.2  Impact on Borrowing Costs 
The credit channel exerts a direct impact on borrowing costs, influencing 
interest rates on loans and other forms of credit extended to households and 
businesses. When central banks lower policy rates, borrowing costs decline, 
stimulating demand for credit and encouraging investment and consumption. 
Conversely, raising policy rates increases borrowing costs, dampening credit 
demand and slowing economic activity. 
Moreover, changes in central bank policy rates influence the pricing of fixed-
income securities and debt instruments in capital markets, affecting yields on 
corporate bonds, mortgage-backed securities, and other financial assets. 
These changes in asset prices and market interest rates further influence 
borrowing costs for businesses and consumers, shaping their investment and 
financing decisions. 
9.4.3  Credit Availability and Investment 
The credit channel also influences credit availability and investment 
decisions by affecting banks’ willingness to extend loans and the overall 
supply of credit in the economy. Lowering policy rates encourages banks to 
expand their lending activities, leading to increased credit availability and 
easier access to financing for households and businesses. This, in turn, 
stimulates investment in productive activities, such as business expansion, 
capital expenditure, and housing construction. 
Conversely, tightening monetary policy reduces credit availability as banks 
become more risk-averse and tighten their lending standards. This 
constriction in credit supply can impede investment and economic growth, 
leading to a slowdown in economic activity and a decline in asset prices.
