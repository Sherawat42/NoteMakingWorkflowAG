## Section: Unit 7 Summary (Let Us Sum Up — completion) 🟡
<!-- See chunk 002 for start of this section -->

### Core Idea
The remaining bullets of Unit 7's summary anchor the *adoption* of unconventional monetary policy and India's specific transition to inflation targeting (IT), including the RBI–GoI Monetary Policy Framework Agreement of 20 February 2015 and the use of headline CPI as nominal anchor from April 2014.

### Key Concepts

#### Unconventional Monetary Policy — Recap (continued bullets)
- **Adoption of unconventional monetary policies** is the significant change post-GFC.
- **Conventional instrument**: central bank's control of the interest rate on **risk-free nominal bonds**.
- **Unconventional monetary policy = residual category**: any other type of intervention by the central bank that does **not depend** for its operation on changing the risk-free nominal interest rate now or in the future.
- Includes **balance-sheet policies** (QE, credit easing), **emergency lending programmes**, **subsidised access to credit**.
- Also includes newer **macro-prudential policies** that place additional restrictions on access to credit *above and beyond* the interest rate charged.

#### Macro-Prudential Coordination
- Macro-prudential measures need to be **coordinated with monetary and fiscal policies**.
- Vulnerability to external shocks with widespread effects on the domestic financial system brings to the fore the **usefulness of macro-prudential instruments**.
- ⚠️ **Important caveat**: macro-prudential policies should **NOT be seen as substitutes for monetary and fiscal discipline** — in particular, they should not be used to offset the harmful effects of unsustainable macroeconomic policies.

#### India's Adoption of IT
- When India adopted IT in the second half of 2016, it was supposed to be a case of *"catching up with the modern Joneses."*
- It was deemed a **cutting-edge framework** providing conceptual clarity to monetary-policy-making, plus **legal cover ensuring RBI independence** in conduct of monetary policy.
- **Monetary Policy Framework Agreement** signed RBI ↔ GoI on **20 February 2015**.
- Agreement objective: maintain **price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth**.
- Operated by RBI; aims to contain CPI inflation within **6% by January 2016** and within **4% with ±2% band** for all subsequent years.
- RBI uses **headline CPI (combined) inflation** as nominal anchor since **April 2014**.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Unconventional = **residual category** (does not change risk-free nominal rate).
> - Macro-prudential ≠ substitute for monetary/fiscal discipline.
> - India's IT adoption: legal cover + nominal anchor = **headline CPI from April 2014**.

## Section: Unit 7 Key Words 🔴

### Core Idea
Unit 7's glossary defines the most exam-critical terms — Chartalism, Conventional Tools, Helicopter Money, Institutional Background, Intermediate Targets, Macro-Prudential Policy, Quantitative Easing, Unconventional Tools — in the precise wording the source uses.

### Definitions
- **Chartalism**: a non-mainstream theory of money that emphasises the impact of government policies/activities on the value of money. States that money does not have intrinsic value but is **given value by the government**. The central idea: money's value is based on the **power of the issuing authority**, not on embodied or backing precious metal. Central banks cannot control the money supply; rather, **most government fiat money enters the economy as a result of fiscal policy**. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Conventional monetary policy tool**: during normal times, central banks use three tools — **open market operations, discount lending, and reserve requirements** — to control money supply and interest rates; these are referred to as conventional monetary policy tools. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Helicopter money**: a hypothetical, unconventional tool of monetary policy that involves **printing large sums of money and distributing it to the public** to stimulate the economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Institutional background**: as regards the conduct of monetary policy, the choice of targets, instruments, and operating procedures was circumscribed to a large extent by the nature of the financial markets and the institutional arrangements.
- **Intermediate targets**: in view of the fairly stable demand function for money, **broad money (M3)** has been treated as an intermediate target in the conduct of monetary policy in India. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Macro-prudential policy** (regime): a regime created to **damp the financial cycle**, with implications for future dynamics of variables that dominate the monetary policy debate, as well as for the transmission mechanism. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Quantitative Easing (QE)** (key-words definition): essentially an extended open market operation involving the **unsterilised swap of central bank money for privately held assets**. The key difference from a normal OMO: the duration of the swap is **intended to be long-term and of uncertain length**. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Unconventional monetary policy tool**: a non-standard monetary policy tool used by a central bank or monetary authority that falls **out of line with traditional measures** — e.g., credit easing, quantitative easing, forward guidance, and signalling. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Chartalism → money's value from issuer's authority, not metal backing; supply via fiscal policy.
> - QE = **unsterilised swap of CB money for privately held assets**, long-term, uncertain duration.
> - In India, **M3** is the intermediate target due to a stable money demand function.
> - 3 conventional tools: **OMO, discount lending, reserve requirements**.

## Section: Unit 8 Introduction — RBI as Banking-System Regulator 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 8 examines how the RBI regulates the Indian banking sector under three pillars: the **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934**, the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949**, and the **Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021**, together with its role in **combating financial terrorism** and its **prudential norms**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Unit 7 looked at *what* RBI does for monetary policy. Unit 8 looks at *how* RBI keeps Indian banks in line — through licensing, inspection, reserve rules, customer-grievance redressal, and rules against terror financing.

### Key Concepts

#### Unit 8 Objectives
After this unit you should be able to:
- Identify the role and powers of the RBI to regulate Indian banks under the **RBI Act, 1934** and the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949**.
- Understand RBI's **Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021**.
- Identify the role of the RBI in combating financial terrorism.
- Examine RBI's **prudential norms**.

#### Scope of RBI's Regulatory Authority
- Authorised to **inspect and regulate banks** in the interest of the banking system, depositors, and the public.
- Regulates **commercial banks and Local Area Banks (LABs)** under the BR Act 1949, RBI Act 1934, and related laws.
- **DCBR (Department of Co-operative Bank Regulation)** regulates **State Co-operative Banks (StCBs), District Central Co-operative Banks (DCCBs), and Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs)**.
- Main tasks: licensing, branch expansion, reserve maintenance, bank management, approving establishment of bank subsidiaries and new banking activities.
- Aim: foster a strong, diverse, competitive banking system; align regulatory standards with international best practices; build a world-class banking system in India.

### Definitions
- **Local Area Banks (LABs)**: banks regulated by RBI under the BR Act 1949 alongside commercial banks.
- **DCBR (Department of Co-operative Bank Regulation)**: RBI department regulating cooperative banks (StCBs, DCCBs, UCBs).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three pillars: **RBI Act 1934 + BR Act 1949 + Integrated Ombudsman Scheme 2021**.
> - RBI's role: licensing, inspection, branch approvals, reserve rules, prudential norms.
> - Cooperative banks → regulated via **DCBR**.

## Section: The Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 — Key Sections 🔴

### Core Idea
The **RBI Act 1934** confers RBI's foundational powers and obligations and applies to the **whole of India**. Three sections are central: **Section 22** (sole right to issue bank notes), **Section 24** (denominations + demonetisation), and **Section 42** (Cash Reserve Ratio + Statutory Liquidity Ratio).

### Key Concepts

#### Section 22 — Exclusive Power to Issue Currency Notes
- Section 22(1) grants RBI the **sole right to issue bank notes in India**.
- For a period fixed by the **Central Government on the recommendation of the Central Board**, RBI may also issue **currency notes of the Government of India** (e.g., the ₹1 note) supplied by the Central Government.

#### Section 24 — Denominations & Demonetisation
- Section 24(1): RBI can issue bank notes of **₹2, ₹5, ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹500, ₹1,000, ₹5,000, ₹10,000** or such other denominations specified by the Central Government on the recommendation of the Central Board, **not exceeding ₹10,000**.
- Section 24(2): The Central Government may, on Central Board recommendation, **direct the non-issue or discontinuance** of bank notes of specified denominations.
- **Demonetisation example (2016)**: ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes discontinued under Section 24(2).

#### Section 42 — Cash Reserves & SLR

**Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) — Section 42(1):**
- Every bank in the **Second Schedule** must maintain with RBI an **average daily balance** ≥ a specified percentage (the **CRR**) of its **Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL)** in India.
- "Average daily balance" = average of close-of-business balances on **each day of a fortnight** (Saturday to second-following Friday, both inclusive).
- **No upper or lower limit on CRR currently.** Until 2006, the lower and upper CRR limits were **3% and 20%**, respectively.
- **As of 7 May 2024, CRR = 4.5%**.
- Authority for current direction: Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR), Directions – 2021, citing **Section 42(1) of the RBI Act, 1934 and Section 18(1) of the BR Act, 1949**.

**Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) — Section 42(2):**
- RBI directs scheduled commercial banks to maintain an **additional average daily balance** beyond CRR.
- The amount is computed with reference to the **excess of total demand and time liabilities over the level on the date specified** in the notification.
- Effective **23 January 2007**, after BR Act 1949 Section 24 amendment, RBI can prescribe SLR for SCBs in specified assets.
- Value of such assets shall not be **less than such percentage not exceeding 40%** of total DTL in India as on the **last Friday of the second preceding fortnight**.
- **No lower limit on SLR; upper limit = 40%**. As of 7 May 2024, **SLR = 18%**.

### Definitions
- **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)**: percentage of a bank's NDTL that must be maintained as average daily balance with RBI; no upper/lower limit currently. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: percentage of NDTL a bank must hold in specified liquid assets (cash, gold, government-approved securities) — held by the bank itself, **not with RBI**. Upper limit 40%; no lower limit. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL)**: the base on which CRR and SLR are computed.
- **Average daily balance**: average of balances at close of business on each day of a **fortnight** (Saturday to second-following Friday).
- **Central Board**: the Central Board of Directors of the RBI (per Section 2 of the RBI Act).

### Mechanisms / Processes
**CRR vs SLR — operational difference:**
1. CRR balances are held **with the RBI**.
2. SLR assets are held **by the bank itself**, in specified liquid forms (cash, gold, govt securities).
3. Both are computed on NDTL.
4. CRR has no statutory upper/lower limit currently; SLR has no lower limit but a **40% statutory ceiling**.

### Examples
- **2016 Demonetisation**: under Section 24(2), the Central Government directed discontinuance of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: SLR balances are held with the RBI → ✅ Correct: SLR is held **by the bank itself** in specified assets; only CRR is held with RBI.
- ❌ Mistake: CRR has a 20% upper limit → ✅ Correct: 3%–20% range was applicable **only until 2006**; no statutory limits now.
- ❌ Mistake: Inflation handling uses CRR alone → ✅ Correct: under inflationary pressure, RBI may **raise SLR** to diminish credit-creation ability of banks.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- "Bank notes" vs "currency notes": RBI issues bank notes; currency notes (e.g., ₹1) of the Government of India are issued via RBI under the Central Board's recommendation.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Section 22**: sole right to issue bank notes.
> - **Section 24(1)**: denominations up to ₹10,000.
> - **Section 24(2)**: Central Government can direct demonetisation; **2016 example**.
> - **Section 42(1)**: CRR — held with RBI, no statutory limits now (was 3–20% till 2006). **CRR (7 May 2024) = 4.5%**.
> - **Section 42(2)**: SLR — held by bank itself, **upper limit 40%**, no lower limit. **SLR (7 May 2024) = 18%**.
> - SLR effective for SCBs from **23 Jan 2007**; computed on excess of DTL over date in notification.
> - Average daily balance fortnight = Saturday–Saturday (Sat to second-following Fri).

## Section: The Banking Regulation Act, 1949 — Key Sections 🔴

### Core Idea
The **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** is the comprehensive framework for supervision and regulation of commercial banking in India, complementing the Companies Act 1956. Originally the **Banking Companies Act 1949** (effective **16 March 1949**), renamed BR Act on **1 March 1966**. The **Revised BR (Amendment) Act, 2020** (effective **29 September 2020**) brought 1,482 urban + 58 multi-state cooperative banks under RBI oversight and empowered RBI to undertake bank reconstructions/mergers without moratoriums.

### Key Concepts

#### Coverage
- Comprehensive framework for supervision/regulation of commercial banking in India.
- Originally enacted as **Banking Companies Act, 1949** (effective 16 March 1949).
- Renamed **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** on 1 March 1966.
- Initially limited to banking companies; **1965 amendment (Section 56)** extended coverage to **cooperative banks**.
- **Exempt**: primary agricultural credit societies; cooperative land mortgage banks.
- Cooperative banks operating within a single state are established/managed by state governments, but RBI retains licensing and regulatory authority.
- **2020 Amendment** (effective 29 Sep 2020): brought **1,482 urban + 58 multi-state cooperative banks** under RBI; allowed RBI to undertake **bank reconstructions/mergers without moratoriums**.

#### RBI's Powers Under BR Act 1949
RBI is empowered to:
- License banks
- Oversee shareholding and voting rights
- Regulate board appointments and management
- Govern bank operations
- Prescribe audit instructions
- Manage moratoriums, mergers, liquidations
- Issue directives for public welfare and banking policy
- Levy penalties

#### Section 22(1) — Licensing of Banking Companies
- **No banking company can carry on business without a license from RBI**.
- RBI fixes such conditions of license as it thinks fit.

#### Section 22(4) — Cancelling License
RBI may cancel a license if:
1. The company **ceases to carry on banking business** in India.
2. The company **fails to comply with any condition of license** fixed by RBI.
3. The company fails to fulfil conditions referred to in **Section 24(3) and 24(3A)**.

If RBI's cancellation decision is **not appealed to the Central Government**, RBI's decision is **final**.

#### Section 23(1) — Restrictions on Opening / Transfer of Places of Business
- Without RBI approval, no banking company can **open a new place of business** in India or **change its location** (except within the same city/town/village).
- Without RBI approval, no banking company incorporated in India can establish a new branch outside India or relocate one (except within the same city/town/village).
- **Exception**: Temporary opening (up to **one month**) within the same locality where the bank already operates, for events like exhibitions, conferences, or fairs.

#### Section 24 — Maintenance of a Percentage of Assets (SLR)
- A scheduled bank, in addition to CRR under Section 42 RBI Act, and every other banking company, in addition to cash reserve under Section 18 BR Act, must maintain **assets in India not less than such percentage (max 40%) of total demand and time liabilities** as RBI may specify.
- Computed as on the **last Friday of the second preceding fortnight**.
- This is the **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** held in cash, gold, etc. — by the banks themselves, *not* with RBI.
- In inflationary pressure, RBI may **raise SLR to diminish banks' credit-creation ability**.

#### Section 26A — Depositor Education and Awareness Fund
- Added in **2013** by amendment.
- Authorises RBI to establish the **Depositor Education and Awareness Fund**.
- Any account in any bank in India **not operated for ten years**, or any **unclaimed deposit over ten years**, must be credited to the Fund within **3 months** of the 10-year period expiring.
- Purpose: promote depositors' interests + other purposes specified by RBI.
- **Depositors can still claim** their deposits or operate accounts even after the 10 years and Fund transfer; the bank pays the depositor and then claims a refund from the Fund.

#### Section 35 — Inspection
- RBI can **inspect any banking company and its books and accounts at any time**, on direction of the Central Government.
- After inspection, RBI must give the banking company a copy of its report.

### Definitions
- **Banking Regulation Act, 1949**: Indian legislation governing the functioning and operations of banks; empowers RBI to regulate the banking sector. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Section 22(1) (BR Act)**: licensing requirement — no banking business without RBI license. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Section 22(4) (BR Act)**: license cancellation grounds.
- **Section 24 (BR Act)**: SLR — assets ≤ 40% of NDTL on specified Friday.
- **Section 26A (BR Act, added 2013)**: Depositor Education and Awareness Fund — 10-year dormancy → credit to Fund.
- **Section 35 (BR Act)**: RBI inspection power.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**License lifecycle (Sections 22(1) → 22(4) → 23(1) → 35):**
1. RBI grants license under Section 22(1) with conditions it deems fit.
2. New branches/relocations need RBI approval under Section 23(1).
3. RBI inspects under Section 35; report shared with the bank.
4. RBI may cancel license under Section 22(4) for cessation, condition breach, or Section 24(3)/(3A) failures.
5. Cancellation final unless appealed to Central Government.

**Dormancy → Fund flow (Section 26A):**
Account/deposit untouched 10 years → bank credits balance to Depositor Education and Awareness Fund within 3 months → if depositor later claims, bank pays and reclaims from Fund.

### Examples
- **Banking Companies Act 1949** renamed to **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** on 1 March 1966.
- **2020 amendment**: RBI now oversees 1,482 urban + 58 multi-state cooperative banks; can do mergers without moratoriums.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: BR Act covers all banks including primary agricultural credit societies → ✅ Correct: **primary agricultural credit societies and cooperative land mortgage banks are exempt**.
- ❌ Mistake: Cancellation by RBI is always final → ✅ Correct: Final only **if not appealed to Central Government**.
- ❌ Mistake: Branch opening within same city needs RBI approval → ✅ Correct: relocations **within same city/town/village do not require approval**.
- ❌ Mistake: Dormant accounts revert to government after 10 years and depositors lose claim → ✅ Correct: credited to **Depositor Education and Awareness Fund**, but **depositors can still reclaim** anytime.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **1949 BR Act** = framework for commercial banking; **renamed 1 March 1966**.
> - **2020 amendment**: cooperative banks under RBI; mergers without moratoriums.
> - **Section 22(1)** = licensing; **22(4)** = cancellation.
> - **Section 23(1)** = branch/relocation requires RBI approval (1-month exception for fairs/conferences).
> - **Section 24** = SLR ≤ 40% of NDTL.
> - **Section 26A (2013)** = 10-year dormant accounts → Depositor Education and Awareness Fund (3-month deadline).
> - **Section 35** = RBI inspection.
