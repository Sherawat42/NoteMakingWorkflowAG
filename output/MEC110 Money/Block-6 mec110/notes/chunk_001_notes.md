## Section: Unit 18 Objectives & Introduction to Diversification 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 18 builds the foundation of modern portfolio theory: why diversification matters, how to measure portfolio risk and return, and how Markowitz's Efficient Frontier identifies the best risk-return trade-offs. The guiding principle is the proverb "don't put all your eggs in one basket" — spread investments across assets and asset classes to reduce risk and improve returns.

> **In Simple Terms:** If one stock crashes, your other holdings can cushion the blow. Diversification is like ordering a thali instead of betting your whole meal on one dish — variety protects you from one bad item ruining everything.

### Key Concepts

#### Diversification
The practice of spreading investments across various assets and asset classes to mitigate risk and optimize returns. It builds a well-balanced portfolio so that volatility in any single investment is offset by other holdings.

#### Why diversification is needed
Financial markets are inherently volatile — economic recessions, geopolitical unrest, sector shocks make some investments thrive while others decline. Diversification lets investors absorb adverse fluctuations and protect long-run wealth.

#### Portfolio
A collection of investments held by an individual or entity, comprising stocks, bonds, mutual funds, real estate, and other financial instruments. More than a "bunch of investments" — it is a strategic approach to managing wealth.

### Definitions
- **Diversification**: spreading investments across various assets and asset classes to mitigate risk and optimize returns. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Portfolio**: a collection of investments held by an individual or entity, including stocks, bonds, mutual funds, real estate, etc.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Diversification ≠ avoiding risk; it is *managing* risk by combining assets whose returns don't move together.
> - Portfolio theory answers: how to diversify, which assets to pick, how to combine, what is portfolio risk, what is expected return.

## Section: 18.2 Portfolio Management 🟡

### Core Idea
Portfolio management is the practice of overseeing and optimizing investment collections — deciding asset allocation, balancing risk and returns, and adapting to market changes. Its main goal is to build portfolios that match an investor's specific financial goals, risk tolerance, and time horizon.

> **In Simple Terms:** A portfolio manager is like a chef constantly tasting and adjusting a stew — more salt here, less heat there — based on the diner's preferences and what's available in the market today.

### Key Concepts

#### Portfolio management as ongoing process
Not a one-time decision. Markets shift, economies change, and investor situations evolve, so portfolios must be reviewed and adjusted regularly. Successful portfolio management = financial expertise + careful research + proactive decision-making.

#### Active vs Passive management (preview from §18.3)
| Aspect | Active Management | Passive Management |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------|
| Goal | Outperform the market | Match market returns |
| Returns | Potentially higher | Market-based, in diversified portfolios |
| Risk | Greater chance of missing target | Lower risk of underperformance |
| Use case | Beat benchmark | Solve cash-flow / diversification problem |

### Mechanisms / Processes — Stages of Portfolio Management
1. **Understand investor profile** — goals, constraints, preferences → create investment policy.
2. **Strategy formulation & execution** — strategies aligned with the investment policy.
3. **Asset selection** — choose asset classes and securities within each class based on risk-return characteristics.
4. **Performance monitoring** — regularly track performance against market conditions and company performance.
5. **Review & adjust** — analyse outcomes vs. predetermined targets; make adjustments for the future.

### Definitions
- **Portfolio management**: the practice of overseeing and optimizing investment collections — allocating resources, balancing risk and return, and adapting to market changes. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Benefits of Portfolio Management (memorise — list of 7)
1. **Consistent risk measurement** — risk measured the same way every time.
2. **Considering correlation & diversification** — accounts for how parts of the portfolio relate to each other, reducing overall risk.
3. **Guidance for strategic planning** — direction for the company's plans; highlights weak risk-return areas.
4. **Increased transparency in decision making** — consistent evaluation of all business units / options.
5. **Understanding value creation** — helps grasp how new opportunities create value.
6. **Identifying improvement areas** — where the company must focus to enhance risk-return position.
7. **Considering non-financial implications** — qualitative impacts, not only financial ones.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 stages: policy → strategy → selection → monitoring → review.
> - 7 benefits — note the qualitative one (#7) is often overlooked in exams.

## Section: 18.3 Relationship between Risk and Return 🔴

### Core Idea
Most investors are risk-averse — they will take risk only if adequately rewarded. Choosing an investment strategy is fundamentally a trade-off: higher expected return demands accepting higher risk. Investors differ in their *degree* of risk aversion.

> **In Simple Terms:** No one takes risk for fun. They take it because of the reward at the other end. Different people demand different rewards for the same risk — that's why some people put money in stocks and others stick to FDs.

### Key Concepts

#### Risk aversion
Most people are risk-averse but not risk-avoiding. They will accept risk if rewarded enough. People have different *degrees* of risk aversion.

#### Active vs Passive — strategy choice
Decide based on the investor's *primary goal*:
- **Cash-flow problem over time** → passive (diversified, market-based returns).
- **Outperform the market** → active (aim higher, accept higher risk of underperforming).

### Definitions
- **Total risk**: the overall variability of returns of a financial asset; combines diversifiable and non-diversifiable risks. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Total risk = Diversifiable risk + Non-diversifiable risk.
> - Risk-averse investor = demands compensation, doesn't reject risk outright.

## Section: 18.3.1 Types of Risk — Diversifiable vs Non-Diversifiable 🔴

### Core Idea
Risk decomposes into two parts: **diversifiable (unsystematic)** risk that is firm- or sector-specific and can be eliminated by diversification, and **non-diversifiable (systematic / market) risk** that affects the entire economy and cannot be diversified away.

> **In Simple Terms:** If your favourite restaurant shuts down (firm risk), you can switch to another. But if there's a citywide lockdown (market risk), no restaurant is open — switching doesn't help.

### Key Concepts — comparison table

| Aspect | Diversifiable / Unsystematic Risk | Non-Diversifiable / Systematic Risk |
|--------|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Also called | Unique risk, firm-specific risk | Market risk, non-diversifiable risk |
| Source | Firm- or industry-specific events | Macroeconomic, geopolitical, market-wide events |
| Examples | Scams, labour strikes, regulatory penalty, management change, internal issues, firm-specific news | COVID-19, monetary policy, geopolitical conflict, natural disasters |
| Components | Business risk, financial risk, management risk | Market sentiment, macroeconomic indicators |
| Can be eliminated? | Yes — through diversification | No — inherent in the market |
| Mitigation tool | Spread investments across assets/sectors | Asset allocation, hedging |
| Investor control | Reducible | Cannot control, minimise, or avoid |

### Definitions
- **Diversifiable risk (unsystematic risk / unique risk)**: firm-specific risks that affect individual stock prices rather than the entire industry or sector. Can be mitigated through diversification. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Systematic risk (non-diversifiable risk / market risk)**: risks that affect the overall market or a sector; influenced by macroeconomic indicators, market sentiment, geopolitical events. Cannot be eliminated through diversification. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Examples
- **COVID-19** — systematic risk because it impacted the *entire* stock market, no diversification within equities could have escaped it.
- **Labour strike at Company X** — diversifiable; if you also held Companies Y and Z, the impact on portfolio is muted.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Diversification eliminates all risk" → ✅ It only eliminates *unsystematic* risk; market risk remains.
- ❌ Confusing "non-diversifiable" with "uncontrollable for the firm". → ✅ It means uncontrollable *for the investor through diversification*. The investor still has tools (asset allocation, hedging).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Unsystematic = firm-level → diversifiable.
> - Systematic = market-level → non-diversifiable.
> - Total risk = systematic + unsystematic.

## Section: 18.3.2 Measuring Risks — Variance & Standard Deviation 🔴

### Core Idea
Risk attached to a stock = fluctuation (dispersion) in its rate of return. We measure this using the **probability distribution of returns**: variance captures squared deviations from the expected return; standard deviation (its square root) puts the measure back in the same units as the return itself.

### Key Concepts

#### Variance
Average of squared deviations of possible returns from the expected return, weighted by probability.

$$\sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} [R_i - E(R)]^2 \times P_i$$

Where:
- $R_i$ = the *i*-th possible return (i = 1 … n)
- $E(R)$ = expected value of the investment
- $P_i$ = probability of the *i*-th return

#### Standard deviation
Square root of variance — measure of total risk in the same units as the return.

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} [R_i - E(R)]^2 \times P_i}$$

### Definitions
- **Variance ($\sigma^2$)**: probability-weighted average of squared deviations of returns from their expected value. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Standard deviation ($\sigma$)**: square root of variance; standard measure of an asset's total risk. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Higher σ = higher risk.
> - σ has same units as return; σ² does not — always report σ when comparing risk.

## Section: 18.3.3 Expected Returns 🔴

### Core Idea
**Expected return** is the probability-weighted average of all possible returns — the average return an investor anticipates over a given time. It is the central determinant of portfolio selection because future returns are uncertain.

### Key Concepts

#### Formula
$$E(R) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} R_i P_i$$

Where:
- $E(R)$ = expected return on the investment
- $n$ = number of possible outcomes
- $R_i$ = the *i*-th possible return
- $P_i$ = probability of $R_i$

Drivers of expected return: market trends, economic forecasts, historical data.

### Definitions
- **Expected return**: anticipated profit/loss from an investment within a specific timeframe; the probability-weighted average of possible returns. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Examples — Example 18.1 (worked)

**Setup:** Mr X invests an equal amount in Security A and Security B. Boom and Depression are equally likely (P = 0.5 each).

| Economic condition | R_A (%) | R_B (%) | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | 15 | 12 | 0.5 |
| Depression | 5 | 7 | 0.5 |

**Step 1 — Expected returns:**
- $E(R_A) = 0.5 \times 15 + 0.5 \times 5 = 7.5 + 2.5 = 11.0\%$
- $E(R_B) = 0.5 \times 12 + 0.5 \times 7 = 6.0 + 3.5 = 9.5\%$

**Step 2 — Variance and SD of A:**

| Condition | $R_A$ | $R_A - E(R)$ | $(R_A - E(R))^2$ | P | $(R_A - E(R))^2 \times P$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | 15 | 4 | 16 | 0.5 | 8 |
| Depression | 5 | -6 | 36 | 0.5 | 18 |
| | | | | **Var** | **26** |

$\sigma_A = \sqrt{26} = 5.09\%$

**Step 3 — Variance and SD of B:**

| Condition | $R_B$ | $R_B - E(R)$ | $(R_B - E(R))^2$ | P | $(R_B - E(R))^2 \times P$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | 12 | 2.5 | 6.25 | 0.5 | 3.125 |
| Depression | 7 | -2.5 | 6.25 | 0.5 | 3.125 |
| | | | | **Var** | **6.25** |

$\sigma_B = \sqrt{6.25} = 2.5\%$

**Interpretation:** A has higher expected return (11% vs 9.5%) but also higher risk (5.09% vs 2.5%). The risk-return trade-off is visible in the same example.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Forgetting to multiply squared deviations by probability — gives sum of squares, not variance.
- ❌ Reporting variance as the risk measure when standard deviation is asked — variance is in % squared, σ is in %.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - $E(R) = \sum R_i P_i$.
> - $\sigma^2 = \sum [R_i - E(R)]^2 P_i$, $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$.
> - In Example 18.1: A → (E(R)=11, σ=5.09); B → (E(R)=9.5, σ=2.5). Higher reward, higher risk.
