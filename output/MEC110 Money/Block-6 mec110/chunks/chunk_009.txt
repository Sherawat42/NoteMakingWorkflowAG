Corporate Finance 22.4.4  Sources of Working Capital 
Working capital sources encompass the various means by which a company 
secures funds to meet its short-term obligations and support its day-to-day 
operations. These sources can be broadly categorised into short-term and 
long-term sources. Below are some common sources of working capital:  
Short-Term Sources of Working Capital: 
a) Trade Credit: Companies often receive credit from suppliers, allowing
them to procure goods or services and defer payment for a specified
period, typically 30 to 90 days.
b) Bank Loans:  Short-term bank loans, such as working capital loans or
overdraft facilities, offer quick access to funds to manage day-to-day
operational expenses and cash flow needs.
c) Commercial Paper:  Creditworthy corporations may issue commercial
paper, which are short-term unsecured promissory notes, to raise funds
from the capital markets.
d) Factoring and Receivables Financing:  Companies can sell their
accounts receivable to third-party factors at a discount, enabling them to
obtain immediate cash against outstanding invoices.
e) Inventory Financing: Inventory can be used as collateral to secure
short-term loans or lines of credit.
f) Trade Advances : Some businesses receive advance payments from
customers for goods or services, providing an immediate infusion of
working capital.
Long-Term Sources of Working Capital: 
a) Equity Financing:  Companies can raise long-term working capital by
issuing new shares of stock to investors through private placements or
public offerings.
b) Debt Financing: Long-term debt instruments such as corporate bonds or
bank loans with longer repayment periods are used to raise working
capital.
c) Retained Earnings:  Accumulated profits, known as retained earnings,
can be reinvested in the business to serve as a long-term source of
working capital.
d) Venture Capital and Private Equity:  Startups and high-growth firms
may secure working capital from venture capital firms or private equity
investors in exchange for ownership stakes.
e) Asset-Based Lending:  Companies can obtain long-term loans by using
assets like real estate or equipment as collateral.

22.4.5  Approaches for Determining the Financial Mix 
Determining the right financing mix, or capital structure, is a crucial decision 
for any company, as it involves selecting the ideal proportion of debt and 
equity to finance its operations. The financing mix can have a significant 
impact on a company’s risk profile, cost of capital, and overall financial 
performance. There are three common approaches to determining the 
financing mix: the hedging approach, the conservative approach, and the 
trade-off approach.  
A) Hedging Approach:
The hedging approach aims to strike a balance between debt and equity 
financing to minimise the overall cost of capital while effectively managing 
financial risk. Companies adopting this approach calculate the optimal capital 
structure by minimising the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). 
WACC is the average cost of debt and equity, considering their respective 
proportions in the capital structure.  
Companies following the hedging approach increase their debt levels as long 
as the cost of debt remains lower than the cost of equity. This strategy leads 
to a decrease in WACC, resulting in a lower overall cost of capital, which can 
potentially enhance the company’s value.  
However, the hedging approach recognises that excessive leverage can 
increase financial risk, especially if the company encounters challenges in 
meeting its debt obligations. Thus, there is a limit to how much debt a 
company should take on, and the optimal capital structure lies at the point 
where WACC is minimised without jeopardising the company’s financial 
health.  
B) Conservative Approach:
The conservative approach emphasises stability and financial security. 
Companies following this approach rely more on equity financing and 
maintain a lower level of debt in their capital structure. By having a higher 
proportion of equity, the company reduces financial risk and has a stronger 
ability to withstand economic downturns or unforeseen obstacles.  
While the conservative approach provides a more secure financial position, it 
may result in a higher cost of capital compared to the hedging approach. 
Equity financing is often more expensive due to the costs associated with 
issuing new shares and the potential dilution of ownership for existing 
shareholders.

22.5  DIVIDEND POLICY 
The establishment of a dividend policy is an important function of the finance 
manager. This section deals with the determination of dividend policy and the 
various forms of dividends.  
The term dividend refers to that part of the profits of a company which is 
distributed amongst its shareholders. It may, therefore, be defined as the 
return that a shareholder gets from the company, out of its profits. on his 
shareholdings. According to the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, a 
dividend is “a distribution to shareholders out of profits or reserves available 
for this purpose.”  
A) Nature of Dividend Decision
The dividend decision of the firm is of crucial importance for the finance 
manager since it determines the amount of profit to be distributed among 
shareholders and the amount of profit to be required in the business 
(popularly termed as retained earnings) for financing its long-term growth. 
There is a reciprocal relationship between the cash dividends and retained 
earnings. Larger dividends result in less retained earnings. Fewer dividends 
result in larger retained earnings. While making dividend decisions, the 
management will consider the effect of the decision on the maximisation of 
shareholders’ wealth. In case the payment of dividends helps the management 
in achieving this objective, it would be advisable to pay dividends. In case the 
payment of dividends does not help in achieving this objective, the 
management would be well advised to retain the profits and use them for 
financing investment programmes. Thus, the dividend decision is based on its 
impact on the value of the firm.  
B) Conflicting Theories
There are conflicting theories regarding the impact of dividend decisions on 
the valuation of a firm. According to one school of thought, dividend 
decisions do not affect only the shareholders’ wealth and so also the

valuation of the firm. According to another school of thought, dividend 
decision materially affects the shareholders’ wealth and the valuation of the 
firm. For the sake of convenience of discussion, we can put the viewpoints of 
scholars under the following two groups:  
1) Concept of Irrelevance of Dividend
This school of thought is associated with Soloman, Modigliani and Miller. 
According to them, dividend policy does not affect the share prices of a 
company and is, therefore, of no consequence. Investors do not differentiate 
between dividends and capital gains. Their basic desire is to earn a higher 
return on their investment. In case the company has adequate investment 
opportunities giving a higher rate of return than the cost of retained earnings, 
the investors would be content with the firm retaining the earnings. However, 
if the expected return on projects is likely to be less than what it would cost, 
the investors would prefer to receive the earnings (i.e. dividends). Thus, a 
dividend decision is a financing decision, i.e., whether to finance the 
company’s funds requirements by retained earnings or not. In case the 
company has profitable investment opportunities, it will retain the earnings to 
finance them, otherwise distribute them. The shareholders are only interested 
in income whether it is in the form of dividends or capital gains.  
2) Modigliani and Miller’s Approach
Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller have expressed their opinion more 
comprehensively. They have opined that the price of shares of a firm is 
determined by its earning potentiality and investment policy and never by the 
pattern of income distribution. As observed by them, “under conditions of 
perfect capital markets, rational investors, absence of tax discrimination 
between dividend income and capital appreciation, given the firm’s 
investment policy its dividend policy may not influence the market price of 
the shares.”  
The logic put forward by Modigliani and Miller in support of their hypothesis 
is that whatever increase in shareholders’ wealth results from dividend 
payments, will be exactly offset by the effect of raising additional capital. For 
example, if a company, having investment opportunities, distributes all its 
earnings among the shareholders, it will have to raise the capital required 
from outside. This will increase the number of shares, resulting in a fall in the 
future earnings per share. Thus, whatever a shareholder has gained because of 
increased dividends will be neutralised completely on account of a fall in the 
value of shares due to a decline in the expected earnings per share.

22.6.1  Importance of Capital Budgeting 
Capital budgeting holds immense significance for businesses and 
organisations due to various compelling reasons:  
1) Long-term investment choices: Capital budgeting involves making
decisions about long-term investments that have enduring effects. These
investments typically require substantial financial resources, and their
success or failure can profoundly impact the company's future growth
and profitability.
2) Optimal resource allocation: By utilising capital budgeting techniques,
companies can efficiently allocate their limited resources, such as funds
and workforce, to projects that are expected to yield the highest returns.
This ensures that resources are utilised effectively to create maximum
value for the organisation.
3) Maximising shareholder wealth: The main goal of capital budgeting is
to pinpoint and choose projects that contribute to the growth of the
company’s shareholders’ wealth. By investing in projects with
favourable net present values (NPVs) or significant internal rates of
return (IRRs), the company can boost shareholder value and appeal to
potential investors.
4) Strategic alignment: Capital budgeting decisions are closely tied to a
company’s strategic objectives. It ensures that projects chosen for
investment align with the organisation’s long-term goals, enabling the
allocation of resources to endeavours that contribute to the company’s
overall vision.
5) Risk assessment: Capital budgeting involves a comprehensive
evaluation of the risks associated with different investment projects.
Understanding potential risks and uncertainties enables companies to
make informed decisions and implement risk mitigation strategies when
necessary.
6) Capital rationing: In situations where capital is limited, capital
budgeting helps prioritise investment opportunities based on their

of financial resources. Focusing on profitable projects allows companies

22.6.2  Kinds of Capital Investment Proposals 
A firm may be presented with multiple investment proposals, and their 
decision to adopt one, some, or all of them depends on their categorisation as 
independent, contingent, or mutually exclusive.  
a) Independent Proposals:
These investment proposals are non-competing, implying that accepting one 
proposal does not prevent the acceptance of another. The firm can directly 
assess each proposal’s return on investment and accept those that offer 
returns higher than the desired rate of return.  
Proposals meeting this criterion are accepted, while the rest are rejected. 
b) Contingent or Dependent Proposals:
These proposals’ acceptance relies on the approval of one or more other 
proposals. For instance, the acquisition of a new machine may be contingent 
upon a substantial plant expansion. In such cases, the dependent investment 
proposal should be presented along with the related proposal it relies on, 
providing a comprehensive perspective.  
c) Mutually Exclusive Proposals:
These proposals are competing in such a way that accepting one 
automatically excludes the acceptance of others. For instance, if a company 
is evaluating two temperature control systems and decides to invest in one 
system, it means rejecting the other. Consequently, the firm needs to employ 
a decision-making technique to choose the most favourable option, leading to 
the exclusion of other alternatives.
