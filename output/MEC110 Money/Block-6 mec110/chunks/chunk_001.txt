Frontier 18.1 INTRODUCTION 
In the realm of finance, the adage “Don't put all your eggs in one basket” 
holds significant importance. This principle underscores the fundamental 
strategy of spreading investments across various assets and asset classes to 
mitigate risk and optimize returns. 
Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across various assets 
and asset classes to mitigate risk and optimize returns. It means creating a 
well-balanced portfolio, wherein a range of investments is judiciously 
selected and blended to counteract the impact of volatility in any single 
investment. 
The need for diversification stems from the volatility inherent in financial 
markets. Given that certain investments may thrive while others may decline 
due to various factors such as economic recessions or geopolitical unrest, 
diversification serves as a strategy for investors to mitigate the impact of 
adverse market fluctuations. By spreading their investments across different 
assets, investors seek to protect their wealth from significant losses in the 
long run. 
Diversification is a key principle within portfolio management. A portfolio 
represents a collection of investments held by an individual or entity, 
comprising a broad range of assets including stocks, bonds, mutual funds, 
real estate, and other financial instruments. 
Many people find it difficult to make effective investment decisions because 
of uncertainty in the market, conflicting investment goals and sometimes 
even due to heavy information load. In response to these challenges, 
numerous theories in portfolio management have endeavoured to provide 
solutions. They have tried to answer questions like how to diversify. Which 
financial assets to invest in? How to combine the assets into a portfolio? 
What is the portfolio’s risk? how to calculate the expected return? etc. 
In this unit, we will discuss portfolio management, expected return and 
valuation of portfolio, relationship between risk and return. We will also 
discuss Markowitz’s Portfolio theory and Efficient Portfolio frontier. In 
subsequent units, we will talk about the Capital asset pricing model (CAPM) 
and Arbitrage pricing theory (APT). 
18.2  PORTFOLIO MANAGEMENT 
A portfolio is essentially a collection of investments owned by an individual 
or entity, which can include various types of assets like stocks, bonds, mutual 
funds, and real estate. However, it’s more than just a bunch of investments 
grouped; it’s a strategic approach to managing wealth, minimizing risks, and 
achieving financial goals.

Portfolio management is the practice of overseeing and optimizing these 
investment collections. It involves carefully deciding how to allocate 
resources, balancing risk and returns, and adapting to changes in the market. 
This includes activities such as deciding which assets to invest in, evaluating 
the level of risk involved, and monitoring how well the portfolio is 
performing. 
The main goal of portfolio management is to create portfolios that match the 
specific financial goals, risk tolerance, and timeframes of investors. Whether 
someone wants to preserve capital, generate income, or increase wealth, 
portfolio managers develop strategies tailored to these objectives. They do 
this through thorough analysis and smart decision-making, aiming to achieve 
the best possible returns while minimizing the chances of losses. 
Additionally, portfolio management is an ongoing process that requires 
constant attention and adjustment. Changes in the market shifts in the 
economy, and alterations in investors’ situations mean that portfolios need to 
be reviewed and adjusted regularly. This means that successful portfolio 
management relies on a combination of financial expertise, careful research, 
and proactive decision-making. 
18.2.1  Stages of Portfolio Management 
Portfolio management is a dynamic procedure that includes the following 
fundamental stages: 
 Understanding the goals, constraints, and preferences of investors to
create an investment policy.
 Creating and executing strategies aligned with the formulated investment
policy.
 Choosing asset classes and specific securities within each class based on
their risk-return characteristics.
 Regularly assessing and tracking the portfolio’s performance by
consistently evaluating market conditions and company performance.
 Analyzing the portfolio’s outcomes to compare against predetermined
targets and making necessary adjustments for future enhancements.
18.2.2  Benefits of Portfolio Management 
1) Consistent Risk Measurement: It ensures that risks are measured in the
same way every time, providing a clear understanding of potential risks.
2) Considering Correlation and Diversification : It takes into account
how different parts of the organization or investment options relate to
each other. This understanding can help in reducing overall risk.

18.3 RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN RISK AND 
RETURN 
Most investors are risk-averse. This does not mean that people will not take a 
risk. That means they will take a risk only if they expect to be adequately 
rewarded for taking it. People have different degrees of risk aversion. Some 
are more willing to take a chance than others. Choosing an investment 
management strategy involves weighing the trade-off between risk and 
return. Active management might yield higher returns but comes with a 
greater risk of not meeting the portfolio’s required return. Conversely, 
passive management offers market-based returns in diversified portfolios, 
reducing risky bets but potentially limiting excess returns. When deciding 
between passive and active management, it’s crucial to determine if the goal 
is to solve a cash flow problem over time or to outperform the market.

18.3.1  Risks Involved in Investments 
Total risk refers to the overall variability of the returns of a financial asset. It 
combines both diversifiable and non-diversifiable risks and represents the 
overall uncertainty and potential for loss associated with an investment. 
Recognizing the types of risks allows investors to implement appropriate risk 
management strategies. Understanding the different types of risks helps 
investors construct well-balanced portfolios. By diversifying across assets 
and sectors, investors can reduce the impact of specific risks on their overall 
portfolio performance. 
The following types of risks are faced in the market: 
Diversifiable risk, also known as unsystematic risk (unique risks), refers to 
firm-specific risks that affect individual stock prices rather than the entire 
industry or sector.  
The key components of diversifiable risk are business, financial, and 
management risks. Diversifiable risk is crucial for investors seeking better 
returns and protecting their initial investment. 
An uncertain risk can arise from various factors such as scams, labour strikes, 
regulatory penalties, management changes, internal issues, or news specific 
to the firm. Diversifiable risk can be mitigated through diversification, which 
involves spreading investments across different assets or sectors. By 
diversifying, investors can reduce the impact of adverse events on any single 
investment. 
Systematic risks, or non-diversifiable risks, are the risks that affect the overall 
market or a specific sector. It is influenced by factors that impact the entire 
economy, such as macroeconomic indicators, market sentiment, or 
geopolitical events. Systematic risks are inherent risks that exist in the stock 
market. They’re also called “non-diversifiable risks” or “market risks” since 
they impact the entire asset class. 
Non-diversifiable means that an organization can’t control, minimize, or 
avoid systematic risks. These risks are typically due to various external 
factors like the current geopolitical situation, monetary policy, and natural 
disasters. Non-diversifiable risk cannot be eliminated through diversification 
because it is inherent in the market itself. Investors are exposed to non-
diversifiable risk regardless of how diversified their portfolios are. The only 
way to manage non-diversifiable risk is through strategies such as asset 
allocation or hedging. For example, COVID-19 is a systematic risk because it 
impacted the entire stock market. 
Diversifiable risk analysis is essential for investors and portfolio managers as 
it helps in understanding and managing the risks associated with individual 
securities or assets. By assessing and diversifying their investments, investors 
can reduce the impact of firm-specific risks and potentially improve their 
risk-return profile. Diversifiable risk analysis enables investors to make

18.3.3  Expected Returns 
Expected return denotes the anticipated profit or loss an investor can 
reasonably foresee from an investment within a specific timeframe. It is an 
important factor that determines portfolio selection. To ascertain return, 
various factors like market trends, economic predictions and historical data 
are analysed.  
The concept of expected return is useful because there is uncertainty about 
the return or investment. Thus, an expected return means the average return 
that an investor expects to receive on investment over some time. It is 
calculated by the sum of each possible return multiplied by its probability.
