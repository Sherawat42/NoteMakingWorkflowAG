21.4  MODELS OF DERIVATIVE PRICING 
Derivative pricing is a complex process as it involves determining the fair 
value of a derivative contract. The pricing of derivatives depends on various 
factors, including the type of derivative, the characteristics of the underlying 
asset, market conditions, and the methodologies used. This section discusses 
two popular derivative pricing models: Binomial Option Pricing Model and 
Black-Scholes Option Pricing Model.  
21.4.1  Binomial Option Pricing Model 
The binomial options pricing model, initially introduced by Cox, Ross, and 
Rubinstein in 1979, offers a versatile numerical approach to valuing options. 
This model employs a “discrete-time” framework to track the evolving price 
of the underlying financial instrument over time, facilitating option valuation 
through the application of the risk neutrality assumption. 
Unlike other models, the binomial options pricing model excels in handling 
diverse conditions, including American options that can be exercised at any 
point and Bermudan options exercisable at various points. Its simplicity and 
mathematical feasibility make it easily implementable in software or 
spreadsheet environments. While it may be slower than the Black-Scholes 
model, practitioners often prefer it for its accuracy, especially in valuing 
longer-dated options and those associated with dividend payments. 
For options involving multiple uncertainties (e.g., real options) or complex 
features (e.g., Asian options), lattice methods encounter challenges, making 
Monte Carlo option models more suitable. However, Monte Carlo simulation, 
although comprehensive, is computationally intensive and not the preferred 
choice when lattice methods or formulas are adequate. 
The binomial pricing model operates within a “discrete-time framework,” 
utilizing a binomial lattice (tree) to trace the evolution of the option’s 
underlying variable across multiple time steps. Each node in the lattice 
represents a potential price of the underlying at a specific point in time, 
forming the basis for option valuation. The valuation process involves three 
key steps: 


--- Page 59 ---
153 
Pricing of Derivatives 1) Price tree generation: Creating a tree to depict the evolution of the
underlying asset’s price.
2) Calculation of option value at final nodes: Determining the option’s
intrinsic (exercise) value at the expiration of the option.
3) Progressive calculation of option value at earlier nodes: Iteratively
calculating the option value at each preceding node using the risk
neutrality assumption, where the fair price today is equal to the
discounted expected value of its future payoff. The “Binomial Value” at
each node is determined by weighting the option values from the later
two nodes by their respective probabilities of an up or down move and
discounting them at the risk-free rate.
For an American option, which can be held or exercised before expiry, the 
value at each node is the maximum of the Binomial Value and the Exercise 
Value. The Binomial Value is computed using the formula: 
Binomial Value=[p × Option up + (1−p)×Option down]×exp(−r×t) 
Here, p represents the probability of an up move in the underlying, 
(1−p) is the probability of a down move, 
r is the risk-free rate, 
and t is the time to expiration. 
This process is repeated for each node, concluding at the valuation date, 
where the final result represents the option's value. 
21.4.2  The Black-Scholes Option Pricing Model 
The Black–Scholes model serves as a mathematical framework for evaluating 
the evolving prices of financial instruments, particularly stocks. Fischer 
Black and Myron Scholes developed the Black–Scholes formula in 1973, 
building upon earlier work by Edward Thorpe, Paul Samuelson, and Robert 
C. Merton. This model is widely used in practice to value call and put 
options. The model is based on the following assumptions:  
1) The underlying share/stock does not generate dividends during the life of
the call option.
2) Transaction costs are zero.
3) The risk-free rate of return is known and it remains constant during the
life of the option.
4) The option can be exercised at the expiry only not before that. It means
the option is a European not American.
5) Short selling is allowed and the short seller will get immediately the full
cash proceeds of today’s price for a security sold short.
6) Stock price moves randomly.

Advantages & Disadvantages of the Black-Scholes Model: 
Advantage: One major advantage of the Black-Scholes Model is its speed, 
allowing for the rapid calculation of a large number of option prices. 
Limitation: The model’s significant limitation is its inability to accurately 
price American-style options, as it only calculates prices at expiration and 
does not account for potential early exercise steps. 
Assumptions underlying the Black-Scholes model include the absence of 
arbitrage opportunities, the possibility of short selling at all times, no 
transaction costs or taxes, perfect divisibility of securities, continuous 
trading, and no dividends paid by the underlying stock during the option’s 
lifetime. Additionally, it assumes that the risk-free rate (r) and stock volatility 
(σ) are known over the option's lifetime. 
21.4.3  Relation between the Binomial Options Pricing Model 
& Black-Scholes Model 
Both the binomial and Black-Scholes models share a foundation rooted in the 
assumption that stock prices follow a stochastic process described by 
geometric Brownian motion. For European options, the binomial model 
approaches the Black-Scholes formula as the number of binomial calculation 
steps increases. In essence, the Black-Scholes model for European options 
can be seen as a special case of the binomial model with an infinite number 
of steps, representing a continuous process approximation. Therefore, the 
binomial model offers discrete approximations to the continuous process 
underlying the Black-Scholes model.

21.5  MARKET OF DERIVATIVES IN INDIA 
India has two types of derivative markets: the exchange-traded market and 
the over-the-counter (OTC) market. In the exchange-traded market, 
standardized contracts are traded on an exchange. In contrast, the OTC 
market is decentralized, with contracts negotiated directly between two 
parties. SEBI is the regulator of the derivative market.  
Trade in equity and index derivatives 
If you want to trade in equity and index derivatives, you can do so through 
the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). The 
derivative market in India started in the early 1990s. The first derivatives 
contract was launched on the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) in June 2000. 
Since then, the derivative market in India has grown exponentially 1. There

21.7  KEY WORDS 
Arbitrageurs  :  Investors engaging in arbitrage to exploit price 
discrepancies in securities to earn riskless 
returns. 
Call Option  :  A financial contract providing the holder the 
right to purchase an asset at a predetermined 
price within a specified timeframe. 
Forward Contract  :  A mutual agreement between two parties to 
exchange an asset for cash at a predetermined 
future date, with the current price fixed at the 
time of agreement. 
Hedge  :
 An investment made to offset potential losses in 
another investment, creating a balance between 
the two.

:    Futures contracts based on fixed-income

Interest Rate Options    : Options contracts related to fixed-income 
securities such as treasury bills. 
Interest Rate Parity  :  The concept suggesting that the difference in 
interest rates between two countries equals the 
disparity between the forward and spot rates of 
those respective countries. 
Long Position  :
 The position of an investor entitled to receive an 
asset in the future, representing the buyer's role 
in the security. 
Margin  : A portion of the transaction value that the 
customer must pay to initiate a transaction, with 
the remaining amount often borrowed from the 
broker. Two types of margins exist: initial 
margin and maintenance margin.
