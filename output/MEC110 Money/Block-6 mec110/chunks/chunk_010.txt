22.6.4  Computation of Capital Investment 
The computation of capital investment required involves calculating the total 
amount of funds needed to initiate and implement a specific project or 
investment opportunity. It encompasses various components and expenses 
that contribute to the overall cost of the project. The capital investment 
required can be calculated using the following steps:  
Step 1: Identify Project Components: 
When computing the capital investment required, the first step is to identify 
all the components and expenses that are essential for the successful initiation 
and implementation of the project. These components could include:  
1) F ixed Assets: This involves the cost of acquiring long-term assets such
as machinery, equipment, land, buildings, vehicles, and any other
infrastructure needed for the project.
2) Working Capital: It refers to the funds required to finance the day-to-
day operations of the project, including inventory, raw materials, and
operational expenses.
3) Initial Setup Costs: This category includes expenses related to project
planning, feasibility studies, legal fees, permits, licenses, and any other
preliminary costs necessary to get the project off the ground.
4) Research and Development (R&D): For projects that involve product
development or innovation, R&D expenses must be accounted for.
5) Marketing and Promotion: For projects that require market entry or
product promotion, marketing and promotional expenses need to be
considered.

Corporate Finance 22.6.5  Forecasting Future Costs 
Forecasting future costs is a crucial aspect of capital budgeting as it enables 
businesses to estimate the financial implications of an investment project 
accurately. When making capital budgeting decisions, organisations need to 
project the costs associated with the proposed project over its entire life 
cycle. Accurate cost forecasting helps in evaluating the feasibility, 
profitability, and risk of the investment, allowing management to make well-
informed decisions. Here are some steps and considerations for forecasting 
future costs during capital budgeting:  
1) Comprehensive Project Analysis:  Start by conducting a thorough
analysis of the proposed project. Understand its scope, objectives, and
the resources required for successful implementation. Consider all cost
elements, including initial investment, operating expenses, maintenance
costs, and potential expansion or replacement costs.
2) Historical Data and Benchmarking : Use historical data from similar
projects or industry benchmarks to gain insights into potential cost
patterns. This data can help in making more accurate assumptions and
estimates for future costs.
3) Inflation and Price Fluctuations:  Account for inflation and potential
price fluctuations of resources, materials, and labour over the project's
duration. These factors can significantly impact project costs over time.
4) Economic and Market Conditions:  Consider the current economic
conditions and future market trends that could influence the cost of
inputs, labour, and other resources.
5) Consultation with Experts:  Engage subject matter experts and
professionals who have experience in similar projects. Their insights and
knowledge can provide valuable inputs for cost estimation.
6) Sensitivity Analysis:  Perform sensitivity analysis by evaluating the
potential impact of changes in cost assumptions on the project's financial
viability. This helps identify the most critical cost drivers and the level of
uncertainty in cost projections.
7) Cost Breakdown Structure:  Develop a cost breakdown structure that
categorises and itemises various cost components, such as direct costs,
indirect costs, fixed costs, variable costs, and overheads.
8) Contingency Planning: Incorporate contingency allowances in the cost
projections to account for unforeseen events and potential cost overruns.
9) Cash Flow Projections:  Prepare cash flow projections that outline the
timing and magnitude of costs throughout the project's life cycle. This
aids in assessing the project's impact on the company's cash flow.

Developing countries like India are attractive destinations for Foreign Direct 
Investment (FDI). FDI is an important source of external private finance. 
FDI is a way of integrating international economics as it creates and 
promotes stable and long-lasting links between economies. It offers the 
following major benefits to economies: a) promotes international trade 
through access to foreign markets, b) facilitates the technology transfer 
between countries, c) leads to economic development, and d) creates job 
opportunities. During the financial year 2021-22, India received foreign 
direct investment inflows of USD 82.274 billion and USD 54.934 billion in 
the first three quarters of the financial year 2022-23. Various initiatives like 
policy reforms, relaxation in regulations etc. are being taken to boost foreign 
investment.  
Similarly, Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI) is essential in modern 
economies with a sophisticated financial system. FPI allows foreign parties to 
invest in financial assets and instruments in the host country. This has a 
positive bearing on the price of domestic assets.  
23.2  CONCEPT OF FDI 
Foreign direct investment (FDI) can be defined as an investment made 
outside the home country where an investor resident in one country invests in 
another country in a way that establishes a long interest in and a high degree 
of influence over an enterprise. Through such investment, the investor owns 
10 per cent or more of the voting power and thereby can exercise a high 
degree of influence in the management and operations of the foreign 
business. 
Definition of FDI 
The International Monetary Fund (IMF) defines FDI as: “A category of 
international investment that reflects the objective of a resident entity in one 
economy (direct investor or parent enterprise) obtaining a lasting interest and 
control in an enterprise resident in another economy (direct investment 
enterprise)”. 
FDI or ‘Foreign Direct Investment’ means investment through capital 
instruments by a person resident outside India in an unlisted Indian company, 
or in ten per cent or more of the post-issue paid-up equity capital on a fully 
diluted basis of a listed Indian company.

23.4  TYPES OF FDI 
The various types of FDI are: 
1) Horizontal FDI: Investing funds in a company other than the domestic
company that belongs to the same industry as that owned or operated by
the FDI investor is called horizontal FDI. In other words, when a
company initiates a similar operation or business model in another
country, it refers to Horizontal FDI. For example, suppose a foreign
company manufacturing apparel invests in or purchases an Indian
company dealing in the same line of business which is apparel. Both
companies belong to the same industry which is apparel, such FDI is
therefore an example of horizontal FDI.
2) Vertical FDI:  This form of FDI refers to the investments that are made
within a supply chain in a company, which may or may not belong to the
same industry. It occurs when a multinational acquires an operation that
either acts as a supplier or distributor. It can be said that when vertical
FDI happens, a business invests in an overseas firm which may supply or
sell products.
Vertical FDIs can take two ways backward vertical integration and
forward vertical integration. For example, A foreign fast-food chain
invests in agricultural operations in India to secure a steady supply of
fresh produce like vegetables and meat for its restaurant outlets.

3) Conglomerate FDI: Such a form of FDI occurs when a company invests
