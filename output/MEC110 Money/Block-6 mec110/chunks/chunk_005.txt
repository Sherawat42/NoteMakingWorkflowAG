As we know from Unit 19, the CAPM, born in the 1960s through the 
collaborative efforts of William Sharpe, John Lintner, and Jan Mossin, laid a 
foundational understanding of expected returns about systematic risk. 
However, as financial markets evolved, critiques arose, with Stephen A. Ross 
leading the charge. Ross’s critique of the CAPM centred on its reliance on a 
single market factor to explain asset prices. In his influential work, “The 
Arbitrage Theory of Capital Asset Pricing,” Ross proposed an alternative 
approach, suggesting that multiple risk factors impact asset prices. This 
critique prompted a paradigm shift, urging scholars to move beyond the 
simplicity of the CAPM towards more nuanced models that could better 
capture the intricacies of financial markets. We have already discussed about 
CAPM model in the previous unit, let us discuss its criticism and the APT 
model as an alternative to the CAPM model. 
20.2  ROSS’S CRITIQUE OF THE CAPITAL ASSET 
PRICING MODEL (CAPM) 
Stephen A. Ross, who developed the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT), 
pointed out several drawbacks and simplifying assumptions of the Capital 
Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). Let us briefly discuss the drawbacks and 
simplifying assumptions of CAPM model once again. 
20.2.1  Drawbacks of CAPM 
1) Single-Factor Model: CAPM is a single-factor model that relies heavily
on the market risk factor (beta). It assumes that all systematic risk can be
captured by a single factor, which might oversimplify the real-world
complexities of asset pricing.
2) Homogeneous Expectations:  CAPM assumes that all investors share
the same expectations about future returns, standard deviations, and
correlations. This assumption overlooks the diversity of investor beliefs
and expectations in the market.
3) Static Risk:  CAPM assumes that the risk associated with an asset is
constant over time. In reality, the risk profile of an asset may change due
to various factors such as economic conditions, technological
advancements, or changes in market sentiment.
4) Risk-Free Rate Assumption: The model relies on a risk-free rate as a
baseline. However, the actual risk-free rate might not be constant, and
using government bond yields as a proxy for the risk-free rate assumes
that there is no default risk, which may not always hold.
20.2.2  Simplifying Assumptions of CAPM 
1) Perfect Markets:  CAPM assumes the existence of perfect and
frictionless financial markets. This implies that there are no transaction
costs, no taxes, and no restrictions on short selling. In reality, markets are
not always perfect, and these assumptions might not hold.

2) Homogeneous Assets: CAPM assumes that all assets are homogeneous
and can be completely described by their expected return and beta. In
reality, assets may have unique characteristics that are not captured by
these simplified parameters.
3) Normal Distribution of Returns:  The model assumes that asset returns
are normally distributed. However, in practice, financial markets often
exhibit non-normalities, such as fat tails and volatility clustering, which
are not accounted for in the CAPM.
4) Constant Correlations:  CAPM assumes constant correlations between
assets. In reality, correlations can change over time, especially during
periods of financial distress or market turbulence.
5) Investor Rationality:  CAPM assumes that investors are rational and
make decisions solely based on expected returns and risks. Behavioural
finance research suggests that investors may not always behave
rationally, and psychological factors can influence decision-making.
Understanding these drawbacks and simplifying assumptions is crucial
for recognizing the limitations of CAPM and considering alternative
models, such as the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT), which seeks to
address some of these issues by allowing for multiple factors and a more
flexible framework.

20.3 INTRODUCTION TO ARBITRAGE PRICING 
THEORY 
Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) is an asset pricing model that was developed 
by economist Stephen Ross in the early 1970s as an alternative to the Capital 
Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). The APT is designed to provide a more 
flexible framework for explaining the expected returns on assets by allowing 
for multiple sources of systematic risk. Unlike CAPM, APT doesn’t rely on 
the assumption of a single systematic risk factor (market risk), and it does not 
specify the nature or number of risk factors in advance.

Stephen Ross introduced APT in his seminal paper titled “The Arbitrage 
Theory of Capital Asset Pricing” published in 1976. The theory was 
developed as a response to perceived limitations and simplifying assumptions 
of CAPM. APT acknowledges that an asset’s expected return is influenced by 
various macroeconomic factors, and it seeks to model these influences more 
realistically and dynamically. 
20.3.1  Assumptions of Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) 
Systematic and Unsystematic Risk in APT: 
1) Separation of Systematic and Unsystematic Risk:  A key assumption 
of APT is the separation of systematic and unsystematic risk. Systematic 
risk is captured by the sensitivity of an asset’s return to various 
macroeconomic factors (as represented by factor betas in the APT 
model). Unsystematic risk, also known as specific or idiosyncratic risk, 
is assumed to be diversifiable and can be eliminated through a well-
diversified portfolio. APT assumes that investors can eliminate 
unsystematic risk through proper portfolio construction. We have 
discussed about portfolio construction in Unit 18. 
Market Efficiency and Rational Expectations: 
2) Efficient Markets: APT assumes that financial markets are efficient. In 
an efficient market, asset prices fully reflect all available information, 
and mispricing is quickly corrected through the actions of rational and 
profit-seeking investors. This assumption is crucial for the functioning of 
arbitrage opportunities, as any deviation from fair value would be 
exploited by arbitrageurs. 
3) Rational Expectations:  APT assumes that investors have rational 
expectations, meaning they form their expectations about future asset 
prices based on all available information. Rational expectations are 
essential for the correct pricing of assets in an efficient market and for 
the proper functioning of arbitrage. 
Arbitrage Opportunities and Their Impact on Asset Pricing: 
4) Absence of Arbitrage Opportunities:  A fundamental assumption in 
APT is the absence of arbitrage opportunities. If arbitrage opportunities 
were present, investors could make risk-free profits by exploiting 
mispricing, leading to market adjustments and undermining the model’s 
ability to explain asset pricing. The assumption of no arbitrage ensures 
that asset prices accurately reflect their underlying risks. 
Rational Behaviour of Investors in APT: 
5) Investor Rationality:  APT assumes that investors are rational decision-
makers. Rational behaviour implies that investors make choices that 
maximize their expected utility, given their information and preferences.

Rationality is crucial for the proper functioning of arbitrage and for 
ensuring that asset prices align with the expectations implied by the

In summary, the assumptions of APT revolve around the separation of 
systematic and unsystematic risk, the efficiency of financial markets, the 
rational expectations of investors, and the absence of arbitrage opportunities. 
These assumptions provide the foundation for the model’s ability to explain 
asset pricing dynamics based on the influence of multiple macroeconomic 
factors. 
20.3.2  Theoretical Foundations of APT 
The Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) provides a framework for understanding 
the relationship between risk and expected return in financial markets. It was 
developed by Stephen Ross in 1976 as an alternative to the Capital Asset 
Pricing Model (CAPM). 
The APT formula for expected asset returns is given by: 
E(Ri)=Rf+βi1×λ1+βi2×λ2+…+βik×λk+ϵi
Where: 
 E(Ri) is the expected return on asset i,
 Rf is the risk-free rate,
 βij represents the sensitivity of asset i to factor j,
 λj is the risk premium associated with factor j,
 ϵi is the specific or idiosyncratic return component.
No Arbitrage Assumption: 
APT operates under the assumption of no arbitrage opportunities in the 
market. This implies that investors cannot consistently earn risk-free profits 
by exploiting price discrepancies between assets. The absence of arbitrage 
opportunities ensures that asset prices are efficient and reflect all available 
information. 
Law of One Price: 
APT relies on the law of one price, which states that assets with similar risk 
exposures should have similar expected returns to prevent arbitrage 
opportunities. In other words, assets that share common risk factors should be 
priced in such a way that investors are indifferent between them. 
Flexibility and Dynamism: 
One of the key advantages of APT is its flexibility and dynamism in pricing 
assets. Unlike CAPM, which relies on a single systematic risk factor (market 
risk), APT accommodates multiple systematic risk factors. This allows for a

more nuanced understanding of asset pricing, as different assets may be

Empirical Identification of Factors: 
APT allows for the empirical identification of systematic risk factors based 
on historical data analysis. By analysing the relationship between asset 
returns and various macroeconomic, financial, and market variables, 
researchers can identify the factors that drive asset prices and determine their

Significance and Contribution: 
The development of APT was a significant contribution to the field of 
finance, as it offered an alternative perspective to the traditional CAPM 
framework. By incorporating multiple systematic risk factors and allowing 
for empirical testing, APT provides a more comprehensive approach to asset 
pricing that better reflects the complexities of real-world markets. 
In summary, APT provides a rigorous framework for understanding the 
relationship between risk and expected return in financial markets. Its 
reliance on the absence of arbitrage opportunities, the law of one price, and 
the accommodation of multiple risk factors make it a valuable tool for both 
academics and practitioners in the field of finance.  
20.3.3  Key Differences between APT and CAPM 
1) Number of Factors:
– CAPM:  The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) relies on a single
systematic risk factor, which is market risk. It assumes that all
systematic risk can be captured by the market portfolio.
– APT:  In contrast, the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) allows for the
consideration of multiple systematic risk factors. It recognizes that
assets may be influenced by a variety of economic variables such as
interest rates, inflation, GDP growth, and industry-specific factors.
2) Factor Identification:
– CAPM:  CAPM assumes a specific market factor, represented by the
market portfolio. This market factor is usually proxied by a broad-
based equity index such as the S&P 500.
– APT:  APT does not predefine factors. Instead, it allows researchers to
empirically identify relevant factors based on statistical analysis of
historical data. Factors may vary depending on the market and the
specific assets being analysed.
3) Sensitivity Measures:
– CAPM : In CAPM, the sensitivity of an asset to systematic risk is
measured by beta ((β)). Beta represents the covariance between the

– APT : APT uses factor sensitivities (βij). Each beta (βij) represents the

4) Assumption about Investor Behaviour:
– CAPM : CAPM assumes that investors are rational and risk-averse. It
suggests that investors seek to maximize their utility by balancing risk
and return and will invest in portfolios that offer the highest expected
return for a given level of risk.
– APT : APT does not make explicit assumptions about investor
behaviour. Instead, it focuses on the relationships between asset
returns and macroeconomic factors. It allows for a more flexible and
dynamic approach to asset pricing by accommodating different views
on investor behaviour.
In summary, while both APT and CAPM are asset pricing models that relate 
expected returns to systematic risk, they differ in terms of the number of 
factors considered, the method of factor identification, the sensitivity 
measures used, and the assumptions about investor behaviour. APT’s 
flexibility in accommodating multiple factors and empirical factor 
identification makes it a valuable tool for asset pricing analysis in real-world 
financial markets.

20.4  EMPIRICAL STUDIES ON APT 
The Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) exhibits practical versatility in various 
applications, particularly in asset allocation, cost of capital computation, and 
the evaluation of managed funds. The utilization of APT in asset allocation 
stems from its connection to the factor structure and mean-variance 
efficiency. With k factors implying the existence of efficient frontier-
spanning assets, constructing a mean-variance efficient portfolio becomes 
more straightforward when k factors are the payoffs of traded securities. APT 
simplifies the optimization problem by reducing dimensionality when k is 
small. However, if the factor structure is incorrect, the resulting portfolio may 
not be mean-variance efficient, necessitating adjustments based on investors’ 
beliefs, as proposed by Pastor and Stambaugh (2000) using Bayesian 
methods. 
In the context of calculating the cost of capital, APT has been applied by 
various researchers, such as Elton, Gruber, and Mei (1994) and Bower and 
Schink (1994). However, the challenge lies in the selection of factors, with 
different studies yielding disparate results. This lack of factor specification is 
a significant drawback, contributing to the reluctance of entities like the U.S. 
Federal Reserve Board to adopt APT for certain purposes. 
APT’s application in evaluating money managers involves regressing 
managed funds’ returns on factors and comparing intercepts with benchmark 
securities returns. Notable studies, including those by Busse (1999) and 
Carhart (1997), have explored this application. However, it is essential to 
recognize the one-period nature of APT and its limitations when pricing 
derivatives or trading strategies due to potential arbitrage issues arising from 
the stochastic discount factor. 
The APT’s ironic challenge is evident in the need for extensions to the basic 
model in some applications, violating its core tenet of market arbitrage 
absence. For instance, evaluating dynamic trading strategies may require 
alternative models like Black-Scholes, as suggested by Glosten and 
Jagannathan (1994), highlighting the complexity and limitations in the 
practical application of APT. 
Identification of Challenges and Criticisms from Empirical Studies: 
1) Data Limitations:  Some empirical studies face challenges related to
data limitations. Identifying and collecting accurate historical data for
various macroeconomic factors and asset returns can be complex, and the
quality of results may depend on the availability and reliability of the
data.

2) Model Misspecification:  Critics argue that APT, like any model, is
subject to misspecification. If the chosen factors do not adequately
capture the relevant sources of systematic risk, the model may provide
inaccurate predictions. Empirical studies have explored the sensitivity of
APT to the selection of factors.
3) Time-Varying Betas:  Empirical evidence suggests that factor
sensitivities (betas) may not be constant over time, challenging the
assumption of a static risk profile in APT. Researchers have explored the
implications of time-varying betas on the model's performance.
4) Factor Redundancy:  Some studies have found that identified factors
may be correlated or redundant, raising questions about the
independence of factors assumed by APT. Redundancy in factors could
affect the reliability of the model’s predictions.
5) Market Efficiency Concerns:  Empirical studies testing APT often
assume market efficiency, but real-world markets may deviate from
perfect efficiency. If markets are not fully efficient, it could impact the
ability of arbitrage to correct mispricing and, consequently, the validity
of APT predictions.
In summary, empirical studies on APT have provided valuable insights into 
the model’s performance in explaining asset pricing. While some findings 
support the theory, challenges and criticisms related to data limitations, 
model misspecification, time-varying betas, factor redundancy, and market 
efficiency concerns have been identified. Researchers continue to refine and 
extend empirical testing to enhance our understanding of APT’s strengths and 
limitations in capturing the complexities of asset pricing. 
20.5  CRITICISMS OF APT 
20.5.1  Critical Evaluation of APT’s Assumptions 
1) Arbitrage Opportunities:  Critics argue that the assumption of no
arbitrage opportunities might be too restrictive. In real markets, various
frictions, transaction costs, and constraints may limit the ability of
arbitrageurs to correct mispricing quickly and efficiently.
2) Market Efficiency:  APT relies on the assumption of efficient markets,
where prices fully reflect all available information. In practice, markets
may not always be perfectly efficient, leading to concerns about the
model’s applicability in less-than-perfectly-efficient environments.
3) Factor Independence:  APT assumes that factors influencing asset
returns are independent. Critics question the independence of these
factors, suggesting that correlations or dependencies may exist,
challenging the model’s assumption of uncorrelated risk factors.
4) Static Betas: While APT allows for multiple factors, it often assumes
that factor sensitivities (betas) are constant over time. Critics argue that

5) Investor Rationality:  APT assumes rational expectations, but
behavioural finance research suggests that investors may not always
behave rationally. Behavioural biases and sentiment-driven behaviour
may influence asset pricing, challenging the assumption of investor

20.5.2  Comparison of APT with Other Asset Pricing Models 
1) Comparison with CAPM:  APT has been compared with the Capital
Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). Critics argue that while APT addresses
some of the limitations of CAPM, it introduces its own set of
assumptions and challenges. The choice between APT and CAPM often
depends on the specific characteristics of the data and the underlying
assumptions deemed most appropriate.
2) Challenges Compared to the Fama-French Model:  The Fama-French
Three-Factor Model, an extension of APT, includes market risk, size
(SMB), and value (HML) factors. Some critics argue that the Fama-
French model may offer better explanatory power than APT, especially
in capturing size and value effects observed in empirical studies.
20.5.3  Identifying Areas of Improvement for APT 
1) Dynamic Factor Sensitivities:  Critics suggest that allowing for time-
varying factor sensitivities in APT could enhance the model’s ability to
capture changing risk profiles over different market conditions.
2) Incorporating Behavioural Aspects:  Integrating behavioural finance
concepts into APT could address the limitations associated with the
assumption of investor rationality. Considering psychological factors and
market sentiment may provide a more realistic depiction of asset pricing
dynamics.
3) Enhancing Factor Identification:  Improving methods for identifying
relevant factors is crucial. Empirical studies and model testing should
focus on refining the process of selecting macroeconomic variables that
genuinely drive asset returns, avoiding redundancy and ensuring
independence.
4) Accounting for Nonlinear Relationships: APT typically assumes linear
relationships between factors and asset returns. Acknowledging the
possibility of nonlinear relationships may lead to a more accurate
representation of the complexities in pricing dynamics.
