As we have discussed earlier, a portfolio is a collection of financial assets 
like stocks, bonds, mutual funds, exchange-traded funds (ETFs), real estate, 
commodities, and other investment vehicles owned by an individual, 
institution, or entity.The purpose of building a portfolio is to diversify risk 
and potentially maximize returns by spreading investments across different 
asset classes, industries, and geographic regions.The expected return and 
valuation of the Portfolio act as guiding principles and significantly influence 
investment choices and the assessment of performance.

essential for evaluating performance, making well-founded investment

18.5  MARKOWITZ PORTFOLIO THEORY 
Traditionally, portfolio theory focused on analyzing individual securities 
while considering the balance between risk and return. This theory operated 
on the assumption that securities should be selected based on minimizing 
risk, measured by their standard deviation from the mean of expected returns. 
According to this approach, there’s a direct relationship between the 
variability of returns and risk: the higher the variability, the higher the risk, 
and vice versa. Therefore, investors typically opt for assets with the lowest 
variability of returns. To determine the return on an individual security, 
factors such as the company’s dividend payments, price-earnings ratio, the 
duration of holding, and the market value of shares are considered. 
Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), a hypothesis put forth by Harry Markowitz 
in his paper “Portfolio Selection,” (published in 1952 by the Journal of 
Finance) opened the door to modern portfolio theory.  It is an investment 
theory based on the idea that risk-averse investors can construct portfolios to 
optimize or maximize expected return based on a given level of market risk, 
emphasizing that risk is an inherent part of higher reward. The modern 
portfolio theory emphasises the need for maximization of returns through a 
combination of securities whose total variability is lower. It is not necessary 
that the success could be achieved by trying to get all securities of minimum

18.5.1  Assumptions of the Markowitz Model 
1) Investors are rational.
2) The supply and demand equilibrium is instantly achieved.
3) There are no arbitrage opportunities.
4) Price moves are efficient
5) Access to information is available to all participants.
6) The market is liquid.
7) There is no transaction cost.
8) There are no taxes.
9) Everyone has the same opportunity of borrowing and lending.
The model is also known as the mean-variance model because it is based on 
the expected returns and the standard deviation of different portfolios. It 
helps to make the most efficient selection by analysing various portfolios of 
the given assets. It shows investors how to reduce the risk in case they have 
chosen assets, not moving together. 
Under the assumptions, a single asset or portfolio of assets is considered to be 
efficient. If no other asset or portfolio of assets offers higher expected returns 
with the same or lower risk or lower risk with the same or higher expected 
Return.

Not every portfolio in the opportunities set is appealing to an investor. Some

18.5.3  Efficient Portfolio Frontier 
Harry Markowitz initially conceptualized portfolio theory by defining the 
objective as identifying a collection of efficient portfolios. An efficient 
portfolio is one where no higher expected return can be attained without 
accepting a higher level of risk. Conversely, it’s a portfolio where no greater 
certainty of returns can be obtained without sacrificing expected returns. 
Once this efficient set of portfolios is determined at the asset class level, the 
investment manager selects an allocation along that frontier that aligns most 
closely with the investor’s desired risk-return profile for the fund.This 
collection of efficient portfolios is commonly referred to as the efficient 
frontier. Figure 18.1 illustrates a typical representation of such efficient 
portfolios.

18.5.4  Criticisms of Markowitz’s Portfolio Theory 
While Markowitz’s portfolio theory provides a valuable framework for 
portfolio construction, it is not without its limitations and criticisms. Some of 
the criticisms are: 
1) Unrealistic assumptions: Markowitz’s theory relies on several
assumptions that might not hold in real-world scenarios. For instance, it
assumes that investors are rational, risk-averse, and have access to
unlimited borrowing and lending at a risk-free rate. However, in reality,
investors may not always behave rationally, and their risk preferences
can vary.
2) Complexity and Practicality : Implementing Markowitz’s theory can be
challenging due to its complexity. It involves intricate calculations and
assumptions about correlations and expected returns, which may not
always be accurate or practical to estimate.
3) Sensitivity to Input Data : The efficiency of portfolios generated by
Markowitz's theory is highly sensitive to input data such as expected
returns and covariance matrices. Small changes in these inputs can lead
to significantly different portfolio allocations, making the results
unstable and difficult to rely on.
4) Neglect of Transaction Costs and Taxes : Markowitz’s theory assumes
there are no transaction costs and taxes associated with portfolio
rebalancing. In real-world investing, these costs can significantly impact
returns and portfolio performance.
5) Single-Period Analysis: The theory primarily focuses on single-period
analysis, neglecting the dynamic nature of markets and investors'
changing risk preferences over time. This limitation can lead to
suboptimal portfolio decisions in dynamic market conditions.
6) Ignorance of Behavioural Finance Insights : Markowitz’s theory
largely ignores insights from behavioural finance, which studies how
psychological biases influence investor behaviour. Behavioural factors
such as herd mentality, overconfidence, and loss aversion can
significantly affect portfolio decisions but are not adequately accounted
for in traditional portfolio optimization frameworks.
7) Inability to Capture Tail Risks:  Traditional portfolio optimization
techniques, including Markowitz’s theory, often fail to adequately
