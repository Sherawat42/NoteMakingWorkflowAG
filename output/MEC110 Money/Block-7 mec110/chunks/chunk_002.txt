24.6 POLICY RESPONSES TO A CRISIS 
Now, that we know the adverse impacts of a financial crisis or an economic 
crisis the question arises what can be done? In this section, we will discuss in 
detail the macroeconomic policies to tackle a crisis. A single policy 
intervention, like monetary or fiscal, or both, is generally not sufficient for 
stability in the economy. A combination of different kinds of policy measures 
is required. The following policy measures can be used to fight a crisis. 
Fiscal Policy: We know that in a crisis, financial or economic, 
unemployment increases, and output and aggregate demand decrease. 
Policymakers can use fiscal expansion, in the form of a tax cut or an increase 
in government spending, to increase aggregate demand in the economy. A tax 
cut would lead to an increase in disposable income in the economy, while, an 
increase in government spending can come in the form of expenditure on 
infrastructure, stimulus to industry, social infrastructure (health and 
education), and social welfare schemes. The magnitude of fiscal intervention 
that is required to counter the cyclical downturn depends upon the 
seriousness of the crisis. The bigger the crisis, the larger should be the fiscal 
expansion. 
Monetary Policy: Like fiscal expansion, monetary policy can also be used to 
increase aggregate demand in the economy which would help in improving 
employment and output. A monetary expansion generally comes in the form 
of a reduction in interest rates and an increase in the liquidity or money 
supply in the economy. A decrease in interest rates would lead to a reduction 
in the cost of borrowing, and an increase in liquidity would help in easing the 
credit crunch in the economy. 
Both monetary and fiscal policies have their limitations. A deep crisis may 
require a drastic decrease in the rate of interest but it cannot go below zero. 
Moreover, monetary policy is not useful in a condition of a liquidity trap. 
Fiscal expansion may hurt the interest rates and prices in the economy. In 
such situations, monetary and fiscal policy should be supplemented by 
different regulations directly addressing the issues related to asymmetric 
information, adverse selection, and moral hazard. 
Strengthening of Regulatory Framework: Financial crisis is generally a 
result of asset-price bubbles. Policymakers should strengthen the regulatory 
and monitoring process of the financial sector. This requires continuous

25.2 HISTORY OF EFFICIENT MARKETS 
HYPOTHESIS 
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) is a theory in finance that states 
that financial markets are “informationally efficient,” meaning that asset 
prices reflect all available information at any given time. The basic idea 
behind the EMH is that in an efficient market, investors cannot consistently 
earn abnormal returns (i.e., returns that are higher than what would be 
expected given the level of risk they are taking on) by using any available 
information, because that information is already reflected in the market price. 
The history of the efficient markets hypothesis dates back to the early 20 th 
century when economists such as John Bates Clark and Alfred Marshall 
developed theories about the relationship between prices and information. 
However, the modern version of the EMH began to take shape in the 1950s 
and 1960s, when economists such as Eugene Fama, Paul Samuelson, and 
Burton Malkiel began to develop and refine the theory. 
Eugene Fama is often credited with creating the modern version of the EMH 
in his 1965 paper “Random Walks in Stock Market Prices,” which argued 


--- Page 18 ---
222 
Financial Stability that stock prices follow a random walk and that it is impossible to 
consistently predict future stock prices based on past prices or any other 
available information. This paper, along with others by Fama and other 
economists, helped establish the EMH as a cornerstone of modern finance 
theory. 
Since its inception, the EMH has been the subject of much debate and 
controversy. Some economists and investors argue that the EMH is an 
accurate representation of how financial markets work, while others believe 
that markets are not completely efficient and that it is possible to consistently 
earn abnormal returns through various investment strategies. Nonetheless, the 
EMH remains an important concept in finance and continues to influence the 
way that investors think about the relationship between information and asset 
prices. 
25.3  EFFICIENT MARKETS HYPOTHESIS (EMH) 
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) is a theory in finance that suggests 
that financial markets are “efficient,” which means that asset prices fully 
reflect all available information at any given time. The hypothesis states that 
it is impossible to consistently achieve returns over average market returns 
because any new information that enters the market is immediately and fully 
reflected in the asset prices. In other words, the EMH suggests that it is 
impossible to consistently beat the market by using information that is 
already publicly available since the market has already used that information 
to arrive at the prevailing price. The theory argues that market players cannot 
earn higher returns than the market as a whole, except by taking on more risk. 
Eugene Fama (1970) has given three forms of the EMH which are as follows:  
1) Weak Form: The weak form of EMH asserts that all historical price and
volume data is already incorporated into current stock prices, making it
impossible to profit from analyzing past trends or patterns. This means
that technical analysis, which involves using charts and past market data
to predict future prices, cannot generate consistent profits.
2) Semi-Strong Form: The semi-strong form of EMH asserts that all
publicly available information is already reflected in stock prices,
making it impossible to achieve abnormal returns through fundamental
analysis. This means that researching and analyzing company financial
statements, news articles, and economic data cannot lead to consistent
profits.
3) Strong Form: The strong form of EMH asserts that all information, both
public and private, is already reflected in stock prices, making it
impossible to achieve abnormal returns through any form of analysis or
insider information.

25.4 ASSUMPTIONS OF EMH 
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis is based on the following assumptions: 
1) Rationality: Investors are rational in the sense that they maximise their
interests. They do their best.
2) Information is freely available:  Every investor has easy accessibility to
all market-related information without any restrictions. In other words,
the information is available at zero cost.
3) No asymmetricity of information: It means every investor has the same
amount of information i.e., whatever information investor A has, then the
same information is available to B also.
4) Prices adjust quickly to new information: The response time of the
market to new information is much less. In other words, as new
information is made available, then its effect can be seen in stock prices
and indices very quickly.

25.5 EMH AND CAPITAL ASSET PRICING 
MODEL (CAPM) 
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) and the Capital Asset Pricing 
Model (CAPM) are two important concepts in finance that are closely related 
to each other. 
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis states that (i) financial markets are 
efficient, and (ii) all available information is reflected in asset prices. 
According to the EMH, it is impossible to consistently beat the market 
because all available information is already reflected in the asset prices. In 
other words, the market is already in equilibrium and all the stocks are valued 
at their intrinsic values. Therefore, a stock cannot perform better than the 
market.  
The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) as we have already discussed in 
Unit 20 is an asset pricing model which was suggested by economists 
William F. Sharpe (1964) and Linter (1965). The CAPM states that the 
expected return of an asset (or a security portfolio or stock) is linearly and 
positively related to its systematic risk (the part of total risk which is beyond 
the control of the firm or the investor; it is determined by various economic, 
social and political factors). The CAPM predicts that the systematic risk of an

Specifically, the CAPM assumes that markets are efficient, which means that 
all relevant information is already incorporated into asset prices. In other 
words, the CAPM assumes that investors cannot earn higher returns by using 
publicly available information, which is a core principle of the EMH. 
The CAPM is also used to test the efficiency of markets. If the CAPM holds, 
it would suggest that markets are efficient, because it implies that investors 
cannot earn higher returns by using publicly available information. However, 
if the CAPM is not able to explain returns on assets, it may suggest that 
markets are not efficient.
