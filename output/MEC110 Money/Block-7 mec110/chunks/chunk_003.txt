25.6 ASSESSMENT OF EFFICIENT MARKETS 
HYPOTHESIS 
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) is a theory that suggests that 
financial markets are informationally efficient, meaning that asset prices 
reflect all available information at any given time. While this hypothesis has 
been widely accepted in the academic world, it has also received significant 
criticism from various scholars and practitioners in finance. Some of the main 
criticisms of the EMH are: 
a) Behavioural finance:  We know that the EMH is based upon an
assumption of the rationality of investors, but the studies conducted
under behavioural finance argue that investors are not always rational,
and their decisions are derived from their emotions, biases, or incomplete
information, causing inefficiencies in the market. This means that market
prices may not always reflect all available information, as suggested by
the EMH.
b) Historical contradictions:  Some historical anomalies or contradictions
cannot be explained by the EMH. For example, the momentum effect,
where stocks that have performed well in the past tend to continue to
perform well in the future, and this tendency contradicts the EMH which
states that future prices cannot be predicted based on past prices.
c) Insider trading: The existence of insider trading suggests that some
market participants have access to information that is not available to the
general public, indicating that the market may not be as efficient as the
EMH suggests.
d) Market bubbles: The EMH contends that the price of a security tends to
its intrinsic value i.e., it is neither overvalued nor undervalued. But, the
cases of market bubbles such as the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s and
the housing bubble of the mid-2000s suggest otherwise. In both cases,
asset prices soared far beyond their true intrinsic value, leading to a crash
when the bubble eventually burst.
e) Information asymmetry: The EMH assumes that all market players
have equal information i.e., nobody has more information than the
others. Do you think so? In reality, some participants may have more or
better information than others. This creates an information asymmetry,
which can lead to market inefficiencies.
f) Limited arbitrage:  The EMH argues that the arbitrage process in the
market corrects these inefficiencies caused by over and under-valuation

limited by factors such as transaction costs, liquidity constraints, and 
regulatory restrictions, which may prevent market prices from reflecting 
all available information. 
So, the overall conclusion is that the above criticisms suggest that the EMH 
may not accurately describe the behaviour of financial markets and that 
market inefficiencies may be more common than the EMH suggests. 
25.7 APPLICATIONS OF THE EMH 
Here are some main applications of the EMH: 
a) Index Funds:  The EMH suggests that it is impossible to consistently
outperform the market by picking individual stocks. In other words, the
market prices of the stock are already at their best values and one stock
cannot perform better than the market return.  Therefore, investors can
choose to invest in index funds that track the performance of the overall
market.
b) Passive Investing: Passive investing means an investing strategy which
aims to track the performance of a market index (such as Sensex or
Nifty) instead of thinking that some stock will perform better than the
market. The EMH supports the idea of passive investing, which involves
buying and holding a diversified portfolio of stocks for the long term,
rather than trying to time the market or pick individual stocks.
c) Rational Expectations Theory : The EMH is closely related to the
Rational Expectations Theory, which suggests that individuals make
decisions based on all available information and that markets adjust
rapidly to new information.
d) Academic Research: The EMH has been the subject of a great deal of
academic research, particularly in the fields of finance and economics.
Researchers have used EMH as a framework to test various hypotheses
about market behaviour.
e) Regulatory Policy: The role of regulatory authorities like the Securities
Exchange Board of India (SEBI) may get diluted by implication of the
EMH. The EMH has implications for regulatory policy, as it suggests
that attempts to regulate markets or prevent insider trading may be futile
since all available information is already reflected in stock prices.
f) Investment Strategies : The EMH suggests that traditional investment
strategies, such as technical analysis and fundamental analysis, may be
ineffective. Instead, investors may wish to focus on strategies such as
low-cost index funds, diversification, and risk management.

25.8 APPLICABILITY OF THE EMH IN INDIA 
In the Indian context, the applicability of the EMH has been a subject of 
much debate and research over the years. Here is a brief literature review on 
the same: 
 Bhatia et al. (2017) conducted a study to test the weak form of the EMH
in the Indian stock market using daily stock returns for the period 2006-
2016. Their findings suggested that the Indian stock market is not weak-
form efficient, as there is evidence of predictable patterns in stock
returns that can be exploited for abnormal profits.
 Kumar and Kamaiah (2017) examined the semi-strong form of the EMH
in the Indian stock market using event study methodology. They found
that the Indian stock market is partially efficient in terms of reacting to
news and events, but there is still room for abnormal profits to be made.
 Patro et al. (2009) analyzed the strong form of the EMH in the Indian
stock market by examining insider trading activity. They found that
insiders in the Indian market were able to generate abnormal profits,
which suggests that the market is not strong-form efficient.
 Gupta and Sehgal (2013) investigated the efficiency of the Indian stock
market in the context of the 2008 global financial crisis. They found that
the market was largely efficient during the crisis period, but some
anomalies suggest the presence of market inefficiencies.
 Bhattacharya and Mukherjee (2017) analyzed the efficiency of the Indian
stock market during the period 2006-2016 using a combination of
technical and fundamental analysis. Their findings suggested that the
market is largely efficient, but there are still some anomalies that can be
exploited for abnormal profits.

efficient market, a stock cannot perform better than the market as a whole. 
Three forms of efficiency can be found in the market: a) weak form, b) semi-
strong form, and c) strong form. Weak form means that all past information 
has been absorbed by the market to arrive at the current prices, semi-strong 
form means that all publically available information is incorporated in the 
prices, and strong form means that all information (including inside 
information) is reflected by the market. The Capital Asset Pricing Model

sound financial system.

26.2  CONCEPT OF FINANCIAL STABILITY 
Financial stability refers to a financial system that is resilient to any shock or 
disruptions. The financial system consists of financial institutions like banks, 
non-banking financial institutions, financial markets, and financial 
instruments. Financial stability is achieved when the market volatility is low. 
The financial stability in a country helps ensure the efficient functioning of 
the entire financial system which would result in promoting economic growth 
and reducing poverty. Financial stability in a country ensures: 
a) sufficient availability of capital to financial institutions to ensure
capital adequacy norms are met.
b) sufficient risk management system to ensure protection against credit
risk, liquidity risk, market risk etc.
c) availability of well-developed infrastructure for trading and payment
settlement.
d) robust regulatory framework to ensure that all legal compliance is
being observed and that laws and regulations are not overlooked.

a) Macroeconomic factors: Factors like economic growth, interest rates,
inflation, exchange rates, monetary and fiscal policy etc. have an impact
on the financial stability of India. For instance, if inflation continues to
grow and interest rates continue to increase, it may reduce the
availability of credit to various sectors, result in high default rates, etc.
and ultimately may lead to financial instability in a country.
b) Default risk factor : The risk of default on loans and other credit
instruments can have a significant bearing on financial stability. For
instance, in case a large number of borrowers start defaulting on their
loans, this may result in a liquidity crisis in the banks and will reduce
the confidence of the savings holders, investors and others in the
financial system of the country.
c) Unexpected change in asset prices factor: The change in asset prices,
like stock prices or real estate prices, have a bearing on the financial
stability of the country. The rapid increase in asset prices may result in
asset bubbles, which may burst at any time and can lead to financial
instability in the country.
d) Operational risk factor:  Operational risk refers to the risk due to
operational inefficiency which decreases the operational profits,
sometimes results in losses and threatens the financial capabilities of
any component of the financial system to meet the operational expenses
or routine expenses and finally can impact the financial stability.
e) Liquidity risk factor : Liquidity risk refers to the inability to meet
short-term obligations in time. For instance, suppose at any point in
time if the number of withdrawals from a bank suddenly increases
beyond expectations then the bank may face liquidity issues which in
turn increases the liquidity risk and may result in financial instability.
