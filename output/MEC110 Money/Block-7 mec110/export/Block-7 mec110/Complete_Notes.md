## Section: Macroeconomics and Business Cycles 🔴

### Core Idea
Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole — employment, inflation, GDP, growth, exchange rate, balance of payments, and business cycles. Two questions sit at its centre: how is GDP determined in the short and long run, and how do consumption, investment, inflation, and unemployment shape that determination. Capital formation drives long-run growth, but the actual path is **never smooth** — economies fluctuate, and business cycles are often tangled with problems in the financial sector.

> **In Simple Terms:** A business cycle is the economy's heartbeat — boom, peak, slowdown, slump, repeat. Macroeconomics is the doctor watching the EKG.

### Key Concepts

#### Business Cycle — Four Stages
A business cycle refers to **ups and downs in economic activities** — output, unemployment, income, and other variables — and indicates how the economy is doing. It has four predominant stages:

| Stage | What happens |
|-------|--------------|
| 1. Expansion | Investment rising (low interest rates), output up, employment up, consumption up, demand up, income up |
| 2. Peak / Boom | Economic activity at its highest — economy doing extremely well; "if there is a boom, there is going to be a burst eventually" |
| 3. Contraction / Recession | Aggregate demand falls, output slows, investment slows, employment falls, income falls, consumption decreases |
| 4. Depression | Worst phase — aggregate demand decreases further, investment decreases, production decreases again, more layoffs |

The cycle is **vicious**: lack of demand in one sector → reduction in investment and production → loss of employment → reduction in income → further fall in aggregate demand. One link drags the next.

### Definitions
- **Macroeconomics**: Study of the economy as a whole — focused on employment, inflation, output/GDP, growth rate, exchange rate, balance of payments, and business cycles.
- **Business Cycle** ⭐: Ups and downs in economic activities namely output, unemployment, and income, among other things; indication of how an economy is doing. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Capital Formation**: Build-up of capital base — a major factor responsible for the growth of an economy.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Vicious-cycle mechanism (downturn):**
Lack of demand → ↓ investment → ↓ production → ↑ unemployment → ↓ income → ↓ aggregate demand → repeat

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Four stages: Expansion → Peak → Contraction → Depression
> - Cycle is vicious — one stage feeds the next
> - Business cycles often linked with financial-sector problems

## Section: Case Study — The Great Depression (1929–1941) 🔴

### Core Idea
The Great Depression is the textbook example of how speculative excess in finance, combined with policy missteps, can crush the real economy. In the 1920s the US enjoyed 4.7% growth and 3.2% unemployment, fuelling a wealth-driven stock-market boom under weak regulation. The 1929 crash wiped out 85% of stock value over three years, peak unemployment reached 24%, and 30% of banks failed. Wrong-direction monetary policy and protectionist tariffs deepened the slump; the Depression only ended in 1941.

> **In Simple Terms:** Cheap credit + lax rules + over-confident investors = a giant bubble. When it popped, the Fed raised rates instead of cutting them, tariffs blocked trade, and the US economy bled out for over a decade.

### Timeline & Key Numbers
| Date / Period | Event |
|---------------|-------|
| 1920s | US growth 4.7%, unemployment 3.2%, exorbitant wealth growth |
| Oct 24, 1929 ("Black Thursday") | Market opened down 11% from previous day |
| 1929–1932 | Stock market fell by 85% |
| Peak of Depression | Unemployment 24% (1 in 4 unemployed); 30% of banks failed |
| Within 4 years of recession | US GDP fell by 29% |
| 1933 | President Roosevelt increased government spending — too late |
| 1941 | Depression ended |

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Causal chain:**
1. Weak stock-market regulation → speculation in equities
2. Public investment in stocks fuelled by high wealth → asset-price bubble
3. Banks lent excessively (lowered reserves) + expansionary monetary policy → more borrowing
4. 1929 crash → Fed **raised** interest rates (instead of lowering) → deflation
5. Higher tariffs on agricultural imports (intended to protect US agriculture) → trade contraction → deeper depression
6. Roosevelt's 1933 fiscal stimulus came too late
7. Outcomes: 30% bank failure, factory shutdowns, rural poverty, falling birth rate, rising women's workforce participation

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "The Fed cut rates after the 1929 crash" → ✅ Correct: The Fed **raised** rates, intending weak banks would fail and a stronger system emerge — this caused deflation.
- ❌ Mistake: "Tariffs helped recovery" → ✅ Correct: Higher agricultural tariffs **worsened** the depression by choking trade.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 1929: Black Thursday, –11% in one day
> - 1929–1932: stock market –85%
> - Peak unemployment: 24% (1 in 4)
> - GDP fell 29% in 4 years
> - Fed *raised* rates after the crash → deflation

## Section: Finance and Economy 🔴

### Core Idea
The financial sector channels savings to investment and is conceptually distinct from the **real economy** (which produces goods and services using land, labour, and capital). The real economy determines an economy's long-run productive capacity, while the financial sector provides the mechanisms — markets, intermediaries, instruments — that let lenders and borrowers transact. Both feed each other: financial development supports growth, and growth deepens financial markets.

> **In Simple Terms:** The real economy bakes the cake (goods and services). The financial sector is the kitchen plumbing — moves ingredients (money) where they're needed, and if it leaks, baking stops.

### Key Concepts

#### Real Economy vs Financial Sector
| Aspect | Real Economy | Financial Sector |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| Inputs | Land, labour, capital | Funds, information |
| Output | Consumption + capital goods (food, clothes, plant, machinery) | Channels & mechanisms for fund flow |
| Long-run effect | Sets capacity to produce/consume | Allocates funds, enables transactions |
| Affects | Inflation, unemployment, income/wealth distribution | Productivity, capital allocation |

#### Why Savings Allocation Matters
Savings fund investment, but **every increase in savings is not good** — too much saving can shrink aggregate demand. The financial system matters because it directs savings to **productive** investment options (some are simply better than others in productivity, outcome, employment effect, etc.).

### Definitions
- **Real Economy**: Sector producing goods and services using land, labour, and capital — provides consumption goods (food, furniture, clothes) and capital goods (plant, machinery, transportation).
- **Financial Sector**: Provides mechanisms and channels through which lenders and borrowers coordinate, facilitating transactions in the real economy.

### Connections
- Builds on: Block 1 introduction of three financial-system elements (institutions, markets, instruments)
- Prerequisite for: every later unit in Block 7 — financial stability rests on this finance↔economy linkage

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Real economy = production of goods/services
> - Financial sector = channels for fund flow
> - Both ways: finance helps growth; growth deepens finance

## Section: Financial System — Direct vs Indirect Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
The financial system's main job is to move funds between borrowers and lenders (individuals, households, firms, governments, foreigners). Transactions happen through **organised/formal** markets or outside them; informal transactions are riskier and costlier. As a country develops, the financial market deepens and informal transactions decline. There are two routes: **direct finance** (lender → borrower without intermediary, via markets) and **indirect finance** (lender → intermediary → borrower).

> **In Simple Terms:** Direct finance = buying Reliance bonds yourself. Indirect finance = depositing in a bank and letting the bank decide who gets the loan.

### Key Concepts

#### Direct vs Indirect Finance
| Aspect | Direct Finance | Indirect Finance |
|--------|----------------|------------------|
| Channel | Financial markets (stocks, bonds) | Financial intermediaries (banks, mutual funds, insurance, pension) |
| Example | Reliance issues bonds / IPO; you buy them | You deposit in a bank, bank lends |
| Information needed by lender | High — must analyse borrower directly | Low — intermediary handles screening |
| Expertise needed | High | Low |

#### Components of the Financial System
- **Financial institutions**: banks, insurance companies, mutual funds, pension funds
- **Financial markets**: stock markets, bond markets

### Definitions
- **Direct Finance** ⭐: Lenders directly provide funds to borrowers, e.g., buying bonds in the bond market or stocks in an IPO. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Indirect Finance** ⭐: Lending through an intermediary (bank, mutual fund, insurance, pension) that receives money from lenders and lends it to borrowers. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial System**: Includes financial institutions (banks, insurance, mutual funds) and financial markets (stocks, bonds) — channels funds from savers to investors and reduces transaction costs and risks.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Direct = via markets (stocks/bonds)
> - Indirect = via intermediaries (banks/MFs/insurance/pensions)
> - Informal markets shrink as economy develops

## Section: Risks in Financial Markets 🔴

### Core Idea
Five major risks are present in financial transactions. Together they can cause the value of a financial intermediary's assets to fall below its liabilities, causing **insolvency**. A core function of intermediaries is to assess and manage these risks; **asymmetric information** is one of the chief reasons risks arise in the first place.

> **In Simple Terms:** Five things that can sink a bank: borrowers don't pay, depositors all rush in, rates jump, currencies crash, or hidden information bites.

### Key Concepts: Five Risks

| Risk | What it is |
|------|-----------|
| **Credit / default risk** | Deposits or credit given may not be paid back partially or fully |
| **Liquidity risk** | Intermediary unable to meet liquidity demands due to sudden, unexpected withdrawals or liability payments |
| **Interest rate & price risk** | Volatility in interest rates and asset prices deteriorates the financial health / balance sheets |
| **Exchange rate risk** | Volatility in the exchange rate affects value of assets/liabilities held in foreign currency, worsening balance sheets |
| **Asymmetric-information risk** | One party has less information — root cause of many other risks (see next section) |

### Definitions
- **Credit (Default) Risk** ⭐: Risk that deposits or credit given will not be paid back partially or fully. ⭐
- **Liquidity Risk** ⭐: Risk that a financial intermediary, including a bank, finds itself unable to meet liquidity demands due to sudden and unexpected increases in demand for liability payments or withdrawal of deposits. ⭐
- **Interest Rate & Price Risk** ⭐: Risk that volatility in interest rates and asset prices deteriorates financial health / balance sheets.
- **Exchange Rate Risk** ⭐: Risk that volatility in the exchange rate affects the value of assets and liabilities held in foreign currency, worsening balance sheets.
- **Insolvency**: When the value of assets of financial intermediaries (or firms / individuals) falls below the value of liabilities.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Five risks: credit, liquidity, interest-rate/price, exchange-rate, asymmetric-information
> - Multiple risks → assets < liabilities → insolvency
> - Intermediaries' core job: assess + manage these risks

## Section: Asymmetric Information, Adverse Selection, Moral Hazard 🔴

### Core Idea
**Asymmetric information** arises when one party in a transaction has more information than the other — typically the borrower knows more about the riskiness of the project than the lender. This produces two distinct problems: **adverse selection** (a *pre*-transaction screening problem — risky borrowers are most eager to borrow) and **moral hazard** (a *post*-transaction behaviour problem — once funds are received, borrowers may divert them or take on extra risk).

> **In Simple Terms:** Adverse selection is "the riskiest borrowers try hardest to get loans." Moral hazard is "after I get the loan, why be careful with someone else's money?"

### Key Concepts

#### Adverse Selection vs Moral Hazard

| Aspect | Adverse Selection | Moral Hazard |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|
| When it occurs | **Before** the transaction | **After** the transaction |
| Trigger | Hidden info about borrower / project type | Hidden actions after funds received |
| Mechanism | Risky borrower hides risk, may even offer higher interest, gets funded | Borrower diverts funds to risky activity, or skips precautions |
| Textbook example | Risky firm gets loan because it offered high interest, defaults later | Insured person skips fire-safety system to save costs; firm puts loan into risky bonds |

### Definitions
- **Asymmetric Information** ⭐: Situation where, in a transaction, one party does not have the same information as the other party. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Adverse Selection** ⭐: Problem arising *before* a financial transaction, where lenders/investors invest in a project without knowing its true risk because the borrower hid risk and pushed for funding (often offering a higher interest rate). ⭐
- **Moral Hazard** ⭐: Problem arising *after* a financial transaction (regardless of whether adverse selection occurred), where the borrower diverts borrowed funds to risky projects or behaves recklessly because the cost of failure is shifted to the lender. ⭐

### Examples
- **Risky firm hiding info**: A firm planning a risky project hides info from lenders and offers a higher interest rate; lenders invest unaware → adverse selection.
- **Diverted loan**: A firm uses borrowed funds to buy risky bonds. If the bonds pay → bank gets repaid. If not → firm defaults. → moral hazard.
- **Fire insurance**: Insured party doesn't install efficient fire-safety system to cut costs after taking insurance. → moral hazard.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Adverse selection and moral hazard are the same thing" → ✅ Correct: Adverse selection is **pre-transaction** (selection of who gets funded); moral hazard is **post-transaction** (behaviour after funding).
- ❌ Mistake: "Moral hazard requires adverse selection first" → ✅ Correct: Moral hazard can occur **whether or not** adverse selection occurred.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Asymmetric info = unequal knowledge between two parties
> - Adverse selection = BEFORE deal; risky borrowers self-select in
> - Moral hazard = AFTER deal; borrowers act recklessly with someone else's money

## Section: Case Study — Satyam Computers Scandal 🟡

### Core Idea
Satyam Computers (founded 1987) was India's 4th-biggest IT firm in the 2000s with 35% annual growth (2003–2008). In January 2009, the Chairman/founder confessed to manipulating accounts — inflating the balance sheet and showing fictitious profits and cash. The motive was cheaper loans, which were diverted to real estate. Auditors looked away. The company lost customers, stock prices crashed, investors lost billions, and Tech Mahindra acquired Satyam in April 2009. The scandal highlights asymmetric information producing both **adverse selection** (false info about the company) and **moral hazard** (behaviour of managers/auditors), and the role of **prudent regulation** in promoting transparency.

> **In Simple Terms:** Satyam faked its books to get cheap loans, hid the truth from investors, diverted money to real estate, and the auditors looked the other way — a real-world demo of asymmetric information, adverse selection, and moral hazard.

### Definitions
- **Prudent Regulation**: Regulation that decreases information asymmetry and promotes transparency in financial markets.

### Connections
- Illustrates: Asymmetric Information, Adverse Selection, Moral Hazard (Chunk 001: Block 7 Overview — Financial Stability)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Satyam = corporate fraud → fake balance sheet → cheap loans → diverted to real estate

## Section: Financial Crisis — Common Elements 🔴

### Core Idea
A financial crisis is an upset in financial markets — wide fluctuations in asset prices (stocks, real estate), disruptions in credit flow, and breakdown of intermediaries. Although crises differ in nature and magnitude, they share **four sequential elements** that typically begin with an asset-price boom: (1) asset-price boom and bust, (2) insolvency of financial institutions, (3) banking failure (bank runs), and (4) credit shortage and debt deflation. The sequence is a vicious circle: financial-sector deterioration → credit shortage → economic downturn → more insolvencies → further financial-sector deterioration.

> **In Simple Terms:** Crises follow a script — bubbles inflate, banks lose collateral value, depositors panic, lending dries up, and prices fall while debts stay the same.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Element 1 — Asset Price Booms and Busts:**
1. Investor optimism + easy credit availability → rising prices above fundamentals = **asset-price bubble**
2. Easy credit fuels investment → investment fuels prices → feedback loop = **credit boom / credit bubble**
3. Even a small shock → bust; investors withdraw → prices crash

**Element 2 — Insolvency of Financial Institutions:**
1. Banks lend against collateral / mortgages
2. Banks **leverage** (borrow from other institutions to lend more)
3. Asset-price bust → mortgage value falls → defaults → banks can't recover loans
4. Banks fall short of payment obligations → reduce lending → asset prices fall further
5. Assets < liabilities → insolvency

**Element 3 — Banking Failure / Bank Runs:**
1. Depositors fear losing money → line up to withdraw
2. Deposit insurance (where present) is small
3. Banks lack reserves → start selling assets
4. Asset prices already low + few buyers → further collapse = **fire sale**
5. Confidence collapses → multiple banks fail in a short duration = banking failure

**Element 4 — Credit Shortage and Debt Deflation:**
1. Bank failures → low confidence → harder to get new bank capital
2. Banks cut lending → SMEs especially can't get loans → consumer credit also drops
3. Credit shortage → fall in investment, operations, and consumption demand → recession
4. Prices fall → in real terms, debt rises = **debt deflation**
5. Falling prices and incomes + rising real debt → firm insolvencies → further financial-sector damage = vicious cycle

### Definitions
- **Financial Crisis** ⭐: An upset in the financial markets in the form of wide fluctuations in asset prices (e.g., stocks, real estate), associated with fluctuations in flow of credit and disruptions in the working of financial intermediaries and markets. ⭐
- **Asset-Price Bubble** ⭐: When prices of financial assets like stocks or real estate increase by a large amount over their fundamental values. ⭐
- **Credit Boom / Credit Bubble** ⭐: A rapid increase in credit availability that, fuelled by investor optimism about asset returns, intensifies an asset-price bubble.
- **Leveraging** ⭐: Banks borrow from other financial institutions to increase their lending capacity. The borrowed funds raise lending capacity but increase fragility when asset prices fall. ⭐
- **Fire Sale** ⭐: Rapid selling of assets by banks (or others) at depressed prices when asset markets are already weak — drives prices down further. ⭐
- **Bank Run / Banking Failure** ⭐: Depositors rush to withdraw money fearing bank failure; banks lack reserves; multiple banks fail in a short duration.
- **Debt Deflation** ⭐: With prices falling, in real terms the debt of firms increases — incomes fall but debt burden rises, leading to insolvency. ⭐
- **Financial Stability**: Conditions enabling better assessment and management of financial risk; promotes efficient allocation of resources and economic growth.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "All crises are basically the same" → ✅ Correct: Crises differ in nature and magnitude but share **four common elements** that typically unfold in sequence.
- ❌ Mistake: "Leveraging is just borrowing" → ✅ Correct: Leveraging specifically means **banks borrowing from other financial institutions** to increase lending capacity — it amplifies both gains and losses.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Crisis sequence: asset bubble → bank insolvency → bank runs → credit crunch + debt deflation
> - Speculation + asymmetric information are common triggers
> - Vicious cycle: financial-sector damage ↔ economic downturn

## Section: Case Study — The Great Recession (2007–2009) 🔴

### Core Idea
After the dot-com crisis, the US Fed cut rates to boost IT, but cheap money flowed instead into **real estate**. Soft loans, multiple loans on the same mortgage, and lending to those with poor credit history inflated a housing bubble. Banks packaged these mortgages into financial instruments (shares, bonds, futures) sold globally. When the real-estate bubble burst, defaults rose; mortgage-backed securities collapsed in value — a **double-edged sword** for banks. Lehman Brothers (4th largest US bank) collapsed in September 2008. Output fell 4.3%, housing prices fell 31.8%, unemployment rose from 5% (Dec 2007) to 10% (Oct 2009), public debt and protectionism rose. India's impact came through financial channels and reduced US/Europe demand for Indian exports.

> **In Simple Terms:** Cheap money intended for tech instead pumped a housing bubble; banks bundled bad mortgages into globally-sold securities; when the bubble popped, banks lost both on loans and on securities; Lehman fell, the world followed.

### Timeline & Key Numbers
| Indicator | Pre / Date | Post / Date |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Unemployment (US) | 5% (Dec 2007) | 10% (Oct 2009) |
| Output | — | –4.3% |
| Housing prices | — | –31.8% |
| Lehman Brothers | 4th-largest bank | Collapsed Sep 2008 |
| Public debt | — | Increased |
| Protectionism | — | Increased |

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Dot-com crisis → Fed cuts rates to boost IT
2. Cheap money flows into real estate (not IT)
3. Soft loans + multiple mortgages on same property + sub-prime lending → housing bubble
4. Banks securitise mortgages → instruments sold globally

## Section: Financial Crises and Economic Crises 🟡

### Core Idea
Financial and economic crises feed each other — neither necessarily causes the other, but each amplifies the other. A financial crisis worsens the economy (asset prices fall, interest rates rise, profitability shrinks, defaults rise, capital may fly abroad — hurting credit and exchange rate). An economic downturn can also trigger a financial crisis (lower sales and profits → loan defaults → bank balance-sheet damage).

> **In Simple Terms:** Finance and the real economy are joined at the hip. Hurt one, the other limps. Hurt both, you get a crisis amplifier.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Financial crisis → economic crisis:**
↓ asset prices + ↑ interest rates → ↓ firm profitability + credit crunch → ↓ overall economic activity → ↑ defaults & insolvencies → capital flight → ↓ credit + exchange-rate damage → trade disturbance → deeper recession

**Economic crisis → financial crisis:**
Economic downturn → ↓ sales & profitability → ↑ loan defaults → bank balance-sheet damage → financial crisis

### Connections
- Builds on: Financial Crisis — Common Elements (Chunk 001: Block 7 Overview — Financial Stability)
- Continues into: Chunk 002 — Policy Responses to a Crisis

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Two-way street: financial ↔ economic crises feed each other
> - Capital flight = double damage (credit crunch + FX hit)
> - A financial crisis isn't *required* to cause an economic crisis (and vice versa) — but each amplifies the other

## Section: Policy Responses to a Crisis 🔴
<!-- See chunk 001 for the crisis-types background that motivates this section -->

### Core Idea
A single policy intervention is rarely enough — combating a financial or economic crisis requires a **combination** of fiscal expansion, monetary expansion, and a strengthened regulatory framework. Each tool has limits: fiscal expansion can hurt interest rates and prices; monetary expansion fails at the zero lower bound and during a liquidity trap. Regulation must keep pace with continuous financial innovation. A recession with a financial crisis lasts longer, deepens output and employment losses, and stretches fiscal outlays (governments must rescue institutions *and* boost demand).

> **In Simple Terms:** Crisis-fighting needs three legs of a stool: cut taxes / spend more (fiscal), cut rates / pump liquidity (monetary), and tighten the rule-book (regulation). Lean on one leg only and the stool falls over.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Policy Tools

| Tool | Mechanism | Channels |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Fiscal Policy** | Tax cuts (↑ disposable income) or ↑ government spending — magnitude scales with severity of crisis | Infrastructure, industry stimulus, social infrastructure (health, education), social welfare schemes |
| **Monetary Policy** | ↓ interest rates (↓ borrowing cost), ↑ liquidity / money supply (eases credit crunch) | Open market operations, rate cuts |
| **Strengthening Regulatory Framework** | Continuous supervision and evaluation of financial institutions and instruments; reform pace must match financial-innovation pace | Direct curbs on asymmetric information, adverse selection, moral hazard |

#### Limits of Monetary and Fiscal Policy

| Tool | Limit |
|------|-------|
| Monetary policy | Interest rates **cannot fall below zero** (zero lower bound); ineffective in a **liquidity trap** |
| Fiscal policy | May hurt interest rates and prices (crowding out / inflation) |

When these limits bind, monetary and fiscal policy must be **supplemented** with regulations targeting asymmetric information, adverse selection, and moral hazard directly.

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Policy** ⭐: Used to increase output through government purchases and taxes. Expansionary fiscal policy raises aggregate demand via tax cuts or ↑ government spending. ⭐
- **Monetary Policy** ⭐: Increase or decrease in money supply by the central bank to target macroeconomic variables (unemployment, output, inflation). Expansionary monetary policy = ↓ rates + ↑ liquidity. ⭐
- **Liquidity Trap**: Condition in which monetary policy is ineffective — increases in money supply do not lower interest rates further (because rates are already very low).
- **Zero Lower Bound**: Floor below which nominal interest rates cannot fall.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Effective crisis response chain:**
Crisis (↑ unemployment, ↓ output, ↓ aggregate demand) → fiscal expansion (tax cut or ↑ G) + monetary expansion (↓ i, ↑ M) + regulatory tightening → ↑ aggregate demand → ↑ employment + output

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three tools: fiscal, monetary, regulatory — combine them
> - Monetary fails at the **zero lower bound** and during **liquidity traps**
> - Fiscal can crowd out and stoke inflation
> - Recessions with financial crises last longer and cost more

## Section: Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) — Introduction & History 🔴

### Core Idea
The Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) is a cornerstone idea in modern finance: financial markets are **informationally efficient** — asset prices fully and continuously reflect all available information. As a consequence, investors cannot consistently earn **abnormal returns** (returns higher than what the level of risk justifies) by trading on available information, because that information is already in the price. New information moves prices **quickly and precisely**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Stock prices already know what you know. By the time you read the news and place a trade, the price has moved.

### History
- Early 20th century — John Bates Clark and Alfred Marshall: theories on prices and information
- 1950s–1960s — Paul Samuelson, Burton Malkiel and others develop the modern theory
- **Eugene Fama (1965)** — "Random Walks in Stock Market Prices": argued stock prices follow a random walk; impossible to predict future prices from past prices or any available information
- **Eugene Fama (1970)** — formalised the **three forms** of EMH (weak, semi-strong, strong)
- Continues to be debated: some economists treat EMH as accurate; others believe markets are *not* fully efficient and that abnormal returns are achievable

### Definitions
- **Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH)** ⭐: Theory that financial markets are "informationally efficient" — asset prices reflect all available information at any given time, so investors cannot consistently earn abnormal returns by using available information. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Abnormal Returns** ⭐: Returns higher than what would be expected given the level of risk taken on. ⭐
- **Random Walk** ⭐: Stock prices evolve such that future prices cannot be predicted from past prices — Fama (1965). ⭐

## Section: Three Forms of EMH 🔴

### Core Idea
Eugene Fama (1970) classified market efficiency into **three forms**, each defined by what set of information is fully reflected in prices. Each stronger form swallows the previous: strong ⊃ semi-strong ⊃ weak.

> **In Simple Terms:** Weak says "past prices won't help you." Semi-strong says "even today's news won't help you." Strong says "even private inside information won't help you." Each is a higher claim than the last.

### Key Concepts: Three Forms

| Form | What is reflected in prices | What cannot generate consistent profits |
|------|------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Weak** | All historical price and volume data | Technical analysis (charts, past trends) cannot consistently profit |
| **Semi-Strong** | All publicly available information (price/volume + news, financial statements, economic data) | Fundamental analysis cannot consistently profit |
| **Strong** | All information — public **and private** | No analysis or even insider information can consistently earn abnormal returns |

### Definitions
- **Weak-Form EMH** ⭐: All historical price and volume data is already incorporated into current stock prices, making it impossible to profit from analysing past trends or patterns; technical analysis cannot generate consistent profits.
- **Semi-Strong-Form EMH** ⭐: All publicly available information is already reflected in stock prices; fundamental analysis cannot achieve abnormal returns.
- **Strong-Form EMH** ⭐: All information, both public and private, is reflected in prices; impossible to earn abnormal returns through any analysis or insider information.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Weak form means weak markets" → ✅ Correct: It means a *weaker* claim — only past price/volume data is in prices. Stronger forms make stronger claims.
- ❌ Mistake: "If strong form holds, weak form may not hold" → ✅ Correct: Strong form **subsumes** semi-strong, which **subsumes** weak.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Weak: past prices in price → no technical analysis
> - Semi-strong: public info in price → no fundamental analysis
> - Strong: public + private in price → not even insider info works
> - Strong ⊃ Semi-strong ⊃ Weak

## Section: Assumptions of EMH 🔴

### Core Idea
EMH rests on six tight assumptions about investors, information, and the market mechanism. Real-world deviations from these assumptions are precisely the sources of EMH critiques.

### Six Assumptions

| # | Assumption | Meaning |
|---|------------|---------|
| 1 | **Rationality** | Investors maximise their interests; they do their best |
| 2 | **Information freely available** | Every investor has easy access to all market-related information without restrictions, **at zero cost** |
| 3 | **No information asymmetry** | Every investor has the same information — whatever A has, B has too |
| 4 | **Prices adjust quickly to new information** | Market response time is very short; new info shows up in prices and indices very quickly |
| 5 | **No individual investor can influence prices** | No investor or group can control prices and steer the market |
| 6 | **No transaction costs** | No transaction costs or trading obstacles prevent investors from acting on information |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "EMH assumes some investors are smarter" → ✅ Correct: EMH assumes **all investors have the same information** (no asymmetry) and act rationally.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Six assumptions: rational, free info, no asymmetry, fast adjustment, no price-makers, no transaction costs
> - Each assumption is a target for empirical/theoretical criticism

## Section: EMH and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) 🔴

### Core Idea
CAPM (Sharpe 1964; Lintner 1965) is an asset-pricing model that builds **on the assumption that the market is efficient**. CAPM states that the expected return of an asset is **linearly and positively related to its systematic risk** (the part of total risk beyond firm/investor control — driven by economic, social, political factors). Because CAPM assumes EMH, CAPM also gives us a **way to test EMH empirically**: if CAPM holds well, markets are efficient; if not, markets may not be efficient.

> **In Simple Terms:** CAPM is a tidy formula for what return a stock should earn given its risk. It assumes EMH is true. So if CAPM works in the data, EMH probably works too.

### Key Equations

**CAPM equation (Sharpe–Lintner):**
$$E(R_i) = R_f + [E(R_m) - R_f]\beta_i \quad (25.1)$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $E(R_i)$ | Expected return of asset i |
| $R_f$ | Risk-free rate of return (e.g., T-bills, government securities) |
| $E(R_m)$ | Expected return of market portfolio (e.g., SENSEX, NIFTY index) |
| $\beta_i$ | Measure of systematic risk of asset i |
| $E(R_m) - R_f$ | **Market risk premium** — excess return of market over risk-free |

### CAPM Assumptions
1. Investors are rational
2. Investors diversify their portfolios across various assets/instruments
3. **The market is efficient**

So CAPM **assumes EMH holds**.

### Black–Jensen–Scholes (1972) Test for EMH using CAPM

For each security i:
$$R_{it} - R_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i (R_{mt} - R_{ft}) + u_{it} \quad (25.2)$$

Equivalently:
$$ER_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i \cdot ER_{mt} + u_{it} \quad (25.3)$$

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| $ER_{it} = R_{it} - R_{ft}$ | **Excess return** of security i over the risk-free return |
| $ER_{mt} = R_{mt} - R_{ft}$ | **Market risk premium** — excess return of market portfolio over risk-free return |
| $\alpha_i$ | Intercept |
| $\beta_i$ | Systematic risk |
| $u_{it}$ | Error term |

**Test logic:**
- **Null hypothesis**: $\alpha_i = 0$ for all n securities
- If $\alpha_i \neq 0$ statistically → market is **not efficient** (EMH rejected)
- If EMH holds, the **characteristic line** (graph of equation 25.3) should pass through the origin (Fig 25.2)

### Definitions
- **CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)** ⭐: Model stating that the expected return of an asset is linearly and positively related to its systematic risk. ⭐
- **Systematic Risk** ⭐: Part of total risk beyond the control of the firm or investor; determined by economic, social, and political factors. ⭐
- **Market Risk Premium** ⭐: $E(R_m) - R_f$ — excess return of the market portfolio over the risk-free return. ⭐
- **Excess Return**: Return on a security minus the risk-free rate.
- **Characteristic Line**: Graph of the regression of excess return on market risk premium; should pass through the origin under EMH.
- **Risk-Free Rate** ($R_f$): Return on a risk-free asset like Treasury bills or government securities.

### Connections
- Builds on: Three Forms of EMH (Chunk 002: Policy Responses to a Crisis), Unit 20 CAPM background (referenced as already-covered)
- Used by: Empirical EMH testing in Indian markets (Chunk 003: Assessment of EMH — Six Criticisms)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - CAPM: $E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i [E(R_m) - R_f]$
> - CAPM **assumes** EMH (market is efficient)
> - Black–Jensen–Scholes (1972) test: regress excess return on market risk premium; null is $\alpha_i = 0$
> - $\alpha_i \neq 0$ → reject EMH
> - Characteristic line passes through origin if EMH holds

## Section: Assessment of EMH — Six Criticisms 🔴

### Core Idea
EMH has dominated academic finance but has drawn six major criticisms. Each criticism points to a real-world deviation from one of EMH's tight assumptions: investors aren't always rational; price patterns persist; insiders profit; bubbles form; information is unequal; and arbitrage is limited. Taken together, these critiques suggest **market inefficiencies may be more common than EMH suggests**.

> **In Simple Terms:** EMH says markets are flawless calculators of information. Critics list six everyday cases where the calculator misfires — emotions, momentum, insider edges, bubbles, hidden info, and friction.

### Six Criticisms

| # | Criticism | Source assumption violated |
|---|-----------|----------------------------|
| **a** | **Behavioural finance** — investors are not always rational; emotions, biases, and incomplete information drive decisions, causing inefficiencies | Rationality |
| **b** | **Historical contradictions** — anomalies like the **momentum effect** (past winners keep winning) contradict EMH's claim that past prices can't predict future prices | Random walk / weak-form |
| **c** | **Insider trading** — its existence shows some participants have information that the public doesn't | No information asymmetry / strong-form |
| **d** | **Market bubbles** — EMH says prices = intrinsic value, but the **dot-com bubble (late 1990s)** and **housing bubble (mid-2000s)** sent prices far above intrinsic values until they crashed | Prices reflect intrinsic value |
| **e** | **Information asymmetry** — in reality some investors have more or better information; this creates inefficiencies | No information asymmetry |
| **f** | **Limited arbitrage** — EMH expects arbitrage to correct mispricings, but real arbitrage is limited by transaction costs, liquidity constraints, and regulatory restrictions | No transaction costs |

### Definitions
- **Behavioural Finance** ⭐: Field studying how investors are not always rational; their decisions are derived from emotions, biases, or incomplete information, causing inefficiencies. ⭐
- **Momentum Effect** ⭐: Tendency of stocks that have performed well in the past to continue to perform well — contradicts EMH (specifically, weak-form). ⭐
- **Limited Arbitrage**: Real-world constraint where transaction costs, liquidity, and regulatory restrictions prevent arbitrage from fully correcting market inefficiencies.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "EMH says bubbles cannot occur" → ✅ Correct: EMH says prices = intrinsic value; observed bubbles like dot-com (late 1990s) and housing (mid-2000s) **contradict** EMH.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 6 criticisms: behavioural, momentum, insider, bubbles, asymmetry, limited arbitrage
> - Bubbles cited: **dot-com (late 1990s)** and **housing (mid-2000s)**
> - Each criticism maps to violation of an EMH assumption

## Section: Applications of EMH 🟡

### Core Idea
EMH influences six practical domains, mainly nudging investors toward **passive strategies**. If you can't beat the market, ride it — invest in index funds, hold long-term, diversify. EMH also shapes academic research and dilutes the case for some kinds of regulation.

### Six Applications

| # | Application | Implication |
|---|-------------|-------------|
| **a** | **Index Funds** | Cannot consistently outperform the market by stock-picking → invest in index funds tracking the overall market |

| **c** | **Rational Expectations Theory** | Closely related — individuals make decisions based on all available information; markets adjust rapidly to new info |
| **d** | **Academic Research** | EMH is a benchmark framework for testing hypotheses about market behaviour |

| **f** | **Investment Strategies** | Technical and fundamental analysis may be ineffective; focus on low-cost index funds, diversification, risk management |

### Definitions
- **Passive Investing** ⭐: Strategy that aims to track a market index (Sensex, Nifty) rather than try to beat it. Buy-and-hold a diversified portfolio for the long term.
- **Index Fund**: Mutual fund or ETF that tracks the performance of a market index.
- **Rational Expectations Theory**: Theory that individuals make decisions based on all available information and markets adjust rapidly to new information — closely related to EMH.

## Section: Applicability of EMH in India — Empirical Studies 🔴

### Core Idea
Indian-market evidence is **mixed**: weak-form efficiency is rejected by some studies; semi-strong efficiency is partial; strong-form is rejected because insiders earn abnormal profits. During the 2008 global financial crisis the market behaved largely efficiently with some anomalies. Overall the Indian market is "largely efficient" but with exploitable anomalies — a partial-EMH picture.

> **In Simple Terms:** Indian markets are mostly but not completely efficient. Past prices, news, and insider info can sometimes still produce abnormal returns.

### Indian Empirical Studies

| Study | Form tested | Period | Finding |
|-------|-------------|--------|---------|
| **Bhatia, Gupta, Goyal (2017)** | Weak-form | 2006–2016 daily returns | Indian stock market is **NOT weak-form efficient** — predictable patterns exist that can be exploited for abnormal profits |
| **Kumar & Kamaiah (2017)** | Semi-strong-form | Event-study methodology | Indian stock market is **partially efficient** in reacting to news/events; some room for abnormal profits |
| **Patro, Paudyal & Kallapur (2009)** | Strong-form | Insider-trading activity | Insiders generate abnormal profits → market is **NOT strong-form efficient** |
| **Gupta & Sehgal (2013)** | Efficiency during 2008 GFC | Crisis period | Market was **largely efficient** during crisis, with some anomalies |
| **Bhattacharya & Mukherjee (2017)** | Combined technical + fundamental | 2006–2016 | Market **largely efficient** with some exploitable anomalies |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Indian markets are fully efficient" → ✅ Correct: Empirical studies show Indian markets are **only partially efficient** — weak-form is rejected by Bhatia et al. (2017), and strong-form is rejected by Patro et al. (2009).
- ❌ Mistake: "All Indian studies say markets are inefficient" → ✅ Correct: Some studies (Gupta & Sehgal 2013; Bhattacharya & Mukherjee 2017) report **largely efficient** markets with limited anomalies.

## Section: Issues in Financial Stability 🔴

### Core Idea
Five major **issues** can arise when policymakers try to maintain financial stability — different from "factors" in that they are systemic problems that *threaten* stability rather than just driving it. The five are: systemic risk, shadow banking, cyber-security, international spillovers, and too-big-to-fail.

> **In Simple Terms:** Five things that keep regulators up at night: cascade failures (systemic), unregulated lenders (shadow banking), hackers (cyber), foreign contagion (spillovers), and "rescue-me-no-matter-what" mega-banks (too-big-to-fail).

### Five Issues

| # | Issue | What it is | How it threatens stability |
|---|-------|------------|----------------------------|
| **a** | **Systemic risk** | Risk imposing a barrier in the efficient financial system that can severely impact the entire economy | Contagion from one institution's failure → cascade through markets → collapse, loss of confidence |
| **b** | **Shadow banking** | Credit and financial services provided by **unregulated** entities (unlike banks) | Risky lending, no accountability, no review mechanism → builds systemic risk |
| **c** | **Cyber-security** | Cyber attacks on financial institutions, payment systems | Disrupts fund flow, exposes sensitive info, enables money laundering / unauthorised transactions → loss of public confidence |
| **d** | **International spill-ins and spill-outs** | Cross-border financial-shock spread via market interconnections | Loss of confidence, currency depreciation, asset-price volatility, increased systemic-risk threat |
| **e** | **Too-big-to-fail** | Some institutions are so large their failure would catastrophically hit the economy | Government/RBI guarantee → moral hazard (excessive risk-taking by these institutions); erodes public confidence (taxes used to bail out big banks) |

### Definitions
- **Systemic Risk** ⭐: Risk that imposes a barrier in the efficient financial system, with severe impact on the entire economy. Sources: interconnections and interdependence between institutions/markets, and contagion effects from the failure of one institution. Refers to risk inherent to the entire market or market segment due to certain events (natural disasters, inflation, war). ⭐
- **Shadow Banking** ⭐: System where credit and related financial services are provided by entities that are **not regulated**, unlike banks. ⭐
- **Cyber-Security Threat**: Cyber attacks (illegal access, fraudulent transactions, money laundering) on banks, NBFCs, investment firms, payment systems.
- **International Spill-ins / Spill-outs**: Cross-border financial-shock transmission via interconnected markets — disruption in one country quickly affects others.
- **Too-Big-To-Fail (TBTF)** ⭐: Some institutions are so large that their failure would catastrophically harm the economy; their implicit guarantee creates moral hazard. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Shadow banking is illegal banking" → ✅ Correct: Shadow banking is **unregulated** (not necessarily illegal) — entities like some NBFCs that provide bank-like services without bank-level regulation.
- ❌ Mistake: "Too-big-to-fail makes banks safer" → ✅ Correct: TBTF creates **moral hazard** — these banks take **excessive risks** because they expect to be bailed out.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 issues: systemic risk, shadow banking, cyber-security, international spillovers, too-big-to-fail
> - TBTF → moral hazard
> - Shadow banking = unregulated → systemic risk

| **g** | **Interest rate risk** | Changes in interest rates affecting financial-instrument value and FI profitability | Stress testing, interest-rate risk-management framework |
| **h** | **Political risk** | Political events (policy changes, geopolitical tensions) damaging investor confidence | Diversified geographic exposure; risk-management framework |

### Definitions
- **Credit Risk** (Unit 26 keyword): Probability of a financial loss resulting from a borrower's failure to repay a loan.
- **Liquidity Risk** (Unit 26 keyword): Risk that a company or individual will not have enough cash to meet financial obligations on time.
- **Foreign Exchange Risk** (Unit 26 keyword): Risk of financial impact due to exchange-rate fluctuations.
- **Market Risk** (Unit 26 keyword) ⭐: Possibility that an individual or entity will experience losses due to factors affecting overall investment performance in financial markets.
- **Operational Risk** (Unit 26 keyword): Uncertainties and hazards a company faces in day-to-day business activities.
- **Political Risk** ⭐: Risk arising from political events or actions like changes in government policy or geopolitical tensions; damages investor confidence.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 8 risks: cyber, systemic, credit, market, operational, liquidity, interest-rate, political
> - Each risk has a corresponding control toolkit
> - Macro-prudential policy is the systemic-risk antidote

## Section: Stability Measures for Ensuring Financial Stability 🔴

### Core Idea
Five measures help achieve financial stability. They span regulation (robust framework), prevention (macro-prudential), supervision (monitoring), response (crisis-management framework), and external coordination (global cooperation).

### Five Measures

| # | Measure | What it does |
|---|---------|--------------|
| **a** | **Robust regulatory framework** | Promotes fairness, transparency, accountability, risk management → sound financial system |
| **b** | **Macro-prudential policies** | Prevent systemic risk via counter-cyclical capital buffers, reserve requirements, loan-to-value ratios |
| **c** | **Supervision and monitoring** | Regular checks on FI compliance and financial health |
| **d** | **Crisis management framework** | Set of policies/procedures to manage and respond to a crisis |
| **e** | **Global cooperation** | Coordination among global regulators, central banks, organisations — share information, coordinate policy |

### Definitions
- **Macro-prudential Policy** ⭐: Policies that prevent systemic risk; includes counter-cyclical capital buffers, reserve requirements, loan-to-value ratios. ⭐
- **Micro-prudential Norms**: Regulation focused on stability of **individual** banking institutions and protection of their customers, rather than the economy as a whole.
- **Crisis Management Framework**: Set of policies and procedures for managing and responding to a crisis.
- **Counter-Cyclical Capital Buffer**: Macro-prudential tool — additional capital requirement in good times, released in bad times.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Macro-prudential and micro-prudential policies are the same" → ✅ Correct: **Macro-prudential** focuses on system-wide stability; **micro-prudential** focuses on individual institutions and their customers.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 measures: regulation, macro-prudential, supervision, crisis-management, global cooperation
> - Macro-prudential targets system-wide systemic risk
> - Micro-prudential targets individual institutions

## Section: Unit 27 Introduction — What are NPAs? 🔴

### Core Idea
A **non-performing asset (NPA)** is a loan or advance in default or arrears — when the principal is due, or interest payments are late or missed. Banks' main role is lending; NPAs reflect **asset quality, credit risk, and resource allocation efficiency**. The level of NPAs creates serious concern for banking-system stability. NPAs can be manageable in the short term, but accumulating NPAs threaten resilience and growth. India's NPA accumulation cannot be attributed to a single event or timeline — it spans both small and major banks.

> **In Simple Terms:** A loan a bank has effectively lost or fears losing. Too many of these and the bank's profits, reputation, and ability to lend all suffer.

### Definitions
- **Non-Performing Asset (NPA)** ⭐: Loan or advance that is in default or in arrears. A loan is in arrears when the principal is due or interest payments are late or missed. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Performing Asset / Standard Asset** ⭐: Asset generating regular income with the expected usual commercial risk; standard assets do not require supplementary provisions.
- **Loan in Default**: When the lender considers the loan agreement broken and the debtor unable to meet obligations.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - NPA = loan in default / arrears
> - Reflects asset quality, credit risk, allocation efficiency
> - 90 days non-repayment is the standard NPA classification trigger

## Section: Profile of NPAs in India — Gross vs Net NPAs 🔴

### Core Idea
For measurement, NPAs are classified as **Gross NPAs** (absolute total of NPAs) and **Net NPAs** (Gross NPAs minus specific provisions). Gross NPAs show the total irrecoverable amount; Net NPAs show the **actual burden** on the bank after provisioning.

### Definitions
- **Gross NPAs** ⭐: Indicates the absolute amount of the total value of loans that are due within 90 days in a particular quarter or financial year.
- **Net NPAs** ⭐: Net value of NPAs after the bank makes specific provisions for them. Arrived at by subtracting doubtful and unpaid assets (provisions) from gross NPAs.
- **Gross NPA Ratio** ⭐: Gross NPAs / Gross Advances.

### Key Formula
$$\text{Net NPAs} = \text{Gross NPAs} - \text{Gross Advances} - \text{Provisions}$$
*(as stated in source — note the unusual subtraction of Gross Advances; standard textbook formula is Gross NPAs − Provisions)*

$$\text{Gross NPA Ratio} = \frac{\text{Gross NPAs}}{\text{Gross Advances}}$$

### Direct vs Net NPAs
| Metric | Meaning | What it shows |
|--------|---------|---------------|
| Gross NPAs | Absolute amount considered irrecoverable | Total NPA value for a particular financial year |
| Net NPAs | After provisioning | Actual burden on the bank |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Gross NPAs = absolute irrecoverable amount
> - Net NPAs = Gross − provisions (true burden)
> - Gross NPA Ratio = Gross NPAs / Gross Advances

## Section: Nature of NPAs — Three Categories by Period of Default 🔴

### Core Idea
RBI categorises NPAs into three classes based on **period of default**: sub-standard, doubtful, and loss assets. Banks must classify each NPA into one category. Beyond term loans, NPAs also include over-due overdraft / cash-credit accounts, agricultural loans by crop-season rules, and any account with payments overdue 90+ days.

### Three Categories

| Category | Period of default | Description |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Sub-Standard Asset** ⭐ | Outstanding ≤ 12 months as NPA | First-stage NPA |
| **Doubtful Asset** ⭐ | Outstanding > 12 months as NPA, already classified sub-standard | Higher-risk NPA |
| **Loss Asset** ⭐ | Uncollectible / little to no recovery; bank may consider some recovery value left as it has not been written off | Bank must record loss; entire loan written off |

### Other Forms of NPAs (beyond term loans)
1. **Overdraft / Cash-Credit (OD/CC) accounts** out of order for more than **90 days**
2. **Agricultural loans**:
   - Short-period crops: NPA if instalment of principal/interest unpaid for **two crop seasons**
   - Long-period crops: NPA if instalment of principal/interest unpaid for **one crop season**
3. **Any other account** with payment overdue more than 90 days

### Mechanisms / Processes
**NPA classification trigger:**
1. Loan becomes NPA after **90 days of non-repayment**
2. Bank asks borrower to liquidate assets / mortgage as per loan agreement
3. If no assets pledged → bank may write off as bad debt and **sell to a "bad bank"** (e.g., **NARCL — National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited**)
4. NARCL is a public-sector asset-reconstruction company in India established to acquire and aggregate NPAs

### Definitions
- **Sub-Standard Asset** ⭐: Loan/advance that has remained NPA for ≤ 12 months.
- **Doubtful Asset** ⭐: Loan/advance that has been NPA for > 12 months and was previously classified sub-standard.
- **Loss Asset** ⭐: Loan/advance that is uncollectible or has little/no recovery value, but has not yet been wholly written off — must record loss; entire amount must be written off completely.
- **NARCL (National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited)** ⭐: Public-sector asset-reconstruction company in India established to acquire and aggregate NPAs ("bad bank"). ⭐
- **Bad Bank**: Specialised institution that takes bad loans off bank balance sheets to ease the burden.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Sub-standard ≤ 12 months
> - Doubtful > 12 months (was sub-standard)
> - Loss = uncollectible
> - 90-day rule for general NPAs; 1–2 crop seasons for agriculture
> - **NARCL** = India's "bad bank"

## Section: Magnitude and Trend of NPAs in India 🔴

### Core Idea
NPAs grew sharply in the mid-2000s during the 2006–2008 boom (over-optimism, infrastructure pipeline). The Gross NPA ratio of scheduled commercial banks peaked above **11% in March 2018** (over **14% for public-sector banks**), then declined steadily: 8.2% (Mar 2020), 7.3% (Mar 2021), down to **2.8% in March 2024** (per RBI's FSR). Public-sector banks dominate NPAs (75.75% of total net NPAs in March 2022). The decline partly reflects use of write-offs (which erode the capital base) and pandemic-era moratoria.

| Total net NPAs, March 31, 2022 | ₹2,04,225.90 crore |

### Trend Drivers
- Mid-2000s boom + over-optimism (2006–2008)
- Easy credit during high-growth period
- 2017–18: huge spike in NPAs, then decline

- NPAs in agriculture / priority sector are **comparatively lower** than corporate houses
- PSB NPAs > Private-sector-bank NPAs

### Definitions
- **Twin Balance Sheet (TBS) Problem** ⭐: Situation in which the **borrower (corporate sector)** is under stress due to high debt and the **lender (banks)** is under stress due to non-repayment of loans (NPAs). Both balance sheets — borrower's and lender's — have problems. ⭐
- **Early Warning System (EWS)** ⭐: Software-based monitoring system used by banks to track customer portfolios regularly and identify signs of distress in borrower asset quality (signs not catchable manually). Procedure varies bank to bank. ⭐
- **"Dirty Dozen"**: Slang for India's top industrialists who account for a major portion of NPAs.

### Causes of the Decline (Chandrasekhar, 2022)
- **Write-offs** (erode banks' capital base)
- **Time-bound moratorium on debt repayments** during the pandemic for small units / selected borrowers
- Government-guaranteed lending to MSMEs

> **Quick Recall:**

> - PSBs hold ~76% of net NPAs (Mar 2022)
> - Twin Balance Sheet (TBS) = stressed borrower + stressed lender
> - EWS = software-based early-distress detection
> - Decline driven partly by write-offs + pandemic moratorium

## Section: Major Causes of NPAs — Bank-Specific & Macro 🔴

### Core Idea
Causes of NPAs split into **bank-specific factors** (six) and **macroeconomic factors** (three). ### Core Idea
RBI has progressively tightened NPA-resolution rules. ### Key Initiatives Timeline

| Year | Initiative | Purpose |

## Section: Impact of NPAs 🔴
<!-- Continues in chunk 006 — final impact discussion + measures + recent policy -->

### Core Idea
NPAs trigger a **vicious cycle** affecting bank profitability, credit creation, investor/depositor confidence, and macroeconomic growth. They increase operating costs (recovery), hurt interest earnings, raise lending rates (which feeds back to less credit), erode reputation, and amplify risk aversion. NPA-driven write-offs erode banks' capital base, partly offset by government recapitalisation of PSBs. The Twin Balance Sheet crisis significantly contributed to India's late-2010s growth deceleration.

### Channels of Impact

| Channel | Effect |
|---------|--------|
| **Profitability** | NPAs negatively affect ability to generate income; ↓ Return on Assets, ↓ Return on Equity |
| **Income vs Profit** | Positive relation between gross NPAs and total income; **negative** relation between gross NPAs and total profit |
| **Lending rates** | Banks raise lending rates to maintain margins → ↓ borrowing for infrastructure / industrial projects → ↓ credit creation → ↓ revenue |
| **Confidence** | Investors, lenders, depositors lose confidence; reputation harm |
| **Risk-aversion** | Banks become risk-averse → hesitate to extend credit |
| **Capital base** | Write-offs erode capital base — PSB recapitalisation only partially neutralises |
| **Macro growth** | Twin Balance Sheet crisis significantly contributed to late-2010s growth deceleration |

### Mechanisms / Processes
**NPA Vicious Cycle:**
↑ NPAs → ↓ interest earnings + ↑ recovery costs → ↓ profitability → ↑ lending rates → ↓ credit demand and creation → ↓ economic activity → ↑ corporate stress → ↑ defaults → ↑ NPAs

### Definitions
- **Twin Balance Sheet Crisis Effect**: NPAs combined with non-financial-firm balance-sheet deterioration significantly contributed to the deceleration of growth in the late 2010s.

- Builds on: Twin Balance Sheet (this chunk), Vicious-cycle pattern (Chunk 001: Block 7 Overview — Financial Stability)
- Continues into: Measures, Effectiveness, Recent Policy (Chunk 006: Measures to Tackle NPAs)

## Section: Measures to Tackle NPAs 🔴

### Core Idea
Until the mid-1980s, NPA management was left to banks and auditors. Reforms came in waves: **Narasimhan Committee on the Financial System (1991)**, prudential norms (1992), **Narasimhan Committee on Banking Sector Reforms (1998)**, **90-day NPA classification norm (2001)**, **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016)**, **NARCL + IDRCL "bad bank"** mechanism, **Mission Indradhanush (2015)**, and the **2021 SARFAESI amendment** strengthening bank rights over forfeited assets. Banks also use **provisioning** (setting aside profits in anticipation of bad assets), with norms differing for Tier-I vs Tier-II banks.

> **In Simple Terms:** Decades of layered reforms — classify loans by risk, set aside provisions, build legal tools (IBC, SARFAESI), create a "bad bank" (NARCL), merge weak PSBs, and modernise scrutiny.

### Reform Timeline

| Year | Reform | Purpose |
|------|--------|---------|
| **1991** | **Narasimhan Committee on the Financial System** | Suggested classifying advances into 4 groups: standard, sub-standard, doubtful, loss assets |
| **1992** | Prudential norms introduced (income recognition, asset classification, provisioning) | Phased introduction following Narasimhan I |
| **1993** | Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act | Statutory recovery tool |
| **1998** | **Narasimhan Committee on Banking Sector Reforms** | Tightening of prudential standards on par with international best practices |
| **2001** | **90-day NPA classification norm** | Brought NPA guidelines to international standards |
| **2002** | **SARFAESI Act** (Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act) | Statutory tool for asset recovery |
| **2015** | **Mission Indradhanush** | Make PSB working more transparent and professional |
| **2015** | Strategic Debt Restructuring Scheme | Empowered banks to take over distressed companies |
| **2016** | **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC)** | Resolves > 50% of NPA matters; sets max 180 days (extendable by 90 days); NCLT operationalised |

## Section: Exchange Rate — Direct vs Indirect Quotes 🔴

### Core Idea
An **exchange rate** is the price of one country's currency in terms of another. Exchange rates can be reported in two ways:
- **Direct quote**: how many units of *domestic* currency one unit of *foreign* currency buys
- **Indirect quote**: how many units of *foreign* currency one unit of *domestic* currency buys

Appreciation of the dollar against the rupee → one dollar buys *more* rupees. Depreciation → one dollar buys *fewer* rupees.

### Definitions
- **Exchange Rate** ⭐: Price of the currency of a country (e.g., Indian rupee) in terms of the currency of another country (e.g., US dollar).
- **Direct Quote** ⭐: How many units of domestic currency can be bought by **one unit of foreign currency**. Example: $1 = ₹83.17. ⭐
- **Indirect Quote** ⭐: How many units of foreign currency can be bought by **one unit of domestic currency**. Example: ₹1 = $0.0120. ⭐
- **Currency Appreciation**: Domestic currency rises in value — one foreign currency unit buys *fewer* domestic currency units.
- **Currency Depreciation**: Domestic currency falls in value — one foreign currency unit buys *more* domestic currency units.

### Examples
- $1 = ₹83.17 → direct quote (rupees per dollar)
- ₹1 = $0.0120 → indirect quote (dollars per rupee)
- "Dollar appreciates against rupee" → $1 buys more ₹

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Direct and indirect quotes are interchangeable terminology" → ✅ Correct: They are **reciprocals** but follow specific conventions — direct = domestic currency per foreign unit; indirect = foreign currency per domestic unit.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Direct: ₹/$ (how much ₹ for $1)
> - Indirect: $/₹ (how much $ for ₹1)
> - Dollar appreciates → $1 buys more rupees

## Section: Exchange Rate Systems — Fixed vs Floating 🔴

### Core Idea
Two pure exchange-rate systems: **fixed** (peg by central bank) and **floating** (set by demand and supply). Each has trade-offs: fixed gives stability but is hard to maintain long-term; floating gives flexibility but causes volatility.

### Fixed Exchange Rate System (Fig 28.1)

**Setup:**
- Central bank (e.g., RBI) sets the **official exchange rate**
- Central bank ensures the actual rate stays at the official level

**Mechanism — RBI wants ₹50/$ but market equilibrium is ₹60/$:**
1. Equilibrium at E1: D ∩ S1 = ₹60/$
2. To bring rate down to ₹50/$, RBI **sells dollars** equal to Q1Q2 → supply shifts S1 → S2
3. New equilibrium E2 at ₹50/$ on D ∩ S2
4. **Arbitrage process**: arbitrageur buys $1 from RBI at ₹50, sells in market at ₹60 → profit ₹10/$
5. Total arbitrageur profit: ₹10 × Q1Q2
6. **Result**: RBI's foreign-exchange reserves **fall** by Q1Q2 dollars
7. Reverse case: if equilibrium rate < desired, RBI buys dollars → reserves **rise**

**Key implication:**
- When the foreign currency (dollar) is **overvalued** in the market (above target), maintaining the fixed rate **drains** FX reserves
- When undervalued, central bank **accumulates** reserves

### Floating Exchange Rate System (Fig 28.2)

**Setup:**
- Equilibrium exchange rate determined by **demand for** and **supply of** foreign currency
- Equilibrium when demand = supply

**Mechanism (worked through demand/supply shifts):**
1. **Initial**: D1 ∩ S1 at E1 → equilibrium rate e1
2. **↑ Supply**: S1 → S2 with constant D1 → new equilibrium E2 at rate e2 (e2 < e1, **rate falls**)
3. **↑ Demand**: D1 → D2 with constant S1 → new equilibrium E3 at rate e3 (e3 > e1, **rate rises**)

**Conclusions:**
- ↑ supply (constant demand) → ↓ rate
- ↑ demand (constant supply) → ↑ rate
- Both can change simultaneously → rate fluctuates

### Key Concepts: Fixed vs Floating

| Aspect | Fixed | Floating |
|--------|-------|----------|
| Rate determined by | Central bank (official rate) | Market forces (demand & supply) |
| Stability | High — predictable | Low — volatile |
| Long-run sustainability | Difficult | Easier |
| Reserve impact | Drains reserves when defending overvalued foreign currency | None directly |
| Example mechanism | RBI sells/buys dollars to maintain ₹50/$ | E1 → E2 → E3 in response to demand/supply shifts |

### Definitions
- **Fixed Exchange Rate System** ⭐: Currency pegged to another currency or fixed value (e.g., gold); central bank maintains the rate; the rate is the **official exchange rate**. ⭐
- **Floating Exchange Rate System** ⭐: Exchange rate determined by market forces — demand for and supply of the foreign currency; equilibrium where demand = supply. ⭐
- **Equilibrium Exchange Rate** ⭐: Rate at which demand for foreign currency equals supply.
- **Arbitrage** ⭐: Buying a currency in one market and selling in another to profit from price differences.
- **Arbitrageur**: Person executing arbitrage trades.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "RBI prints rupees to defend a fixed rate" → ✅ Correct: RBI **sells (or buys) foreign-exchange reserves** (dollars) to defend the fixed rate; this **drains (or builds) reserves**.
- ❌ Mistake: "Floating rate means no central-bank involvement" → ✅ Correct: Pure floating has none, but most countries practise managed/dirty floating (next chunk).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Fixed: official rate set by central bank; RBI sells dollars to keep rate down → reserves fall
> - Floating: market-driven equilibrium; ↑D → ↑rate; ↑S → ↓rate
> - Arbitrage profit example: buy $1 at ₹50, sell at ₹60 → ₹10/$ profit

## Section: Issues in FX Stability 🔴
<!-- Continues in chunk 008 — Trade Balance algebra continues, then measures + summary -->

### Core Idea
Five major issues affect FX stability: **managed/dirty floating** (most prevalent regime worldwide), **exchange-rate policy** (strong vs weak currency policy), **parallel FX systems** (legal dual or illicit markets in developing countries), **political instability** (devalues currency), and **trade balances** (linked to net exports). The first four are introduced here; trade-balances algebra continues into the next chunk.

### Five Issues

#### Issue 1: Managed or Dirty Floating

A regime where the rate is **neither fully fixed nor completely free**. Central-bank intervention keeps the currency within a range relative to another currency or basket. Some countries are accused of currency manipulation for trade advantage, leading to international tensions / disputes. Per IMF (2014), **82 countries (43% of economies) used managed float** — the most prevalent exchange-rate regime.

#### Issue 2: Exchange Rate Policy

A country's exchange-rate policy reflects economic goals. Two stylised stances:
- **Strong currency policy** → control inflation
- **Weak currency policy** → boost exports

**Example — India in the 1990s:**
- 39% of government revenue went to interest payments
- Fiscal deficit cut to 7.8% of GDP
- India faced risk of being branded a global defaulter
- **Devaluation of the Indian rupee** was necessary
- Effect: ↑ cost of imports + ↓ price of exports

#### Issue 3: Parallel Foreign Exchange Systems

Common in developing countries — combine a **market-driven rate for financial transactions** alongside one or more **fixed rates**. Two scenarios:

| Scenario | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| (a) Government establishes legal parallel/dual FX market | For financial transactions; mitigates immediate impact of depreciation on domestic prices while retaining capital-outflow control |
| (b) Strict FX controls limit official-market access | Illicit parallel market emerges; tighter controls → more illicit trade |

**Examples:**
- **Burundi**: parallel market trades at nearly **60% premium** over the official market
- **South Asia** (1993–2005): **informal trade was 50% of formal trade** — large parallel-market footprint

#### Issue 4: Political Instability

Politics matter: stability raises currency demand; unrest depresses it.
- **Switzerland** — political stability + neutrality + non-confrontation → Swiss franc is a **safe haven** for FX trading; offsets currency risk
- Political unrest, coups, protests → currency devalues; less demand; more risk

#### Issue 5: Trade Balances (introduced — algebra continues in chunk 008)

National income identity in a closed economy:
$$Y = C^d + I^d + G^d + X$$

Where:
- $Y$ = National output
- $C^d$ = Consumption of domestically made goods/services
- $I^d$ = Investment in domestically made goods/services
- $G^d$ = Government expenditure on domestically made goods/services
- $X$ = Exports of domestically made goods/services

But in an **open economy** (globalisation), people consume/invest/government-spends on **both** domestic and foreign goods:
- $C = C^d + C^F$
- $I = I^d + I^F$
- $G = G^d + G^F$

Where the superscript $F$ denotes foreign-goods consumption / investment / government expenditure.

Re-writing the national-income identity:
$$Y = (C - C^F) + (I - I^F) + (G - G^F) + X$$
$$Y = C + I + G + X - (C^F + I^F + G^F)$$

Since $C^F + I^F + G^F$ = total imports (IM):
$$Y = C + I + G + X - IM$$

**Net Exports (NX) = X − IM = Trade Balance**

So the open-economy national-income identity is:
$$Y = C + I + G + NX$$
$$NX = Y - (C + I + G)$$

Therefore:
$$\textbf{Net exports} = \textbf{National output} - \textbf{Domestic spending}$$

### Definitions
- **Managed Float / Dirty Float** ⭐: Exchange-rate regime where the rate is neither fully fixed nor completely free; central bank intervenes to keep currency within a range. Most prevalent regime per IMF 2014 — 82 countries / 43% of economies. ⭐
- **Devaluation**: Country lowering the value of its currency on the global market while maintaining intrinsic value. Associated with fixed/managed regimes.
- **Parallel FX System** ⭐: Combination of a market-driven rate (financial transactions) alongside one or more fixed rates; common in developing countries; can be legal (dual market) or illicit. ⭐
- **Trade Balance / Net Exports (NX)** ⭐: NX = X − IM. Difference between exports and imports. ⭐
- **National Income Identity (Open Economy)** ⭐: Y = C + I + G + NX. ⭐

## Section: Real Interest Rate, Saving and Investment in a Small Open Economy 🔴
<!-- See chunk 007 for start of Trade Balance algebra -->

### Core Idea
Continuing from chunk 007, the open-economy identity NX = Y − (C + I + G) collapses, in the **small open economy** assumption, to **NX = S̄ − I(r*)**: net exports equal national saving minus investment evaluated at the **world** real interest rate. With Y fixed at full employment and C a function of disposable income (also fixed), saving S becomes a constant S̄. Investment is a downward-sloping function of the real interest rate. Whether the country runs a trade surplus, deficit, or balance is determined by the gap between the world rate r* and the closed-economy domestic equilibrium rate r.

> **In Simple Terms:** In a small open economy, you can't change the world interest rate — you take it as given. Whatever isn't invested at home (S̄ minus I) flows out as net exports. If the world pays more than home would, savers send money abroad → trade surplus. If the world pays less, capital floods in → trade deficit.

### Key Concepts

#### From Y = C + I + G + NX to S̄ − I(r*) = NX
Starting with **NX = Y − (C + I + G)**, in a small open economy we assume Y is fixed at the full-employment level and C is a function of disposable income (also fixed by Y). Therefore S = Y − C − G becomes a **constant S̄**. Investment I depends on the real interest rate, and a small open economy must take the **world** real interest rate r* as given (it cannot influence the world rate). Substituting:

$$\bar{S} - I(r^*) = NX$$

Net exports thus depend only on the world real interest rate r* and the constant S̄.

#### Three cases (Fig 28.3 — Real Interest Rate, Saving and Investment)
The figure has saving as a vertical line at S̄ and an investment function I(r) sloping downward in r. The closed-economy equilibrium is at point E where I(r) cuts S̄ (giving domestic rate r). When the economy opens at world rate r*:

| World Rate vs Domestic | Investment | Trade Balance | Distance on Fig 28.3 |
|------------------------|-----------|---------------|----------------------|
| **r* > r** | I < S̄ → excess saving exported | **Trade surplus** | AB |
| **r* < r** | I > S̄ → must import capital | **Trade deficit** | CD |
| **r* = r** | I = S̄ | **Zero trade balance** | At E |

### Definitions
- **Small Open Economy** ⭐: An economy that takes the world real interest rate r* as given — it cannot influence world prices/rates with its own actions. ⭐
- **Trade Surplus** ⭐: NX > 0 — country exports more than it imports; arises when r* > r (excess saving lent abroad).
- **Trade Deficit** ⭐: NX < 0 — country imports more than it exports; arises when r* < r (capital inflow funds excess investment).

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. World rate r* > domestic rate r → I(r*) < S̄ → excess S̄ flows out → **trade surplus** (AB on Fig 28.3)
2. World rate r* < domestic rate r → I(r*) > S̄ → capital imported → **trade deficit** (CD on Fig 28.3)
3. World rate r* = r → I = S̄ → **zero trade balance** (E)

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "A country can choose its own interest rate even when open" → ✅ Correct: The **small open economy** assumption is precisely that the country must accept the world rate r*; it cannot move it.
- ❌ Mistake: "Trade surplus comes from exporting more goods" → ✅ Correct: It is equivalently the result of the **saving–investment gap** at world rates: surplus = S̄ − I(r*) > 0.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **S̄ − I(r*) = NX** ⭐ (key formula — Check Your Progress 2 Q1)
> - r* > r → trade surplus; r* < r → trade deficit; r* = r → zero TB
> - In small open economy, r* is exogenous

## Section: Real Exchange Rate and Net Exports 🔴

### Core Idea
The **real exchange rate (α)** combines the nominal exchange rate (e) with the relative price level between domestic and foreign countries (θ): α = e × θ. Net exports are a **negative function** of the real exchange rate: NX = f(θ). When the real exchange rate rises, foreign goods become relatively cheaper → imports rise; domestic goods become relatively expensive → exports fall → NX falls.

### Definitions
- **Real Exchange Rate (α)** ⭐: α = e × θ, where e is the nominal exchange rate and θ = (domestic price level) / (foreign price level). ⭐
- **Nominal Exchange Rate (e)**: The market price of one currency in terms of another (₹/$, etc.).
- **θ (Price Ratio)**: Price level in domestic country / Price level in foreign country.

### Key Concepts

#### Why NX is a negative function of θ
For a given e, α rises when θ rises (domestic prices rise relative to foreign). When α (real exchange rate) increases:
- Foreign goods are **relatively inexpensive** → home country **imports rise**
- Domestic goods are **relatively expensive** for foreigners → home country **exports fall**
- ⇒ Net exports **fall**

Symbolically: **NX = f(θ)**, with f′(θ) < 0 (negative slope on Fig 28.4 — real exchange rate plotted on y-axis, NX on x-axis, downward-sloping).

#### Trade balance and currency value (intuition)
When a country **consistently exports more than imports** (trade surplus), demand for its products → demand for its currency → currency **appreciates**. Conversely, persistent **trade deficit** → weaker demand for currency → currency **depreciates**. This is the supply–demand feedback between trade flows and FX value.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - α = e × θ ⭐
> - **NX = f(θ)** with **negative** slope ⭐ (Check Your Progress 2 Q2)
> - Real ER ↑ → imports ↑, exports ↓ → NX ↓
> - Persistent trade surplus → currency appreciates; deficit → depreciates

## Section: Concept of Behavioural Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
Traditional finance assumes "**efficient markets**" — prices reflect fundamental value and all available information; investors are **rational, self-controlled and self-interested** ("homo economicus"). Traditional theory failed to explain market volatility, crashes, irrational investor behaviour, and bubbles. **Behavioural Finance** is the alternative paradigm: it focuses on how psychological and sociological factors shape investor decisions and acknowledges that **investors are not always rational and prices are not always efficient**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Traditional finance treats investors like calculators — perfectly rational, no emotions. Behavioural finance treats them like real humans — sometimes scared, sometimes greedy, often following the crowd. Real markets need the second view to make sense of bubbles and crashes.

### Definitions
- **Behavioural Finance** ⭐: A field that focuses on cognitive, sociological and psychological influences on the decision-making of investors and financial markets. ⭐
- **Homo Economicus** ⭐: The traditional finance ideal investor — bounded by three traits: **rationality, self-control, self-interest**. ⭐
- **Efficient Markets** (concept): The idea that asset prices reflect fundamental value and all available information.

### Key Concepts

#### Why traditional theory broke down
Traditional finance could not explain:
- Market volatility and unpredictability
- Market crashes
- Irrational investor behaviour
- Stock market bubbles
- "Why does an investor trade, how does an investor trade, what factors decide a portfolio?"

#### Two branches of behavioural finance
| Branch | Focus | Studies |
|--------|-------|---------|
| **Microbehavioural Finance** ⭐ | Individual investor | Cognitive, psychological, sociological influences; behavioural biases distinguishing them from rational economic beings; heuristics as rules of thumb leading to suboptimal decisions |
| **Macrobehavioural Finance** ⭐ | Whole market | Market anomalies that make markets inefficient; reasons behind stock bubbles, market volatility, limited arbitrage |

#### Kahneman's Nobel Prize (2002)
Behavioural finance became popular after **2002**, when **Daniel Kahneman** was awarded the Nobel Prize for psychological research into economic science — especially human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Cognitive bias is one of the three traits of homo economicus" → ✅ Correct: The three are **rationality, self-control, self-interest** (Check Your Progress 1 Q3 — answer is c, cognitive bias is NOT a trait).
- ❌ Mistake: "Microbehavioural finance studies bubbles and limited arbitrage" → ✅ Correct: That's **macrobehavioural** finance. **Microbehavioural** = irrational investor behaviour (Check Your Progress 1 Q4 — answer is a).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Behavioural finance = cognitive + sociological + psychological influences ⭐
> - Three traits of homo economicus: **rationality, self-control, self-interest** ⭐
> - **Micro**behavioural = individual investor; **Macro**behavioural = whole market
> - Kahneman Nobel 2002 → behavioural finance went mainstream

## Section: Difference Between Traditional and Behavioural Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
Traditional finance is built on **investor and market rationality** — investors process complete information unbiased by emotion. Behavioural finance is built on **bounded rationality** — investors are influenced by emotions, biases, and heuristics. The two are not opposites but **alternative perspectives** providing a fuller picture of markets.

### Five Key Contrasts

| Dimension | Traditional Finance | Behavioural Finance |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Investor decision basis** | Rational, unbiased information processing | Influenced by overconfidence, familiarity bias, herding, etc.; emotions and psychology may make investors irrational |
| **Information assumption** | Complete information available to all; investors examine it rationally; investors always make right decisions | **Bounded rationality** — investors don't process all info; they **satisfice instead of maximise**; even with accurate info, they err in judgment |
| **Market efficiency** | EMH — markets are efficient; price reflects fundamental value | EMH disputed; anomalies cause volatility unexplainable by EMH |
| **Self-control** | Investors have **perfect self-control**; past/personality don't influence | Self-control problems exist and affect both investor and market |
| **Arbitrage** | Any "noise" balanced out by rational traders | **Limit to arbitrage**; prices not always right; non-equilibrium can persist |

### Definitions
- **Bounded Rationality** ⭐: Investors don't process all available information; they **satisfice** (settle for "good enough") rather than maximise. ⭐
- **Limit to Arbitrage** ⭐: Behavioural finance proposition that arbitrage cannot fully correct mispricing — prices may stay in non-equilibrium for periods. ⭐
- **Satisficing**: Choosing an acceptable rather than optimal outcome.

### Examples
- **Herding example**: When buying stocks, investors follow others "because they think everyone else knows more" → herding mentality → sub-optimal decisions

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Traditional: rational + complete info + EMH + self-control + arbitrage corrects all
> - Behavioural: bounded rationality + biases + EMH-failures + self-control problems + limit to arbitrage
> - Investors **satisfice** rather than maximise
> - "Rational, irrational" is the right pair (CYP2 Q3 → answer b)
> - "Complete information available to everyone" is **NOT** behavioural — it's traditional (CYP2 Q4 → answer a)

## Section: Growth and Origin of Behavioural Finance 🟡

### Core Idea
Behavioural finance has roots in 1960s–70s cognitive psychology but really emerged in the **1980s** to explain market anomalies — driven by Kahneman and Tversky's work. It evolved in **two phases**: (i) documenting examples of anomalies, then (ii) explaining them. The late 1990s/early 2000s saw it absorbed into mainstream finance, cemented by two consecutive Nobel Prizes (2001, 2002).

### Two-Phase Evolution

| Phase | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Phase 1** | Documenting **examples of market anomalies** and deviations from standard finance assumptions |
| **Phase 2** | **Explaining** these anomalies |

### Timeline of Key Contributions

| Year | Contribution | Author(s) |
|------|--------------|-----------|
| 1981 | Market anomalies — rational factors alone can't explain stock market volatility | **Robert James Shiller** |
| 1985 | Stock market overreaction → value anomaly | **De Bondt and Richard Thaler** |
| 1980s | Concepts: **disposition effect**, **equity premium puzzle** | Various economists |
| Late 1990s/early 2000s | Behavioural finance becomes mainstream; concepts: **limit to arbitrage**, **myopic loss aversion**, **over-reactions and under-reactions** |  |
| 2000 | Book *Irrational Exuberance* — predicted dot-com crash | **Robert Shiller** |
| Around 2000 | Noise traders → limited arbitrage → market anomalies | **Andrei Shleifer** |
| **2001** | Nobel Prize "for analyses of markets with **asymmetric information**" | **George Akerlof, Michael Spence, Joseph Stiglitz** |
| **2002** | Nobel Prize "for integrating insights from **psychological research** into economic science, especially concerning human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty" | **Daniel Kahneman** |

### Critique
Behavioural finance is criticised for being "**Micro-efficient but not Macro-inefficient**" — i.e., it explains individual investor behaviour effectively but is not very useful in **predicting the overall market**. It explains market anomalies in the **long run**, but cannot reliably predict **short-run** stock-market variations.

### Definitions
- **Disposition Effect** (originated 1980s): Tendency to prematurely sell winning assets while holding losing ones (detailed in Chunk 010).
- **Equity Premium Puzzle** (originated 1980s): Empirical observation that stocks have outperformed bonds yet investors still hold bonds — contradicts pure risk-aversion models.
- **Myopic Loss Aversion**: Frequent portfolio evaluation amplifies loss aversion (detailed in Chunk 010).
- **Irrational Exuberance** (Shiller 2000): Investor enthusiasm pushing stock prices above fundamentals.
- **Noise Traders** (Shleifer): Traders whose decisions are not based on fundamentals; they cause limited arbitrage.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Behavioural finance evolved in **1980s**
> - Two phases: (1) document anomalies (2) explain them
> - **Kahneman & Tversky** are the foundational figures
> - **Shiller 1981** (volatility anomalies) and **De Bondt-Thaler 1985** (overreaction)
> - **2001 Nobel** — Akerlof/Spence/Stiglitz (asymmetric info); **2002 Nobel** — Kahneman
> - Critique: "**Micro-efficient, not Macro-inefficient**" — long-run yes, short-run no

## Section: Efficient Market Hypothesis and Its Critique 🔴

### Core Idea
The **Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)** says market prices fully reflect all available information; new information is rapidly incorporated, so current prices are the best approximation of intrinsic value. EMH is associated with the "**random walk**" — past prices cannot predict future movements, and no one can outperform the market without taking extra risk. Behavioural finance counters with **momentum** in short-run prices: investors follow trends ("bandwagon effect"), under-react to new information, and exhibit biases that produce **predictable** rather than random price movements.

### Definitions
- **Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)** ⭐: Market prices fully reflect all available information; current prices are the best approximation of a company's intrinsic value. Developed independently by **Paul A. Samuelson** and **Eugene F. Fama** in the **1960s**. ⭐
- **Random Walk Theory** ⭐: Past stock-price movements/trends cannot predict future movement; impossible to outperform market without additional risk. Logic: news is unpredictable, so today's situation cannot predict future values. ⭐
- **Bandwagon Effect / Momentum**: Investors follow market trends — rising price → more investment → further price rise.
- **Irrational Exuberance**: Investor enthusiasm pushing stock price above fundamental value.
- **Under-reaction**: Investors take time to update beliefs about a stock; full impact realised over a period → positive correlation in prices (not random).

### Key Concepts

#### EMH "currency-note-on-the-road" intuition
"If a currency note is lying on the road, it will not be there for long as it will be surely picked up by someone." Similarly, all profitable information is picked up and incorporated by market agents → no persistent free lunches.

#### Behavioural critique of randomness
1. **Bandwagon effect** — herding into rising stocks → momentum
2. **Under-reaction** — investors take time to update beliefs → news is gradually priced in → **positive serial correlation** (changes in prices are NOT random)
3. **Behavioural biases** — sociological, psychological, cognitive influences cause behavioural anomalies

### Three Core Puzzles Driven by Behavioural Biases

| Puzzle | What it says |
|--------|--------------|
| **Equity Premium Puzzle** | Stocks have earned disproportionately higher returns than bonds, yet investors are relatively unwilling to hold them |
| **Volatility Puzzle** | Stock returns are highly variable with **large dispersions** |
| **Predictability Puzzle** | Stock returns are predictable — persistent profits NOT eroded by arbitrage |

### Common Behavioural Biases (introduced)
- **Herding**, **overconfidence**, **overreaction**, **loss aversion**, **mental accounting** — these dominate investor thinking and produce the three puzzles above.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **EMH** = Samuelson & Fama, 1960s ⭐
> - EMH ↔ **Random Walk**: past prices ≠ predictor of future ⭐
> - Behavioural counter: momentum + under-reaction → positive serial correlation
> - 3 puzzles: **equity premium, volatility, predictability**
> - 5 dominant biases: herding, overconfidence, overreaction, loss aversion, mental accounting

## Section: Market Anomalies — Three Types 🔴

### Core Idea
Tversky & Kahneman (1986) defined a market anomaly as: "*a deviation from the presently accepted paradigms that are too widespread to be ignored, too systematic to be dismissed as random error and too fundamental to be accommodated by relaxing the normative system*." Anomalies fall into **three types**: **Fundamental**, **Calendar/Seasonal**, and **Technical**.

### Definitions
- **Market Anomaly** ⭐: A deviation from the predictions of EMH that is too widespread, systematic, and fundamental to be dismissed as random error. (Tversky & Kahneman 1986). ⭐

### Three Types of Anomalies

#### (i) Fundamental Anomalies
| Anomaly | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Value Anomaly** | Over-estimation of future earnings and returns of growth companies |
| **Low Price-to-Book** | Stocks with high book-to-market generate **less return** than stocks with low price-to-book |
| **High Dividend Yield** | Positive correlation between yield and stock return; high-dividend-yield stocks **outperform** the market |
| **Neglected Stocks** | Prior neglected stocks generate **more return** subsequently; prior best performers underperform the index |
| **Low Price-to-Earnings (P/E)** | Low P/E stocks tend to **outperform**; high P/E stocks tend to **underperform** |

#### (ii) Calendar/Seasonal Anomalies
Irregular patterns of stock returns across periods — day-to-day, month-to-month, year-to-year.

| Anomaly | Pattern |
|---------|---------|
| **Weekend Effect** | Monday closing < previous Friday closing |
| **Turn-of-the-Month Effect** | Stock prices rise in **last trading day** of month + **first three days** of next month |
| **Turn-of-the-Year Effect** | Prices and trading volume rise in **last week of December** + **first half of January** |
| **January Effect** | Small-company stocks generate more return than other assets in **first 2–3 weeks of January** |

#### (iii) Technical Anomalies
Techniques to forecast prices from past prices — contradicting weak-form EMH which says past prices are already incorporated.

| Anomaly | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Moving Averages** | Short-period MA crossing **above** long-period MA = **buy** signal; crossing **below** = **sell** signal |
| **Trading Range Break** | Identify support/resistance + price trends → entry/exit points; "breakout" = potential trading opportunity |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **3 anomaly types**: Fundamental, Calendar/Seasonal, Technical ⭐ (CYP3 Q1)
> - 5 fundamental: Value, Low P/B, High Div Yield, Neglected, Low P/E
> - 4 calendar: Weekend, Turn-of-Month, Turn-of-Year, January
> - 2 technical: Moving Averages, Trading Range Break
> - Tversky & Kahneman 1986 definition of "anomaly"

## Section: Market Puzzles and Behavioural Explanations 🔴
<!-- Continues in chunk 010 — Predictability puzzle (disposition effect, momentum effect) -->

### Core Idea
Behavioural finance explains three market puzzles that EMH cannot: **Equity Premium Puzzle**, **Volatility Puzzle**, and **Predictability Puzzle**. The first two are fully covered in this chunk; the predictability puzzle's behavioural explanation continues into Chunk 010.

### Puzzle (i): Equity Premium Puzzle

**The puzzle (Mehra and Prescott)**: Stocks have **greatly outperformed bonds** over the last century — earned disproportionately higher returns — yet **people still buy bonds**. The level of risk aversion required to justify this is implausibly high. Pure risk-aversion models cannot explain it.

**Behavioural explanations** (Prospect Theory):
1. **Loss aversion** — investors are more sensitive to losses than to gains
2. **Myopic loss aversion** — investors evaluate their portfolio frequently (even when saving for retirement); frequent evaluation amplifies the felt impact of short-term losses
3. **Ambiguity aversion** — preference for **known** risks over **unknown** risks; equity-return distribution is unknown, so people overweigh probability of returns from bonds

### Puzzle (ii): Volatility Puzzle

**The puzzle**: High variability of stock prices with large dispersions. Changes in stock prices are **disproportionately higher than changes in fundamentals** — contradicts EMH (which says prices represent fundamentals).

**Behavioural explanations** (cognitive, social, emotional biases):
- Investors **overweigh recent information** and **consensus beliefs**
- Investors seek **confirming evidence** → under-react or overreact in the market
- **Focal points** — investors focus more on similar pieces of information
- **Loss aversion** — overweighing of negative information leads to **steep falls** (rather than gradual upward correction)

### Puzzle (iii): Predictability Puzzle (introduced — continues in chunk 010)

**The puzzle**: Stock returns are **predictable from price-dividend ratios** — contradicts random-walk hypothesis.

**Behavioural explanations** (begun, continues into next chunk):
- **Disposition effect** — tendency to prematurely sell assets that have made financial gains while holding on to losing assets

## Section: Heuristics — Concept and Origin 🔴

### Core Idea
Behavioural finance replaces "**econs**" (rational decision-makers) with "**humans**" who are influenced by cognitive, social and emotional factors. Some daily decisions are automatic; complex decisions like investments require focus and analysis. When facing difficult situations, humans use **heuristics** — "rules of thumb" or imperfect, functional shortcuts that simplify complex situations and enable quick decisions. Heuristics are necessary but produce **systematic and predictable errors called biases**.

### Definitions
- **Heuristics** ⭐: Imperfect but functional shortcuts ("rules of thumb") that help investors simplify complex situations and make quick decisions. ⭐
- **Substitution** (Kahneman): When faced with a difficult problem, individuals replace it with an easier question and answer that instead. *Example*: instead of "how much profit will this stock make?" → "what do I feel about this stock?" (uses **affect heuristic**).
- **Bias**: Systematic and predictable error introduced by heuristic substitution.
- **Econs vs Humans**: "Econs" = traditional rational decision-makers; "humans" = real-world decision-makers influenced by cognitive/social/emotional factors.

### Origin
- **1950s** — **Herbert A. Simon** introduced the concept of heuristics; argued there are limitations to rational decision-making (bounded rationality)
- **1970s** — **Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman** worked on heuristics to understand cognitive biases; explained why intuitive judgement deviates from logical rules

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Heuristics are inherently bad and should be avoided" → ✅ Correct: Heuristics are **functional** shortcuts — they enable quick decisions in complex situations. The problem is that **substitution** causes systematic biases as a side effect.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Heuristic = imperfect but functional shortcut
> - Origin: **Simon (1950s)** → **Tversky & Kahneman (1970s)**
> - Mechanism: **substitution** of hard question with easier one
> - Side effect: systematic & predictable **biases**

## Section: Four Prominent Heuristics 🔴

### Core Idea
Four heuristics dominate investor decision-making: **Anchoring and Adjustment**, **Representativeness**, **Availability**, and **Affect**. Each is a useful shortcut that produces a specific type of bias when applied to investment problems.

### Heuristic 1: Anchoring and Adjustment

**Definition** ⭐: People use a **starting point ("the anchor")** and make adjustments until they reach an acceptable value. The anchor's value can be **statistically irrelevant**, yet still influences the final estimate; adjustments are typically insufficient.

**Why people use it**: To reduce cognitive load on the brain.

**Investment example**: Asked to estimate Reliance's stock value 4 months ahead, most investors **anchor on the present value** and adjust up/down. The present value biases the answer.

**Real-world example**: Property dealers quote high prices to set anchors and bargain down — buyers feel satisfaction even if the final price is still high.

### Heuristic 2: Representativeness

**Definition** ⭐ (Kahneman & Tversky 1972): In situations of uncertainty, people evaluate the probability of an uncertain event by the degree to which it is (i) **similar in essential properties** to its parent population and (ii) **reflects salient features** of the process by which it is generated. People decide based on representative information rather than logical, probabilistic reasoning.

**Three judgment errors caused by representativeness**:
| Error | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Neglecting Base Rate** | People ignore general/base-rate information when specific info is also provided |
| **Conjunction Fallacy** | Assuming specific conditions are more likely than general conditions |
| **Law of Small Numbers** | Exaggerating the degree to which a small sample resembles its population |

**Investment manifestation**:
- Investors see past performance as representative of future performance
- They **overweight recent information** to the detriment of past information
- A few profitable years → assumption of continued great performance
- Sometimes leads to **stock-market overreactions and underreactions**

### Heuristic 3: Availability

**Definition** ⭐: People assess the probability of an event based on the **ease with which it comes to mind**. Principle: "if you can think of it, it must be important." Things easily recalled are believed to be more common and accurate representations of reality.

**Real-world example**: How easily you can recall theft in your locality determines how you'd rate the law-and-order situation, even though a recent crime may not be representative.

**Investment manifestation**:
- Recent market news/events affect investment decisions more than fundamentals
- Often causes **overreactions** → market crashes or bubbles
- Investors overestimate probability of similar events recurring

**"Hot Hand" bias** (an availability-heuristic bias):
- Originally noticed in **basketball** — players scoring maximum baskets keep getting maximum passes (even if average players)
- Generalizes to unrelated events (dice rolls, coin flips)
- In financial markets: investors prefer traders who have recently performed well, even though **past performance ≠ future performance**

### Heuristic 4: Affect

**Definition** ⭐: People assign probabilities of an event based on **how a person feels about it**. Good feelings → higher probability assigned to good consequences; bad feelings → higher probability assigned to bad outcomes.

**Properties**:
- Emotions and mood drive decisions
- People take **more risky decisions when happy** (positive state of mind)
- Most prominent when people lack **time or resources to reflect**
- Can lead to **overconfidence bias**

**Investment example**: Investors invest in a company's stock because "they have a good feeling about it" — feelings unrelated to fundamentals → irrational decisions.

### Summary Table — Four Heuristics

| Heuristic | Mechanism | Typical Bias |
|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| **Anchoring & Adjustment** | Use a starting value, adjust insufficiently | Estimates biased toward (often irrelevant) anchor |
| **Representativeness** | Judge by similarity to prototype | Base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, law of small numbers, overreaction/underreaction |
| **Availability** | Probability ≈ ease of recall | Overweighing recent events; "hot hand" bias |
| **Affect** | Probability ≈ how it feels | Emotion-driven decisions; overconfidence |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **4 heuristics**: Anchoring, Representativeness, Availability, Affect ⭐
> - Anchoring → reduces cognitive load; insufficient adjustments
> - Representativeness → 3 errors: base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, law of small numbers
> - Availability → "if you can think of it, it must be important"; **hot hand** bias
> - Affect → emotion-driven; risky decisions when happy

## Section: Cognitive Influences on Investor Behaviour 🔴

### Core Idea
Six cognitive biases shape investor decisions: **Over-Confidence**, **Cognitive Dissonance**, **Under-reaction & Overreaction**, **Disposition Effect**, **Diversification Bias**, and **Mental Accounting**. Each leads to systematically suboptimal financial decisions.

### Six Cognitive Influences

#### (i) Over-Confidence
- Often arises from **self-attribution bias** — attributing successes to one's ability and failures to external factors
- Leads to **excessive trading** — overconfident investors buy too often, must sell too often to free capital
- Reinforced by **hindsight bias** — believing one "knew it would happen" after the fact (e.g., "I knew the market would crash")
- Hindsight bias makes investors believe they have exceptional foresight → uncalculated risks

#### (ii) Cognitive Dissonance (Festinger)
**Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance**: People feel internal tension and anxiety when subjected to **conflicting beliefs**. They reduce dissonance by:
- Changing past values or feelings
- Justifying the decision

**Investment manifestation**: Investors **sell losers too late** because they cannot accept making a wrong decision; they avoid feelings of regret and embarrassment from reporting a loss.

#### (iii) Under-reaction and Overreaction
- **Under-reaction**: When new info contradicts existing beliefs, investors **underweigh** it; adjust over weeks
- **Over-reaction**: When new info **aligns** with existing beliefs, investors overreact
- Both produce sub-optimal decisions and **stock-market volatility**

#### (iv) Disposition Effect
- Tendency to **prematurely sell winners** while **holding on to losers** (in hope of recovery)
- Investors **lose out on momentum gains** when they sell winning assets too quickly
- **Kahneman's Prospect Theory explanation**: Investors become **risk-averse after gains** (lock in profit) and **risk-seeking after losses** (hold losing assets, hoping to recover)

#### (v) Diversification Bias
Two opposite errors:
| Error | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Over-diversification** | Choosing variety when making **simultaneous** future choices; overestimating need for diversity → diversifying when concentrated investment would be optimal |
| **Insufficient diversification** | **Home bias** — heavy investment in companies from one's home country, driven by **availability heuristic + familiarity bias**; no economic justification but familiarity reduces perceived risk |

#### (vi) Mental Accounting
- Investors classify money into separate "**mental accounts**" based on **subjective criteria**
- Both **safe** and **speculative** investments kept in different mental accounts so negative speculative returns don't "affect" positive safe returns
- Investors hold **money they can afford to lose** for speculation
- **Rationally, there should be no division** between "safety capital" and "money I can afford to lose"
- Leads to financially counterproductive decisions

### Definitions
- **Self-Attribution Bias** ⭐: Attributing successes to oneself and failures to external factors. ⭐
- **Hindsight Bias** ⭐: Belief, after an event, that one knew the outcome before it happened. ⭐
- **Cognitive Dissonance** ⭐ (Festinger): Internal tension/anxiety from conflicting beliefs; reduced by changing beliefs or justifying decisions. ⭐
- **Under-reaction**: Investors underweigh new information that conflicts with prior beliefs.
- **Over-reaction**: Investors overweigh new information that confirms prior beliefs.
- **Disposition Effect** ⭐: Sell winners early, hold losers (already defined Chunk 009 wrap-up).
- **Diversification Bias** ⭐: Choosing more variety than optimal when picking simultaneous items; opposite error: home bias / under-diversification. ⭐
- **Home Bias**: Heavy investment in domestic companies driven by availability + familiarity.
- **Mental Accounting** ⭐: Cognitive operations classifying money into different mental accounts based on subjective criteria. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Cognitive dissonance is felt only after big losses" → ✅ Correct: Cognitive dissonance arises from any **conflicting beliefs**; investors experience it whenever an outcome contradicts their prior view (selling losers late is the canonical example).
- ❌ Mistake: "Mental accounting is rational risk management" → ✅ Correct: There should be **no division** between "safe" and "speculative" capital — money is fungible. Mental accounting often produces irrational, counterproductive choices.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **6 cognitive influences**: Overconfidence, Cognitive Dissonance, Under/Overreaction, Disposition Effect, Diversification Bias, Mental Accounting ⭐ (CYP4 Q1)
> - Overconfidence ← self-attribution + hindsight bias
> - Disposition effect ← Prospect Theory: risk-averse after gains, risk-seeking after losses
> - Diversification bias = both over-diversification AND home bias
> - Self-attribution bias = success self-credited, failure blamed externally (CYP4 Q3b)

## Section: Social Influences on Investor Behaviour 🔴

### Core Idea
Five social influences shape investor decisions: **Herding**, **Reputation**, **Beauty Contests**, **Success Stories from Family**, and **Expert Influence**. All flow from **social learning** — the assumption that others may know better.

### Five Social Influences

#### (i) Herding
- In financial markets, herding generates **speculative bubbles** when traders track the decisions of others rather than fundamental values
- Investors with herding instincts follow others rather than their own analysis
- Causes: **asset bubbles, panic buying or selling**
- Outcome of **social learning** — believing others are better informed
- During financial speculation, investors buy at high prices not because they believe the asset is worth it but because they believe **others** believe it is

#### (ii) Reputation
- Herding emerges as a **reputation concern**
- **Keynes**: it is rational to follow the crowd because it preserves reputation
- *"It is better to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally"*

#### (iii) Beauty Contests (Keynes)
**Keynes's analogy**: A newspaper competition where competitors must select photos that **the majority of others** will select. Similar to **iterated reasoning** in financial speculation:
- Speculators care about **identifying short-term investment plans of others**, NOT what they themselves think is the best investment
- Asset prices subjected to **financial loops** → financial instability
- Instability magnified when borrowings fund the assets

#### (iv) Success Stories and Advice from Family
- People invest **more** when neighbours and family have profited from stocks
- Asymmetric: investors are NOT discouraged by failures of those around them
- Reason: **people share success stories more than failures**

#### (v) Expert Influence
- Analysts may have incentives to tout particular shares or are influenced by other analysts
- Leads to **herding** and inferior returns

### Definitions
- **Herding** ⭐: Outcome of social learning when people believe others are better informed and thus follow the crowd. ⭐
- **Beauty Contest** (Keynes): Iterated-reasoning analogy in financial speculation — what others think will be popular, not what is fundamentally best.
- **Financial Loops**: Self-reinforcing speculative price cycles, magnified by borrowing.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **5 social influences**: Herding, Reputation, Beauty Contests, Success Stories from Family, Expert Influence
> - Herding = **social learning** outcome; "others know better" assumption ⭐ (CYP4 Q3a)
> - Keynes: "**Better to fail conventionally than succeed unconventionally**"
> - **Beauty contest** = iterated reasoning; predict what others predict
> - Success stories outweigh failure stories in investor influence

## Section: Emotional Influences on Investor Behaviour 🔴

### Core Idea
Three emotional influences shape investor decisions: **Feelings of Regret**, **Loss Aversion**, and **Sunk Cost Fallacy**. Together they explain why investors avoid realising losses, overvalue what they own, and throw good money after bad.

### Three Emotional Influences

#### (i) Feelings of Regret
- **Theory of Regret**: Emotional reaction after realising one has made a wrong decision
- When an investment goes bad, investors **avoid selling** to **avoid feelings of regret**
- Investors reduce regret by **following others** — losing money in popular investments feels less embarrassing than losing in unpopular ones
- Connected to **cognitive dissonance** (above, Cognitive Influences)

#### (ii) Loss Aversion
- Investors find losses **disproportionately painful** compared with the pleasure of equivalent gains
- Makes people **prefer things as they are** (status quo bias)
- Causes divergence between **willingness to pay** and **willingness to accept**:
  - People pay **less** for an object they do not own
  - But demand **more** when selling the same object they own
- Example: An antique art piece worth ₹50,000 — you wouldn't pay ₹50,000 to buy it but would expect more if selling

#### (iii) Sunk Cost Fallacy
- **Emotional tendency to invest more money, time, effort** into a failing project because of prior investment
- **Closely linked to loss aversion** — emotional involvement prevents accepting failure
- Leads to two further mistakes:
  - **Aggressive Investing**: Risk-averse investors become aggressive **after** losing money → take undue risks → huge losses
  - **Averaging**: Investing more in losing stocks to "average down" → larger losses

### Definitions
- **Theory of Regret** ⭐: When an investment goes bad, people act irrationally by avoiding selling to avoid feelings of regret. ⭐
- **Loss Aversion** ⭐: Investors find losses disproportionately painful compared to equivalent gains; makes people prefer things as they are. ⭐
- **Willingness to Pay vs Willingness to Accept**: Loss aversion's manifestation — own → demand more; not own → pay less.
- **Sunk Cost Fallacy** ⭐: Emotional tendency to invest more money/time/effort into a failing project because we have already invested. ⭐
- **Aggressive Investing** (sunk-cost related): Risk-averse investors becoming risk-takers after losses.
- **Averaging** (sunk-cost related): Investing more in losing stocks to lower average cost — usually compounds losses.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Loss aversion just means you don't like losing" → ✅ Correct: Loss aversion means losses feel **disproportionately worse** than equivalent gains feel good — it's an asymmetry, not just dislike.
- ❌ Mistake: "Sunk cost fallacy is the same as risk aversion" → ✅ Correct: Sunk cost fallacy often leads investors **toward** risk (aggressive investing, averaging) — it's emotional commitment to past decisions, not risk preference.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **3 emotional influences**: Regret, Loss Aversion, Sunk Cost Fallacy
> - Loss aversion: asymmetric — losses **disproportionately** painful (CYP4 Q3d)
> - Sunk cost → **aggressive investing** + **averaging** (both compound losses)
> - Regret avoidance is one reason investors herd (links to social influences)

### Likely High-Yield Question (Q12 commentary)
**"Behavioural finance is micro-efficient but not macro-inefficient"** means: behavioural finance can effectively explain individual investor behaviour (micro) but is NOT very useful in **predicting the overall market** (macro). It explains anomalies in the **long run**, but cannot predict short-run market variations. This is the key critique of the field — students should be able to defend or critique this position using examples from heuristics (micro success) versus 2008 crisis prediction failures (macro limitation).
