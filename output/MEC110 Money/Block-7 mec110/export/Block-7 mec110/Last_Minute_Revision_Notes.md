### Macroeconomics and Business Cycles 🔴

**Business Cycle — Four Stages**
| Stage | What happens |
|-------|--------------|
| 1. Expansion | Investment rising (low interest rates), output up, employment up, consumption up, demand up, income up |
| 2. Peak / Boom | Economic activity at its highest — economy doing extremely well; "if there is a boom, there is going to be a burst eventually" |
| 3. Contraction / Recession | Aggregate demand falls, output slows, investment slows, employment falls, income falls, consumption decreases |
| 4. Depression | Worst phase — aggregate demand decreases further, investment decreases, production decreases again, more layoffs |
- **Macroeconomics**: Study of the economy as a whole — focused on employment, inflation, output/GDP, growth rate, exchange rate, balance of payments, and business cycles.
- **Business Cycle** ⭐: Ups and downs in economic activities namely output, unemployment, and income, among other things; indication of how an economy is doing. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Capital Formation**: Build-up of capital base — a major factor responsible for the growth of an economy.

**Quick Recall:**
- Four stages: Expansion → Peak → Contraction → Depression
- Cycle is vicious — one stage feeds the next
- Business cycles often linked with financial-sector problems

### Case Study — The Great Depression (1929–1941) 🔴
| Date / Period | Event |
|---------------|-------|
| 1920s | US growth 4.7%, unemployment 3.2%, exorbitant wealth growth |
| Oct 24, 1929 ("Black Thursday") | Market opened down 11% from previous day |
| 1929–1932 | Stock market fell by 85% |
| Peak of Depression | Unemployment 24% (1 in 4 unemployed); 30% of banks failed |
| Within 4 years of recession | US GDP fell by 29% |
| 1933 | President Roosevelt increased government spending — too late |
| 1941 | Depression ended |
1. Weak stock-market regulation → speculation in equities
2. Public investment in stocks fuelled by high wealth → asset-price bubble
3. Banks lent excessively (lowered reserves) + expansionary monetary policy → more borrowing
4. 1929 crash → Fed **raised** interest rates (instead of lowering) → deflation
5. Higher tariffs on agricultural imports (intended to protect US agriculture) → trade contraction → deeper depression
6. Roosevelt's 1933 fiscal stimulus came too late
7. Outcomes: 30% bank failure, factory shutdowns, rural poverty, falling birth rate, rising women's workforce participation

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "The Fed cut rates after the 1929 crash" → ✅ Correct: The Fed **raised** rates, intending weak banks would fail and a stronger system emerge — this caused deflation.
- ❌ Mistake: "Tariffs helped recovery" → ✅ Correct: Higher agricultural tariffs **worsened** the depression by choking trade.

**Quick Recall:**
- 1929: Black Thursday, –11% in one day
- 1929–1932: stock market –85%
- Peak unemployment: 24% (1 in 4)
- GDP fell 29% in 4 years
- Fed *raised* rates after the crash → deflation

### Financial System — Direct vs Indirect Finance 🔴

**Direct vs Indirect Finance**
| Aspect | Direct Finance | Indirect Finance |
|--------|----------------|------------------|
| Channel | Financial markets (stocks, bonds) | Financial intermediaries (banks, mutual funds, insurance, pension) |
| Example | Reliance issues bonds / IPO; you buy them | You deposit in a bank, bank lends |
| Information needed by lender | High — must analyse borrower directly | Low — intermediary handles screening |
| Expertise needed | High | Low |

**Components of the Financial System**
- **Financial institutions**: banks, insurance companies, mutual funds, pension funds
- **Financial markets**: stock markets, bond markets
- **Direct Finance** ⭐: Lenders directly provide funds to borrowers, e.g., buying bonds in the bond market or stocks in an IPO. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Indirect Finance** ⭐: Lending through an intermediary (bank, mutual fund, insurance, pension) that receives money from lenders and lends it to borrowers. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial System**: Includes financial institutions (banks, insurance, mutual funds) and financial markets (stocks, bonds) — channels funds from savers to investors and reduces transaction costs and risks.

**Quick Recall:**
- Direct = via markets (stocks/bonds)
- Indirect = via intermediaries (banks/MFs/insurance/pensions)
- Informal markets shrink as economy develops

### Risks in Financial Markets 🔴
| Risk | What it is |
|------|-----------|
| **Credit / default risk** | Deposits or credit given may not be paid back partially or fully |
| **Liquidity risk** | Intermediary unable to meet liquidity demands due to sudden, unexpected withdrawals or liability payments |
| **Interest rate & price risk** | Volatility in interest rates and asset prices deteriorates the financial health / balance sheets |
| **Exchange rate risk** | Volatility in the exchange rate affects value of assets/liabilities held in foreign currency, worsening balance sheets |
| **Asymmetric-information risk** | One party has less information — root cause of many other risks (see next section) |
- **Credit (Default) Risk** ⭐: Risk that deposits or credit given will not be paid back partially or fully. ⭐
- **Liquidity Risk** ⭐: Risk that a financial intermediary, including a bank, finds itself unable to meet liquidity demands due to sudden and unexpected increases in demand for liability payments or withdrawal of deposits. ⭐
- **Interest Rate & Price Risk** ⭐: Risk that volatility in interest rates and asset prices deteriorates financial health / balance sheets.
- **Exchange Rate Risk** ⭐: Risk that volatility in the exchange rate affects the value of assets and liabilities held in foreign currency, worsening balance sheets.
- **Insolvency**: When the value of assets of financial intermediaries (or firms / individuals) falls below the value of liabilities.

**Quick Recall:**
- Five risks: credit, liquidity, interest-rate/price, exchange-rate, asymmetric-information
- Multiple risks → assets < liabilities → insolvency
- Intermediaries' core job: assess + manage these risks

### Asymmetric Information, Adverse Selection, Moral Hazard 🔴

**Adverse Selection vs Moral Hazard**
| Aspect | Adverse Selection | Moral Hazard |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|
| When it occurs | **Before** the transaction | **After** the transaction |
| Trigger | Hidden info about borrower / project type | Hidden actions after funds received |
| Mechanism | Risky borrower hides risk, may even offer higher interest, gets funded | Borrower diverts funds to risky activity, or skips precautions |
| Textbook example | Risky firm gets loan because it offered high interest, defaults later | Insured person skips fire-safety system to save costs; firm puts loan into risky bonds |
- **Asymmetric Information** ⭐: Situation where, in a transaction, one party does not have the same information as the other party. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Adverse Selection** ⭐: Problem arising *before* a financial transaction, where lenders/investors invest in a project without knowing its true risk because the borrower hid risk and pushed for funding (often offering a higher interest rate). ⭐
- **Moral Hazard** ⭐: Problem arising *after* a financial transaction (regardless of whether adverse selection occurred), where the borrower diverts borrowed funds to risky projects or behaves recklessly because the cost of failure is shifted to the lender. ⭐
- **Risky firm hiding info**: A firm planning a risky project hides info from lenders and offers a higher interest rate; lenders invest unaware → adverse selection.
- **Diverted loan**: A firm uses borrowed funds to buy risky bonds. If the bonds pay → bank gets repaid. If not → firm defaults. → moral hazard.
- **Fire insurance**: Insured party doesn't install efficient fire-safety system to cut costs after taking insurance. → moral hazard.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Adverse selection and moral hazard are the same thing" → ✅ Correct: Adverse selection is **pre-transaction** (selection of who gets funded); moral hazard is **post-transaction** (behaviour after funding).
- ❌ Mistake: "Moral hazard requires adverse selection first" → ✅ Correct: Moral hazard can occur **whether or not** adverse selection occurred.

**Quick Recall:**
- Asymmetric info = unequal knowledge between two parties
- Adverse selection = BEFORE deal; risky borrowers self-select in
- Moral hazard = AFTER deal; borrowers act recklessly with someone else's money

### Financial Crisis — Common Elements 🔴
1. Investor optimism + easy credit availability → rising prices above fundamentals = **asset-price bubble**
2. Easy credit fuels investment → investment fuels prices → feedback loop = **credit boom / credit bubble**
3. Even a small shock → bust; investors withdraw → prices crash
1. Banks lend against collateral / mortgages
2. Banks **leverage** (borrow from other institutions to lend more)
3. Asset-price bust → mortgage value falls → defaults → banks can't recover loans
4. Banks fall short of payment obligations → reduce lending → asset prices fall further
5. Assets < liabilities → insolvency
1. Depositors fear losing money → line up to withdraw
2. Deposit insurance (where present) is small
3. Banks lack reserves → start selling assets
4. Asset prices already low + few buyers → further collapse = **fire sale**
5. Confidence collapses → multiple banks fail in a short duration = banking failure
1. Bank failures → low confidence → harder to get new bank capital
2. Banks cut lending → SMEs especially can't get loans → consumer credit also drops
3. Credit shortage → fall in investment, operations, and consumption demand → recession
4. Prices fall → in real terms, debt rises = **debt deflation**
5. Falling prices and incomes + rising real debt → firm insolvencies → further financial-sector damage = vicious cycle
- **Financial Crisis** ⭐: An upset in the financial markets in the form of wide fluctuations in asset prices (e.g., stocks, real estate), associated with fluctuations in flow of credit and disruptions in the working of financial intermediaries and markets. ⭐
- **Asset-Price Bubble** ⭐: When prices of financial assets like stocks or real estate increase by a large amount over their fundamental values. ⭐
- **Credit Boom / Credit Bubble** ⭐: A rapid increase in credit availability that, fuelled by investor optimism about asset returns, intensifies an asset-price bubble.
- **Leveraging** ⭐: Banks borrow from other financial institutions to increase their lending capacity. The borrowed funds raise lending capacity but increase fragility when asset prices fall. ⭐
- **Fire Sale** ⭐: Rapid selling of assets by banks (or others) at depressed prices when asset markets are already weak — drives prices down further. ⭐
- **Bank Run / Banking Failure** ⭐: Depositors rush to withdraw money fearing bank failure; banks lack reserves; multiple banks fail in a short duration.
- **Debt Deflation** ⭐: With prices falling, in real terms the debt of firms increases — incomes fall but debt burden rises, leading to insolvency. ⭐
- **Financial Stability**: Conditions enabling better assessment and management of financial risk; promotes efficient allocation of resources and economic growth.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "All crises are basically the same" → ✅ Correct: Crises differ in nature and magnitude but share **four common elements** that typically unfold in sequence.
- ❌ Mistake: "Leveraging is just borrowing" → ✅ Correct: Leveraging specifically means **banks borrowing from other financial institutions** to increase lending capacity — it amplifies both gains and losses.

**Quick Recall:**
- Crisis sequence: asset bubble → bank insolvency → bank runs → credit crunch + debt deflation
- Speculation + asymmetric information are common triggers
- Vicious cycle: financial-sector damage ↔ economic downturn

### Policy Responses to a Crisis 🔴

**Three Policy Tools**
| Tool | Mechanism | Channels |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Fiscal Policy** | Tax cuts (↑ disposable income) or ↑ government spending — magnitude scales with severity of crisis | Infrastructure, industry stimulus, social infrastructure (health, education), social welfare schemes |
| **Monetary Policy** | ↓ interest rates (↓ borrowing cost), ↑ liquidity / money supply (eases credit crunch) | Open market operations, rate cuts |
| **Strengthening Regulatory Framework** | Continuous supervision and evaluation of financial institutions and instruments; reform pace must match financial-innovation pace | Direct curbs on asymmetric information, adverse selection, moral hazard |

**Limits of Monetary and Fiscal Policy**
| Tool | Limit |
|------|-------|
| Monetary policy | Interest rates **cannot fall below zero** (zero lower bound); ineffective in a **liquidity trap** |
| Fiscal policy | May hurt interest rates and prices (crowding out / inflation) |
- **Fiscal Policy** ⭐: Used to increase output through government purchases and taxes. Expansionary fiscal policy raises aggregate demand via tax cuts or ↑ government spending. ⭐
- **Monetary Policy** ⭐: Increase or decrease in money supply by the central bank to target macroeconomic variables (unemployment, output, inflation). Expansionary monetary policy = ↓ rates + ↑ liquidity. ⭐
- **Liquidity Trap**: Condition in which monetary policy is ineffective — increases in money supply do not lower interest rates further (because rates are already very low).
- **Zero Lower Bound**: Floor below which nominal interest rates cannot fall.

**Quick Recall:**
- Three tools: fiscal, monetary, regulatory — combine them
- Monetary fails at the **zero lower bound** and during **liquidity traps**
- Fiscal can crowd out and stoke inflation
- Recessions with financial crises last longer and cost more

**Quick Recall:**
- Financial sector channels funds, supports growth
- Speculation + asymmetric info + moral hazard = crisis triggers
- Bubbles burst → crises → policy must combine fiscal + monetary + regulatory

### Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) — Introduction & History 🔴
- Early 20th century — John Bates Clark and Alfred Marshall: theories on prices and information
- 1950s–1960s — Paul Samuelson, Burton Malkiel and others develop the modern theory
- **Eugene Fama (1965)** — "Random Walks in Stock Market Prices": argued stock prices follow a random walk; impossible to predict future prices from past prices or any available information
- **Eugene Fama (1970)** — formalised the **three forms** of EMH (weak, semi-strong, strong)
- Continues to be debated: some economists treat EMH as accurate; others believe markets are *not* fully efficient and that abnormal returns are achievable
- **Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH)** ⭐: Theory that financial markets are "informationally efficient" — asset prices reflect all available information at any given time, so investors cannot consistently earn abnormal returns by using available information. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Abnormal Returns** ⭐: Returns higher than what would be expected given the level of risk taken on. ⭐
- **Random Walk** ⭐: Stock prices evolve such that future prices cannot be predicted from past prices — Fama (1965). ⭐
- Builds on: financial-system / financial-markets discussion (Unit 24, Chunk 001)
- Prerequisite for: CAPM linkage (this chunk) and EMH critique (Chunk 003)

**Quick Recall:**
- EMH = prices reflect ALL available information
- Cannot consistently earn abnormal returns from public info
- Eugene Fama 1965 (random walk) and 1970 (three forms) — foundational
- Continues to be debated

### Three Forms of EMH 🔴
| Form | What is reflected in prices | What cannot generate consistent profits |
|------|------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Weak** | All historical price and volume data | Technical analysis (charts, past trends) cannot consistently profit |
| **Semi-Strong** | All publicly available information (price/volume + news, financial statements, economic data) | Fundamental analysis cannot consistently profit |
| **Strong** | All information — public **and private** | No analysis or even insider information can consistently earn abnormal returns |
- **Weak-Form EMH** ⭐: All historical price and volume data is already incorporated into current stock prices, making it impossible to profit from analysing past trends or patterns; technical analysis cannot generate consistent profits.
- **Semi-Strong-Form EMH** ⭐: All publicly available information is already reflected in stock prices; fundamental analysis cannot achieve abnormal returns.
- **Strong-Form EMH** ⭐: All information, both public and private, is reflected in prices; impossible to earn abnormal returns through any analysis or insider information.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Weak form means weak markets" → ✅ Correct: It means a *weaker* claim — only past price/volume data is in prices. Stronger forms make stronger claims.
- ❌ Mistake: "If strong form holds, weak form may not hold" → ✅ Correct: Strong form **subsumes** semi-strong, which **subsumes** weak.

**Quick Recall:**
- Weak: past prices in price → no technical analysis
- Semi-strong: public info in price → no fundamental analysis
- Strong: public + private in price → not even insider info works
- Strong ⊃ Semi-strong ⊃ Weak
---

### Assumptions of EMH 🔴
| # | Assumption | Meaning |
|---|------------|---------|
| 1 | **Rationality** | Investors maximise their interests; they do their best |
| 2 | **Information freely available** | Every investor has easy access to all market-related information without restrictions, **at zero cost** |
| 3 | **No information asymmetry** | Every investor has the same information — whatever A has, B has too |
| 4 | **Prices adjust quickly to new information** | Market response time is very short; new info shows up in prices and indices very quickly |
| 5 | **No individual investor can influence prices** | No investor or group can control prices and steer the market |
| 6 | **No transaction costs** | No transaction costs or trading obstacles prevent investors from acting on information |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "EMH assumes some investors are smarter" → ✅ Correct: EMH assumes **all investors have the same information** (no asymmetry) and act rationally.

**Quick Recall:**
- Six assumptions: rational, free info, no asymmetry, fast adjustment, no price-makers, no transaction costs
- Each assumption is a target for empirical/theoretical criticism

### EMH and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) 🔴
| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $E(R_i)$ | Expected return of asset i |
| $R_f$ | Risk-free rate of return (e.g., T-bills, government securities) |
| $E(R_m)$ | Expected return of market portfolio (e.g., SENSEX, NIFTY index) |
| $\beta_i$ | Measure of systematic risk of asset i |
| $E(R_m) - R_f$ | **Market risk premium** — excess return of market over risk-free |
1. Investors are rational
2. Investors diversify their portfolios across various assets/instruments
3. **The market is efficient**
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| $ER_{it} = R_{it} - R_{ft}$ | **Excess return** of security i over the risk-free return |
| $ER_{mt} = R_{mt} - R_{ft}$ | **Market risk premium** — excess return of market portfolio over risk-free return |
| $\alpha_i$ | Intercept |
| $\beta_i$ | Systematic risk |
| $u_{it}$ | Error term |
- **Null hypothesis**: $\alpha_i = 0$ for all n securities
- If $\alpha_i \neq 0$ statistically → market is **not efficient** (EMH rejected)
- If EMH holds, the **characteristic line** (graph of equation 25.3) should pass through the origin (Fig 25.2)
- **CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)** ⭐: Model stating that the expected return of an asset is linearly and positively related to its systematic risk. ⭐
- **Systematic Risk** ⭐: Part of total risk beyond the control of the firm or investor; determined by economic, social, and political factors. ⭐
- **Market Risk Premium** ⭐: $E(R_m) - R_f$ — excess return of the market portfolio over the risk-free return. ⭐
- **Excess Return**: Return on a security minus the risk-free rate.
- **Characteristic Line**: Graph of the regression of excess return on market risk premium; should pass through the origin under EMH.
- **Risk-Free Rate** ($R_f$): Return on a risk-free asset like Treasury bills or government securities.
- Builds on: Three Forms of EMH (Chunk 002), Unit 20 CAPM background (referenced as already-covered)
- Used by: Empirical EMH testing in Indian markets (Chunk 003)

**Quick Recall:**
- CAPM: $E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i [E(R_m) - R_f]$
- CAPM **assumes** EMH (market is efficient)
- Black–Jensen–Scholes (1972) test: regress excess return on market risk premium; null is $\alpha_i = 0$
- $\alpha_i \neq 0$ → reject EMH
- Characteristic line passes through origin if EMH holds
1. How do we reconcile the proliferation of fund managers claiming to beat the market with the empirical evidence often supporting EMH?
2. What does the failure of $\alpha_i = 0$ tell us about the *form* of inefficiency (weak / semi-strong / strong) being violated?

### Assessment of EMH — Six Criticisms 🔴
| # | Criticism | Source assumption violated |
|---|-----------|----------------------------|
| **a** | **Behavioural finance** — investors are not always rational; emotions, biases, and incomplete information drive decisions, causing inefficiencies | Rationality |
| **b** | **Historical contradictions** — anomalies like the **momentum effect** (past winners keep winning) contradict EMH's claim that past prices can't predict future prices | Random walk / weak-form |
| **c** | **Insider trading** — its existence shows some participants have information that the public doesn't | No information asymmetry / strong-form |
| **d** | **Market bubbles** — EMH says prices = intrinsic value, but the **dot-com bubble (late 1990s)** and **housing bubble (mid-2000s)** sent prices far above intrinsic values until they crashed | Prices reflect intrinsic value |
| **e** | **Information asymmetry** — in reality some investors have more or better information; this creates inefficiencies | No information asymmetry |
| **f** | **Limited arbitrage** — EMH expects arbitrage to correct mispricings, but real arbitrage is limited by transaction costs, liquidity constraints, and regulatory restrictions | No transaction costs |
- **Behavioural Finance** ⭐: Field studying how investors are not always rational; their decisions are derived from emotions, biases, or incomplete information, causing inefficiencies. ⭐
- **Momentum Effect** ⭐: Tendency of stocks that have performed well in the past to continue to perform well — contradicts EMH (specifically, weak-form). ⭐
- **Limited Arbitrage**: Real-world constraint where transaction costs, liquidity, and regulatory restrictions prevent arbitrage from fully correcting market inefficiencies.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "EMH says bubbles cannot occur" → ✅ Correct: EMH says prices = intrinsic value; observed bubbles like dot-com (late 1990s) and housing (mid-2000s) **contradict** EMH.

**Quick Recall:**
- 6 criticisms: behavioural, momentum, insider, bubbles, asymmetry, limited arbitrage
- Bubbles cited: **dot-com (late 1990s)** and **housing (mid-2000s)**
- Each criticism maps to violation of an EMH assumption
---
| # | Application | Implication |
|---|-------------|-------------|
| **a** | **Index Funds** | Cannot consistently outperform the market by stock-picking → invest in index funds tracking the overall market |
| **b** | **Passive Investing** | Buy and hold a diversified portfolio for the long term rather than time the market or pick stocks (track Sensex / Nifty) |
| **c** | **Rational Expectations Theory** | Closely related — individuals make decisions based on all available information; markets adjust rapidly to new info |
| **d** | **Academic Research** | EMH is a benchmark framework for testing hypotheses about market behaviour |
| **e** | **Regulatory Policy** | Role of authorities like SEBI may be **diluted** — if all info is in prices, attempts to regulate or prevent insider trading may be futile |
| **f** | **Investment Strategies** | Technical and fundamental analysis may be ineffective; focus on low-cost index funds, diversification, risk management |
- **Passive Investing** ⭐: Strategy that aims to track a market index (Sensex, Nifty) rather than try to beat it. Buy-and-hold a diversified portfolio for the long term.

**Quick Recall:**
- EMH ⇒ Index funds + passive investing
- EMH ⇒ Diluted case for SEBI / insider-trading regulation
- EMH ⇒ Technical and fundamental analysis ineffective

### Applicability of EMH in India — Empirical Studies 🔴
| Study | Form tested | Period | Finding |
|-------|-------------|--------|---------|
| **Bhatia, Gupta, Goyal (2017)** | Weak-form | 2006–2016 daily returns | Indian stock market is **NOT weak-form efficient** — predictable patterns exist that can be exploited for abnormal profits |
| **Kumar & Kamaiah (2017)** | Semi-strong-form | Event-study methodology | Indian stock market is **partially efficient** in reacting to news/events; some room for abnormal profits |

- ❌ Mistake: "All Indian studies say markets are inefficient" → ✅ Correct: Some studies (Gupta & Sehgal 2013; Bhattacharya & Mukherjee 2017) report **largely efficient** markets with limited anomalies.
- Builds on: Three forms (Chunk 002), Six criticisms (this chunk)

### Issues in Financial Stability 🔴
| # | Issue | What it is | How it threatens stability |
|---|-------|------------|----------------------------|
| **a** | **Systemic risk** | Risk imposing a barrier in the efficient financial system that can severely impact the entire economy | Contagion from one institution's failure → cascade through markets → collapse, loss of confidence |

- **Systemic Risk** ⭐: Risk that imposes a barrier in the efficient financial system, with severe impact on the entire economy. Sources: interconnections and interdependence between institutions/markets, and contagion effects from the failure of one institution. Refers to risk inherent to the entire market or market segment due to certain events (natural disasters, inflation, war). ⭐
- **Shadow Banking** ⭐: System where credit and related financial services are provided by entities that are **not regulated**, unlike banks. ⭐

- **International Spill-ins / Spill-outs**: Cross-border financial-shock transmission via interconnected markets — disruption in one country quickly affects others.
- **Too-Big-To-Fail (TBTF)** ⭐: Some institutions are so large that their failure would catastrophically harm the economy; their implicit guarantee creates moral hazard. ⭐

- ❌ Mistake: "Too-big-to-fail makes banks safer" → ✅ Correct: TBTF creates **moral hazard** — these banks take **excessive risks** because they expect to be bailed out.

### Unit 27 Introduction — What are NPAs? 🔴
- **Non-Performing Asset (NPA)** ⭐: Loan or advance that is in default or in arrears. A loan is in arrears when the principal is due or interest payments are late or missed. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Performing Asset / Standard Asset** ⭐: Asset generating regular income with the expected usual commercial risk; standard assets do not require supplementary provisions.
- **Loan in Default**: When the lender considers the loan agreement broken and the debtor unable to meet obligations.

**Quick Recall:**
- NPA = loan in default / arrears
- Reflects asset quality, credit risk, allocation efficiency
- 90 days non-repayment is the standard NPA classification trigger

### Profile of NPAs in India — Gross vs Net NPAs 🔴
- **Gross NPAs** ⭐: Indicates the absolute amount of the total value of loans that are due within 90 days in a particular quarter or financial year.
- **Net NPAs** ⭐: Net value of NPAs after the bank makes specific provisions for them. Arrived at by subtracting doubtful and unpaid assets (provisions) from gross NPAs.
- **Gross NPA Ratio** ⭐: Gross NPAs / Gross Advances.
| Metric | Meaning | What it shows |
|--------|---------|---------------|
| Gross NPAs | Absolute amount considered irrecoverable | Total NPA value for a particular financial year |
| Net NPAs | After provisioning | Actual burden on the bank |

**Quick Recall:**
- Gross NPAs = absolute irrecoverable amount
- Net NPAs = Gross − provisions (true burden)
- Gross NPA Ratio = Gross NPAs / Gross Advances

### Nature of NPAs — Three Categories by Period of Default 🔴
| Category | Period of default | Description |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Sub-Standard Asset** ⭐ | Outstanding ≤ 12 months as NPA | First-stage NPA |
| **Doubtful Asset** ⭐ | Outstanding > 12 months as NPA, already classified sub-standard | Higher-risk NPA |
| **Loss Asset** ⭐ | Uncollectible / little to no recovery; bank may consider some recovery value left as it has not been written off | Bank must record loss; entire loan written off |
1. **Overdraft / Cash-Credit (OD/CC) accounts** out of order for more than **90 days**
2. **Agricultural loans**:
   - Short-period crops: NPA if instalment of principal/interest unpaid for **two crop seasons**
   - Long-period crops: NPA if instalment of principal/interest unpaid for **one crop season**
3. **Any other account** with payment overdue more than 90 days
1. Loan becomes NPA after **90 days of non-repayment**
2. Bank asks borrower to liquidate assets / mortgage as per loan agreement
3. If no assets pledged → bank may write off as bad debt and **sell to a "bad bank"** (e.g., **NARCL — National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited**)
4. NARCL is a public-sector asset-reconstruction company in India established to acquire and aggregate NPAs
- **Sub-Standard Asset** ⭐: Loan/advance that has remained NPA for ≤ 12 months.
- **Doubtful Asset** ⭐: Loan/advance that has been NPA for > 12 months and was previously classified sub-standard.
- **Loss Asset** ⭐: Loan/advance that is uncollectible or has little/no recovery value, but has not yet been wholly written off — must record loss; entire amount must be written off completely.
- **NARCL (National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited)** ⭐: Public-sector asset-reconstruction company in India established to acquire and aggregate NPAs ("bad bank"). ⭐
- **Bad Bank**: Specialised institution that takes bad loans off bank balance sheets to ease the burden.

**Quick Recall:**
- Sub-standard ≤ 12 months
- Doubtful > 12 months (was sub-standard)
- Loss = uncollectible
- 90-day rule for general NPAs; 1–2 crop seasons for agriculture
- **NARCL** = India's "bad bank"

- **Tier-I and Tier-II banks have different provisioning norms**
- Helps maintain healthy book of accounts even with bad assets

### Exchange Rate — Direct vs Indirect Quotes 🔴
- **Direct quote**: how many units of *domestic* currency one unit of *foreign* currency buys
- **Indirect quote**: how many units of *foreign* currency one unit of *domestic* currency buys
- **Exchange Rate** ⭐: Price of the currency of a country (e.g., Indian rupee) in terms of the currency of another country (e.g., US dollar).
- **Direct Quote** ⭐: How many units of domestic currency can be bought by **one unit of foreign currency**. Example: $1 = ₹83.17. ⭐
- **Indirect Quote** ⭐: How many units of foreign currency can be bought by **one unit of domestic currency**. Example: ₹1 = $0.0120. ⭐
- **Currency Appreciation**: Domestic currency rises in value — one foreign currency unit buys *fewer* domestic currency units.
- **Currency Depreciation**: Domestic currency falls in value — one foreign currency unit buys *more* domestic currency units.
- $1 = ₹83.17 → direct quote (rupees per dollar)
- ₹1 = $0.0120 → indirect quote (dollars per rupee)
- "Dollar appreciates against rupee" → $1 buys more ₹

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Direct and indirect quotes are interchangeable terminology" → ✅ Correct: They are **reciprocals** but follow specific conventions — direct = domestic currency per foreign unit; indirect = foreign currency per domestic unit.

**Quick Recall:**
- Direct: ₹/$ (how much ₹ for $1)
- Indirect: $/₹ (how much $ for ₹1)
- Dollar appreciates → $1 buys more rupees

### Exchange Rate Systems — Fixed vs Floating 🔴
- Central bank (e.g., RBI) sets the **official exchange rate**
- Central bank ensures the actual rate stays at the official level
1. Equilibrium at E1: D ∩ S1 = ₹60/$
2. To bring rate down to ₹50/$, RBI **sells dollars** equal to Q1Q2 → supply shifts S1 → S2
3. New equilibrium E2 at ₹50/$ on D ∩ S2
4. **Arbitrage process**: arbitrageur buys $1 from RBI at ₹50, sells in market at ₹60 → profit ₹10/$
5. Total arbitrageur profit: ₹10 × Q1Q2
6. **Result**: RBI's foreign-exchange reserves **fall** by Q1Q2 dollars
7. Reverse case: if equilibrium rate < desired, RBI buys dollars → reserves **rise**
- When the foreign currency (dollar) is **overvalued** in the market (above target), maintaining the fixed rate **drains** FX reserves
- When undervalued, central bank **accumulates** reserves
- Equilibrium exchange rate determined by **demand for** and **supply of** foreign currency
- Equilibrium when demand = supply
1. **Initial**: D1 ∩ S1 at E1 → equilibrium rate e1
2. **↑ Supply**: S1 → S2 with constant D1 → new equilibrium E2 at rate e2 (e2 < e1, **rate falls**)
3. **↑ Demand**: D1 → D2 with constant S1 → new equilibrium E3 at rate e3 (e3 > e1, **rate rises**)
- ↑ supply (constant demand) → ↓ rate
- ↑ demand (constant supply) → ↑ rate
- Both can change simultaneously → rate fluctuates
| Aspect | Fixed | Floating |
|--------|-------|----------|
| Rate determined by | Central bank (official rate) | Market forces (demand & supply) |
| Stability | High — predictable | Low — volatile |
| Long-run sustainability | Difficult | Easier |
| Reserve impact | Drains reserves when defending overvalued foreign currency | None directly |
| Example mechanism | RBI sells/buys dollars to maintain ₹50/$ | E1 → E2 → E3 in response to demand/supply shifts |
- **Fixed Exchange Rate System** ⭐: Currency pegged to another currency or fixed value (e.g., gold); central bank maintains the rate; the rate is the **official exchange rate**. ⭐
- **Floating Exchange Rate System** ⭐: Exchange rate determined by market forces — demand for and supply of the foreign currency; equilibrium where demand = supply. ⭐
- **Equilibrium Exchange Rate** ⭐: Rate at which demand for foreign currency equals supply.
- **Arbitrage** ⭐: Buying a currency in one market and selling in another to profit from price differences.
- **Arbitrageur**: Person executing arbitrage trades.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "RBI prints rupees to defend a fixed rate" → ✅ Correct: RBI **sells (or buys) foreign-exchange reserves** (dollars) to defend the fixed rate; this **drains (or builds) reserves**.
- ❌ Mistake: "Floating rate means no central-bank involvement" → ✅ Correct: Pure floating has none, but most countries practise managed/dirty floating (next chunk).

**Quick Recall:**
- Fixed: official rate set by central bank; RBI sells dollars to keep rate down → reserves fall
- Floating: market-driven equilibrium; ↑D → ↑rate; ↑S → ↓rate
- Arbitrage profit example: buy $1 at ₹50, sell at ₹60 → ₹10/$ profit

### Real Interest Rate, Saving and Investment in a Small Open Economy 🔴

**From Y = C + I + G + NX to S̄ − I(r*) = NX**

**Three cases (Fig 28.3 — Real Interest Rate, Saving and Investment)**
| World Rate vs Domestic | Investment | Trade Balance | Distance on Fig 28.3 |
|------------------------|-----------|---------------|----------------------|
| **r* > r** | I < S̄ → excess saving exported | **Trade surplus** | AB |
| **r* < r** | I > S̄ → must import capital | **Trade deficit** | CD |
| **r* = r** | I = S̄ | **Zero trade balance** | At E |
- **Small Open Economy** ⭐: An economy that takes the world real interest rate r* as given — it cannot influence world prices/rates with its own actions. ⭐
- **Trade Surplus** ⭐: NX > 0 — country exports more than it imports; arises when r* > r (excess saving lent abroad).
- **Trade Deficit** ⭐: NX < 0 — country imports more than it exports; arises when r* < r (capital inflow funds excess investment).
1. World rate r* > domestic rate r → I(r*) < S̄ → excess S̄ flows out → **trade surplus** (AB on Fig 28.3)
2. World rate r* < domestic rate r → I(r*) > S̄ → capital imported → **trade deficit** (CD on Fig 28.3)
3. World rate r* = r → I = S̄ → **zero trade balance** (E)

### Concept of Behavioural Finance 🔴

### Difference Between Traditional and Behavioural Finance 🔴
| Dimension | Traditional Finance | Behavioural Finance |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Investor decision basis** | Rational, unbiased information processing | Influenced by overconfidence, familiarity bias, herding, etc.; emotions and psychology may make investors irrational |
| **Information assumption** | Complete information available to all; investors examine it rationally; investors always make right decisions | **Bounded rationality** — investors don't process all info; they **satisfice instead of maximise**; even with accurate info, they err in judgment |
| **Market efficiency** | EMH — markets are efficient; price reflects fundamental value | EMH disputed; anomalies cause volatility unexplainable by EMH |
| **Self-control** | Investors have **perfect self-control**; past/personality don't influence | Self-control problems exist and affect both investor and market |
| **Arbitrage** | Any "noise" balanced out by rational traders | **Limit to arbitrage**; prices not always right; non-equilibrium can persist |
- **Bounded Rationality** ⭐: Investors don't process all available information; they **satisfice** (settle for "good enough") rather than maximise. ⭐
- **Limit to Arbitrage** ⭐: Behavioural finance proposition that arbitrage cannot fully correct mispricing — prices may stay in non-equilibrium for periods. ⭐
- **Satisficing**: Choosing an acceptable rather than optimal outcome.
- **Herding example**: When buying stocks, investors follow others "because they think everyone else knows more" → herding mentality → sub-optimal decisions

**Quick Recall:**
- Traditional: rational + complete info + EMH + self-control + arbitrage corrects all
- Behavioural: bounded rationality + biases + EMH-failures + self-control problems + limit to arbitrage
- Investors **satisfice** rather than maximise
- "Rational, irrational" is the right pair (CYP2 Q3 → answer b)
- "Complete information available to everyone" is **NOT** behavioural — it's traditional (CYP2 Q4 → answer a)

### Efficient Market Hypothesis and Its Critique 🔴
- **Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)** ⭐: Market prices fully reflect all available information; current prices are the best approximation of a company's intrinsic value. Developed independently by **Paul A. Samuelson** and **Eugene F. Fama** in the **1960s**. ⭐
- **Random Walk Theory** ⭐: Past stock-price movements/trends cannot predict future movement; impossible to outperform market without additional risk. Logic: news is unpredictable, so today's situation cannot predict future values. ⭐
- **Bandwagon Effect / Momentum**: Investors follow market trends — rising price → more investment → further price rise.
- **Irrational Exuberance**: Investor enthusiasm pushing stock price above fundamental value.
- **Under-reaction**: Investors take time to update beliefs about a stock; full impact realised over a period → positive correlation in prices (not random).

**EMH "currency-note-on-the-road" intuition**

**Behavioural critique of randomness**
1. **Bandwagon effect** — herding into rising stocks → momentum
2. **Under-reaction** — investors take time to update beliefs → news is gradually priced in → **positive serial correlation** (changes in prices are NOT random)
3. **Behavioural biases** — sociological, psychological, cognitive influences cause behavioural anomalies
| Puzzle | What it says |
|--------|--------------|
| **Equity Premium Puzzle** | Stocks have earned disproportionately higher returns than bonds, yet investors are relatively unwilling to hold them |
| **Volatility Puzzle** | Stock returns are highly variable with **large dispersions** |
| **Predictability Puzzle** | Stock returns are predictable — persistent profits NOT eroded by arbitrage |
- **Herding**, **overconfidence**, **overreaction**, **loss aversion**, **mental accounting** — these dominate investor thinking and produce the three puzzles above.

**Quick Recall:**
- > - **EMH** = Samuelson & Fama, 1960s ⭐
- > - EMH ↔ **Random Walk**: past prices ≠ predictor of future ⭐
- Behavioural counter: momentum + under-reaction → positive serial correlation
- 3 puzzles: **equity premium, volatility, predictability**
- 5 dominant biases: herding, overconfidence, overreaction, loss aversion, mental accounting

### Market Anomalies — Three Types 🔴
- **Market Anomaly** ⭐: A deviation from the predictions of EMH that is too widespread, systematic, and fundamental to be dismissed as random error. (Tversky & Kahneman 1986). ⭐

**(i) Fundamental Anomalies**
| Anomaly | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Value Anomaly** | Over-estimation of future earnings and returns of growth companies |
| **Low Price-to-Book** | Stocks with high book-to-market generate **less return** than stocks with low price-to-book |
| **High Dividend Yield** | Positive correlation between yield and stock return; high-dividend-yield stocks **outperform** the market |
| **Neglected Stocks** | Prior neglected stocks generate **more return** subsequently; prior best performers underperform the index |
| **Low Price-to-Earnings (P/E)** | Low P/E stocks tend to **outperform**; high P/E stocks tend to **underperform** |

**(ii) Calendar/Seasonal Anomalies**
| Anomaly | Pattern |
|---------|---------|
| **Weekend Effect** | Monday closing < previous Friday closing |
| **Turn-of-the-Month Effect** | Stock prices rise in **last trading day** of month + **first three days** of next month |
| **Turn-of-the-Year Effect** | Prices and trading volume rise in **last week of December** + **first half of January** |
| **January Effect** | Small-company stocks generate more return than other assets in **first 2–3 weeks of January** |

**(iii) Technical Anomalies**
| Anomaly | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Moving Averages** | Short-period MA crossing **above** long-period MA = **buy** signal; crossing **below** = **sell** signal |
| **Trading Range Break** | Identify support/resistance + price trends → entry/exit points; "breakout" = potential trading opportunity |

- 5 fundamental: Value, Low P/B, High Div Yield, Neglected, Low P/E
- 4 calendar: Weekend, Turn-of-Month, Turn-of-Year, January
- 2 technical: Moving Averages, Trading Range Break

### Market Puzzles and Behavioural Explanations 🔴
1. **Loss aversion** — investors are more sensitive to losses than to gains
2. **Myopic loss aversion** — investors evaluate their portfolio frequently (even when saving for retirement); frequent evaluation amplifies the felt impact of short-term losses
3. **Ambiguity aversion** — preference for **known** risks over **unknown** risks; equity-return distribution is unknown, so people overweigh probability of returns from bonds
- Investors **overweigh recent information** and **consensus beliefs**
- Investors seek **confirming evidence** → under-react or overreact in the market
- **Focal points** — investors focus more on similar pieces of information
- **Loss aversion** — overweighing of negative information leads to **steep falls** (rather than gradual upward correction)
- **Disposition effect** — tendency to prematurely sell assets that have made financial gains while holding on to losing assets
- **Equity Premium Puzzle** ⭐: Stocks outperform bonds yet investors still hold bonds — unexplainable by pure risk aversion (Mehra & Prescott). ⭐
- **Loss Aversion** ⭐: Investors are more sensitive to losses than to gains. ⭐
- **Myopic Loss Aversion** ⭐: Frequent portfolio evaluation amplifies short-run loss aversion. ⭐
- **Ambiguity Aversion** ⭐: Preference for known risks over unknown risks. ⭐
- **Volatility Puzzle** ⭐: Stock-price changes far exceed fundamentals changes — high variability with large dispersions. ⭐
- **Predictability Puzzle** ⭐: Stock returns predictable from price-dividend ratios; persistent profits not eroded by arbitrage. ⭐
- **Prospect Theory**: Kahneman framework explaining loss aversion and risk attitudes around gains/losses (used to derive equity premium puzzle explanation).

### Heuristics — Concept and Origin 🔴
- **Heuristics** ⭐: Imperfect but functional shortcuts ("rules of thumb") that help investors simplify complex situations and make quick decisions. ⭐
- **Substitution** (Kahneman): When faced with a difficult problem, individuals replace it with an easier question and answer that instead. *Example*: instead of "how much profit will this stock make?" → "what do I feel about this stock?" (uses **affect heuristic**).
- **Bias**: Systematic and predictable error introduced by heuristic substitution.
- **Econs vs Humans**: "Econs" = traditional rational decision-makers; "humans" = real-world decision-makers influenced by cognitive/social/emotional factors.
- **1950s** — **Herbert A. Simon** introduced the concept of heuristics; argued there are limitations to rational decision-making (bounded rationality)
- **1970s** — **Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman** worked on heuristics to understand cognitive biases; explained why intuitive judgement deviates from logical rules

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Heuristics are inherently bad and should be avoided" → ✅ Correct: Heuristics are **functional** shortcuts — they enable quick decisions in complex situations. The problem is that **substitution** causes systematic biases as a side effect.

**Quick Recall:**
- Heuristic = imperfect but functional shortcut
- Origin: **Simon (1950s)** → **Tversky & Kahneman (1970s)**
- Mechanism: **substitution** of hard question with easier one
- Side effect: systematic & predictable **biases**
- Builds on: Bounded rationality (Chunk 009)

### Four Prominent Heuristics 🔴
- **Definition** ⭐: People use a **starting point ("the anchor")** and make adjustments until they reach an acceptable value. The anchor's value can be **statistically irrelevant**, yet still influences the final estimate; adjustments are typically insufficient.
- **Definition** ⭐ (Kahneman & Tversky 1972): In situations of uncertainty, people evaluate the probability of an uncertain event by the degree to which it is (i) **similar in essential properties** to its parent population and (ii) **reflects salient features** of the process by which it is generated. People decide based on representative information rather than logical, probabilistic reasoning.
| Error | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Neglecting Base Rate** | People ignore general/base-rate information when specific info is also provided |
| **Conjunction Fallacy** | Assuming specific conditions are more likely than general conditions |
| **Law of Small Numbers** | Exaggerating the degree to which a small sample resembles its population |
- Investors see past performance as representative of future performance
- They **overweight recent information** to the detriment of past information
- A few profitable years → assumption of continued great performance
- Sometimes leads to **stock-market overreactions and underreactions**
- **Definition** ⭐: People assess the probability of an event based on the **ease with which it comes to mind**. Principle: "if you can think of it, it must be important." Things easily recalled are believed to be more common and accurate representations of reality.
- Recent market news/events affect investment decisions more than fundamentals
- Often causes **overreactions** → market crashes or bubbles
- Investors overestimate probability of similar events recurring
- Originally noticed in **basketball** — players scoring maximum baskets keep getting maximum passes (even if average players)
- Generalizes to unrelated events (dice rolls, coin flips)
- In financial markets: investors prefer traders who have recently performed well, even though **past performance ≠ future performance**
- **Definition** ⭐: People assign probabilities of an event based on **how a person feels about it**. Good feelings → higher probability assigned to good consequences; bad feelings → higher probability assigned to bad outcomes.
- Emotions and mood drive decisions
- People take **more risky decisions when happy** (positive state of mind)
- Most prominent when people lack **time or resources to reflect**
- Can lead to **overconfidence bias**
| Heuristic | Mechanism | Typical Bias |
|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| **Anchoring & Adjustment** | Use a starting value, adjust insufficiently | Estimates biased toward (often irrelevant) anchor |
| **Representativeness** | Judge by similarity to prototype | Base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, law of small numbers, overreaction/underreaction |
| **Availability** | Probability ≈ ease of recall | Overweighing recent events; "hot hand" bias |
| **Affect** | Probability ≈ how it feels | Emotion-driven decisions; overconfidence |

**Quick Recall:**
- > - **4 heuristics**: Anchoring, Representativeness, Availability, Affect ⭐
- Anchoring → reduces cognitive load; insufficient adjustments
- Representativeness → 3 errors: base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, law of small numbers
- Availability → "if you can think of it, it must be important"; **hot hand** bias
- Affect → emotion-driven; risky decisions when happy

### Cognitive Influences on Investor Behaviour 🔴

**(i) Over-Confidence**
- Often arises from **self-attribution bias** — attributing successes to one's ability and failures to external factors
- Leads to **excessive trading** — overconfident investors buy too often, must sell too often to free capital
- Reinforced by **hindsight bias** — believing one "knew it would happen" after the fact (e.g., "I knew the market would crash")
- Hindsight bias makes investors believe they have exceptional foresight → uncalculated risks

**(ii) Cognitive Dissonance (Festinger)**
- Changing past values or feelings
- Justifying the decision

**(iii) Under-reaction and Overreaction**
- **Under-reaction**: When new info contradicts existing beliefs, investors **underweigh** it; adjust over weeks
- **Over-reaction**: When new info **aligns** with existing beliefs, investors overreact
- Both produce sub-optimal decisions and **stock-market volatility**

**(iv) Disposition Effect**
- Tendency to **prematurely sell winners** while **holding on to losers** (in hope of recovery)
- Investors **lose out on momentum gains** when they sell winning assets too quickly
- **Kahneman's Prospect Theory explanation**: Investors become **risk-averse after gains** (lock in profit) and **risk-seeking after losses** (hold losing assets, hoping to recover)

**(v) Diversification Bias**
|-------|-------------|
| **Over-diversification** | Choosing variety when making **simultaneous** future choices; overestimating need for diversity → diversifying when concentrated investment would be optimal |
| **Insufficient diversification** | **Home bias** — heavy investment in companies from one's home country, driven by **availability heuristic + familiarity bias**; no economic justification but familiarity reduces perceived risk |

**(vi) Mental Accounting**
- Investors classify money into separate "**mental accounts**" based on **subjective criteria**
- Both **safe** and **speculative** investments kept in different mental accounts so negative speculative returns don't "affect" positive safe returns
- Investors hold **money they can afford to lose** for speculation
- **Rationally, there should be no division** between "safety capital" and "money I can afford to lose"
- Leads to financially counterproductive decisions
- **Self-Attribution Bias** ⭐: Attributing successes to oneself and failures to external factors. ⭐
- **Hindsight Bias** ⭐: Belief, after an event, that one knew the outcome before it happened. ⭐
- **Cognitive Dissonance** ⭐ (Festinger): Internal tension/anxiety from conflicting beliefs; reduced by changing beliefs or justifying decisions. ⭐
- **Under-reaction**: Investors underweigh new information that conflicts with prior beliefs.
- **Over-reaction**: Investors overweigh new information that confirms prior beliefs.
- **Disposition Effect** ⭐: Sell winners early, hold losers (already defined Chunk 009 wrap-up).
- **Diversification Bias** ⭐: Choosing more variety than optimal when picking simultaneous items; opposite error: home bias / under-diversification. ⭐
- **Home Bias**: Heavy investment in domestic companies driven by availability + familiarity.
- **Mental Accounting** ⭐: Cognitive operations classifying money into different mental accounts based on subjective criteria. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Cognitive dissonance is felt only after big losses" → ✅ Correct: Cognitive dissonance arises from any **conflicting beliefs**; investors experience it whenever an outcome contradicts their prior view (selling losers late is the canonical example).
- ❌ Mistake: "Mental accounting is rational risk management" → ✅ Correct: There should be **no division** between "safe" and "speculative" capital — money is fungible. Mental accounting often produces irrational, counterproductive choices.

**Quick Recall:**
- > - **6 cognitive influences**: Overconfidence, Cognitive Dissonance, Under/Overreaction, Disposition Effect, Diversification Bias, Mental Accounting ⭐ (CYP4 Q1)
- Overconfidence ← self-attribution + hindsight bias
- Disposition effect ← Prospect Theory: risk-averse after gains, risk-seeking after losses
- Diversification bias = both over-diversification AND home bias
- Self-attribution bias = success self-credited, failure blamed externally (CYP4 Q3b)

### Social Influences on Investor Behaviour 🔴

**(i) Herding**
- In financial markets, herding generates **speculative bubbles** when traders track the decisions of others rather than fundamental values
- Investors with herding instincts follow others rather than their own analysis
- Causes: **asset bubbles, panic buying or selling**
- Outcome of **social learning** — believing others are better informed
- During financial speculation, investors buy at high prices not because they believe the asset is worth it but because they believe **others** believe it is

**(ii) Reputation**
- Herding emerges as a **reputation concern**
- **Keynes**: it is rational to follow the crowd because it preserves reputation
- *"It is better to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally"*

**(iii) Beauty Contests (Keynes)**
- Speculators care about **identifying short-term investment plans of others**, NOT what they themselves think is the best investment
- Asset prices subjected to **financial loops** → financial instability
- Instability magnified when borrowings fund the assets

**(iv) Success Stories and Advice from Family**
- People invest **more** when neighbours and family have profited from stocks
- Asymmetric: investors are NOT discouraged by failures of those around them
- Reason: **people share success stories more than failures**

**(v) Expert Influence**
- Analysts may have incentives to tout particular shares or are influenced by other analysts
- Leads to **herding** and inferior returns
- **Herding** ⭐: Outcome of social learning when people believe others are better informed and thus follow the crowd. ⭐
- **Beauty Contest** (Keynes): Iterated-reasoning analogy in financial speculation — what others think will be popular, not what is fundamentally best.
- **Financial Loops**: Self-reinforcing speculative price cycles, magnified by borrowing.

**Quick Recall:**
- **5 social influences**: Herding, Reputation, Beauty Contests, Success Stories from Family, Expert Influence
- > - Herding = **social learning** outcome; "others know better" assumption ⭐ (CYP4 Q3a)
- Keynes: "**Better to fail conventionally than succeed unconventionally**"
- **Beauty contest** = iterated reasoning; predict what others predict
- Success stories outweigh failure stories in investor influence

### Emotional Influences on Investor Behaviour 🔴

**(i) Feelings of Regret**
- **Theory of Regret**: Emotional reaction after realising one has made a wrong decision
- When an investment goes bad, investors **avoid selling** to **avoid feelings of regret**
- Investors reduce regret by **following others** — losing money in popular investments feels less embarrassing than losing in unpopular ones
- Connected to **cognitive dissonance** (above, Cognitive Influences)

**(ii) Loss Aversion**
- Investors find losses **disproportionately painful** compared with the pleasure of equivalent gains
- Makes people **prefer things as they are** (status quo bias)
- Causes divergence between **willingness to pay** and **willingness to accept**:
  - People pay **less** for an object they do not own
  - But demand **more** when selling the same object they own
- Example: An antique art piece worth ₹50,000 — you wouldn't pay ₹50,000 to buy it but would expect more if selling

**(iii) Sunk Cost Fallacy**
- **Emotional tendency to invest more money, time, effort** into a failing project because of prior investment
- **Closely linked to loss aversion** — emotional involvement prevents accepting failure
- Leads to two further mistakes:
  - **Aggressive Investing**: Risk-averse investors become aggressive **after** losing money → take undue risks → huge losses
  - **Averaging**: Investing more in losing stocks to "average down" → larger losses
- **Theory of Regret** ⭐: When an investment goes bad, people act irrationally by avoiding selling to avoid feelings of regret. ⭐
- **Loss Aversion** ⭐: Investors find losses disproportionately painful compared to equivalent gains; makes people prefer things as they are. ⭐
- **Willingness to Pay vs Willingness to Accept**: Loss aversion's manifestation — own → demand more; not own → pay less.
- **Sunk Cost Fallacy** ⭐: Emotional tendency to invest more money/time/effort into a failing project because we have already invested. ⭐
- **Aggressive Investing** (sunk-cost related): Risk-averse investors becoming risk-takers after losses.
- **Averaging** (sunk-cost related): Investing more in losing stocks to lower average cost — usually compounds losses.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Loss aversion just means you don't like losing" → ✅ Correct: Loss aversion means losses feel **disproportionately worse** than equivalent gains feel good — it's an asymmetry, not just dislike.
- ❌ Mistake: "Sunk cost fallacy is the same as risk aversion" → ✅ Correct: Sunk cost fallacy often leads investors **toward** risk (aggressive investing, averaging) — it's emotional commitment to past decisions, not risk preference.

**Quick Recall:**
- **3 emotional influences**: Regret, Loss Aversion, Sunk Cost Fallacy
- Loss aversion: asymmetric — losses **disproportionately** painful (CYP4 Q3d)
- Sunk cost → **aggressive investing** + **averaging** (both compound losses)
- Regret avoidance is one reason investors herd (links to social influences)
