## Section: Assessment of EMH — Six Criticisms 🔴

### Core Idea
EMH has dominated academic finance but has drawn six major criticisms. Each criticism points to a real-world deviation from one of EMH's tight assumptions: investors aren't always rational; price patterns persist; insiders profit; bubbles form; information is unequal; and arbitrage is limited. Taken together, these critiques suggest **market inefficiencies may be more common than EMH suggests**.

> **In Simple Terms:** EMH says markets are flawless calculators of information. Critics list six everyday cases where the calculator misfires — emotions, momentum, insider edges, bubbles, hidden info, and friction.

### Six Criticisms

| # | Criticism | Source assumption violated |
|---|-----------|----------------------------|
| **a** | **Behavioural finance** — investors are not always rational; emotions, biases, and incomplete information drive decisions, causing inefficiencies | Rationality |
| **b** | **Historical contradictions** — anomalies like the **momentum effect** (past winners keep winning) contradict EMH's claim that past prices can't predict future prices | Random walk / weak-form |
| **c** | **Insider trading** — its existence shows some participants have information that the public doesn't | No information asymmetry / strong-form |
| **d** | **Market bubbles** — EMH says prices = intrinsic value, but the **dot-com bubble (late 1990s)** and **housing bubble (mid-2000s)** sent prices far above intrinsic values until they crashed | Prices reflect intrinsic value |
| **e** | **Information asymmetry** — in reality some investors have more or better information; this creates inefficiencies | No information asymmetry |
| **f** | **Limited arbitrage** — EMH expects arbitrage to correct mispricings, but real arbitrage is limited by transaction costs, liquidity constraints, and regulatory restrictions | No transaction costs |

### Definitions
- **Behavioural Finance** ⭐: Field studying how investors are not always rational; their decisions are derived from emotions, biases, or incomplete information, causing inefficiencies. ⭐
- **Momentum Effect** ⭐: Tendency of stocks that have performed well in the past to continue to perform well — contradicts EMH (specifically, weak-form). ⭐
- **Limited Arbitrage**: Real-world constraint where transaction costs, liquidity, and regulatory restrictions prevent arbitrage from fully correcting market inefficiencies.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "EMH says bubbles cannot occur" → ✅ Correct: EMH says prices = intrinsic value; observed bubbles like dot-com (late 1990s) and housing (mid-2000s) **contradict** EMH.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 6 criticisms: behavioural, momentum, insider, bubbles, asymmetry, limited arbitrage
> - Bubbles cited: **dot-com (late 1990s)** and **housing (mid-2000s)**
> - Each criticism maps to violation of an EMH assumption

## Section: Applications of EMH 🟡

### Core Idea
EMH influences six practical domains, mainly nudging investors toward **passive strategies**. If you can't beat the market, ride it — invest in index funds, hold long-term, diversify. EMH also shapes academic research and dilutes the case for some kinds of regulation.

### Six Applications

| # | Application | Implication |
|---|-------------|-------------|
| **a** | **Index Funds** | Cannot consistently outperform the market by stock-picking → invest in index funds tracking the overall market |
| **b** | **Passive Investing** | Buy and hold a diversified portfolio for the long term rather than time the market or pick stocks (track Sensex / Nifty) |
| **c** | **Rational Expectations Theory** | Closely related — individuals make decisions based on all available information; markets adjust rapidly to new info |
| **d** | **Academic Research** | EMH is a benchmark framework for testing hypotheses about market behaviour |
| **e** | **Regulatory Policy** | Role of authorities like SEBI may be **diluted** — if all info is in prices, attempts to regulate or prevent insider trading may be futile |
| **f** | **Investment Strategies** | Technical and fundamental analysis may be ineffective; focus on low-cost index funds, diversification, risk management |

### Definitions
- **Passive Investing** ⭐: Strategy that aims to track a market index (Sensex, Nifty) rather than try to beat it. Buy-and-hold a diversified portfolio for the long term.
- **Index Fund**: Mutual fund or ETF that tracks the performance of a market index.
- **Rational Expectations Theory**: Theory that individuals make decisions based on all available information and markets adjust rapidly to new information — closely related to EMH.

### Connections
- Builds on: Three Forms of EMH (Chunk 002)
- Tension with: Six Criticisms (this chunk) — empirical evidence of inefficiencies undercuts the case for pure passive investing

> **Quick Recall:**
> - EMH ⇒ Index funds + passive investing
> - EMH ⇒ Diluted case for SEBI / insider-trading regulation
> - EMH ⇒ Technical and fundamental analysis ineffective

## Section: Applicability of EMH in India — Empirical Studies 🔴

### Core Idea
Indian-market evidence is **mixed**: weak-form efficiency is rejected by some studies; semi-strong efficiency is partial; strong-form is rejected because insiders earn abnormal profits. During the 2008 global financial crisis the market behaved largely efficiently with some anomalies. Overall the Indian market is "largely efficient" but with exploitable anomalies — a partial-EMH picture.

> **In Simple Terms:** Indian markets are mostly but not completely efficient. Past prices, news, and insider info can sometimes still produce abnormal returns.

### Indian Empirical Studies

| Study | Form tested | Period | Finding |
|-------|-------------|--------|---------|
| **Bhatia, Gupta, Goyal (2017)** | Weak-form | 2006–2016 daily returns | Indian stock market is **NOT weak-form efficient** — predictable patterns exist that can be exploited for abnormal profits |
| **Kumar & Kamaiah (2017)** | Semi-strong-form | Event-study methodology | Indian stock market is **partially efficient** in reacting to news/events; some room for abnormal profits |
| **Patro, Paudyal & Kallapur (2009)** | Strong-form | Insider-trading activity | Insiders generate abnormal profits → market is **NOT strong-form efficient** |
| **Gupta & Sehgal (2013)** | Efficiency during 2008 GFC | Crisis period | Market was **largely efficient** during crisis, with some anomalies |
| **Bhattacharya & Mukherjee (2017)** | Combined technical + fundamental | 2006–2016 | Market **largely efficient** with some exploitable anomalies |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Indian markets are fully efficient" → ✅ Correct: Empirical studies show Indian markets are **only partially efficient** — weak-form is rejected by Bhatia et al. (2017), and strong-form is rejected by Patro et al. (2009).
- ❌ Mistake: "All Indian studies say markets are inefficient" → ✅ Correct: Some studies (Gupta & Sehgal 2013; Bhattacharya & Mukherjee 2017) report **largely efficient** markets with limited anomalies.

### Connections
- Builds on: Three forms (Chunk 002), Six criticisms (this chunk)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Bhatia et al. (2017) → NOT weak-form efficient (2006–2016)
> - Kumar & Kamaiah (2017) → partial semi-strong efficiency
> - Patro et al. (2009) → NOT strong-form efficient (insider profits)
> - Gupta & Sehgal (2013) → largely efficient during 2008 crisis, with anomalies

## Section: Unit 25 Summary 🟢

### Core Idea
EMH says a stock cannot outperform the market in an efficient market. Three forms — weak (past prices in price), semi-strong (public info in price), strong (public + private info in price). CAPM predicts asset return is linearly and positively related to systematic risk; CAPM **assumes EMH holds** and CAPM can be used to **test** EMH. Black, Jensen, Scholes (1972) gave a popular technique.

### Definitions (Unit 25 keywords consolidated)
- **EMH**: Theory that financial markets are efficient — asset prices fully reflect all available information at any given time.
- **Weak-Form EMH**: All historical price/volume data is in current stock prices; technical analysis cannot consistently profit.
- **Semi-Strong-Form EMH**: All publicly available information is in stock prices; fundamental analysis cannot achieve abnormal returns.
- **Strong-Form EMH**: All info — public and private — is in stock prices; no analysis or insider information can produce abnormal returns.
- **CAPM**: Expected return of an asset is linearly and positively related to its systematic (undiversifiable) risk.
- **Characteristic Line**: A straight line formed using regression analysis that summarises a particular security's systematic risk and rate of return.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 forms: weak ⊂ semi-strong ⊂ strong
> - CAPM uses EMH; CAPM tests EMH
> - Black–Jensen–Scholes (1972) is the classic test

## Section: Unit 26 — Financial Stability: Introduction 🔴

### Core Idea
Financial stability is one of the **two key objectives of the RBI** (alongside macroeconomic stability). After the 2008 global financial crisis — which deceived Indian growth, hit exports, increased stock-market volatility, and grew banking-sector NPAs — the RBI began rigorous focus on financial stability. RBI uses regulation, risk-management improvement, supervisory frameworks, and dedicated bodies (such as the **Financial Stability and Development Council, FSDC, est. 2010**) to monitor banks, NBFCs, and other financial components for stress and risk.

> **In Simple Terms:** "Financial stability" is the RBI's job of making sure the financial plumbing keeps working when things go wrong. Post-2008, the RBI took it much more seriously and built dedicated bodies for it.

### Key Facts
- **Two RBI objectives**: financial stability and macroeconomic stability
- **2008 Global Financial Crisis** — Indian impact: deceleration in growth, decline in exports, stock-market volatility, growing NPAs in banking
- **FSDC (Financial Stability and Development Council)** — established 2010 to coordinate financial regulators and stakeholders in India
- RBI tools: regulatory strengthening, risk-management practices, supervisory/monitoring framework

### Definitions
- **Financial Stability** ⭐: A financial system that is resilient to any shock or disruptions. Achieved when market volatility is low; ensures efficient functioning of the financial system, promotes economic growth, and reduces poverty. ⭐
- **Financial System Components**: Financial institutions (banks, NBFCs), financial markets, financial instruments.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Two RBI objectives: financial + macroeconomic stability
> - FSDC established **2010** to coordinate Indian financial regulators
> - Post-2008 GFC triggered RBI's intensified financial-stability focus

## Section: Concept of Financial Stability 🔴

### Core Idea
Financial stability means the financial system is **resilient to shocks** with low market volatility. It enables efficient functioning, supports growth, and reduces poverty. Four conditions characterise a financially stable economy: (1) **sufficient capital** for institutions to meet capital adequacy norms, (2) **sufficient risk-management** systems against credit/liquidity/market risk, (3) **well-developed infrastructure** for trading and payment settlement, and (4) **robust regulatory framework** for legal compliance.

### Four Conditions of Financial Stability
| # | Condition | Purpose |
|---|-----------|---------|
| a | Sufficient capital availability | Capital adequacy norms met |
| b | Sufficient risk-management system | Protection against credit, liquidity, market risk |
| c | Well-developed trading/payment-settlement infrastructure | Efficient transactions |
| d | Robust regulatory framework | Legal compliance; laws not overlooked |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Stable system = resilient + low volatility + supports growth and poverty reduction
> - Four conditions: capital, risk management, infrastructure, regulation

## Section: Factors Affecting Financial Stability — Seven Factors 🔴

### Core Idea
Seven major factors bear on India's financial stability. They span **macroeconomic conditions** (growth, inflation, rates, FX, fiscal/monetary policy), **risk types** (default, operational, liquidity), **asset markets** (sudden price changes / bubbles), and **institutional / global** drivers (regulatory framework, global linkages).

### Seven Factors (from Fig 26.1)

| Factor | Effect on stability |
|--------|---------------------|
| **a. Macroeconomic factors** | Growth, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, monetary/fiscal policy. Rising inflation + rising rates → less credit → high default rates → instability |
| **b. Default risk factor** | High loan defaults → liquidity crisis at banks → loss of saver/investor confidence |
| **c. Unexpected change in asset prices** | Rapid asset-price rises → bubbles → bursts → instability (e.g., dot-com, housing) |
| **d. Operational risk factor** | Operational inefficiency → ↓ profits or losses → can't meet routine/operational expenses → threatens stability |
| **e. Liquidity risk factor** | Inability to meet short-term obligations (e.g., mass withdrawals) → liquidity issues → instability |
| **f. Lack of robust legal & regulatory framework** | (Implied from Fig 26.1 — covered in following chunk) |
| **g. Global factors** | (Implied from Fig 26.1 — covered in following chunk) |

### Definitions
- **Default Risk Factor**: Risk that borrowers default on loans / credit instruments — high defaults → bank liquidity crisis → loss of confidence in financial system.
- **Operational Risk** ⭐: Risk arising from operational inefficiency — decreases operational profits, may produce losses, threatens institution's ability to meet routine expenses.
- **Asset Bubble Factor**: Rapid asset-price rises that may form bubbles which burst suddenly, destabilising the system.
