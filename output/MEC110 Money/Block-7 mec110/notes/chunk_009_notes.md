## Section: Concept of Behavioural Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
Traditional finance assumes "**efficient markets**" — prices reflect fundamental value and all available information; investors are **rational, self-controlled and self-interested** ("homo economicus"). Traditional theory failed to explain market volatility, crashes, irrational investor behaviour, and bubbles. **Behavioural Finance** is the alternative paradigm: it focuses on how psychological and sociological factors shape investor decisions and acknowledges that **investors are not always rational and prices are not always efficient**.

> **In Simple Terms:** Traditional finance treats investors like calculators — perfectly rational, no emotions. Behavioural finance treats them like real humans — sometimes scared, sometimes greedy, often following the crowd. Real markets need the second view to make sense of bubbles and crashes.

### Definitions
- **Behavioural Finance** ⭐: A field that focuses on cognitive, sociological and psychological influences on the decision-making of investors and financial markets. ⭐
- **Homo Economicus** ⭐: The traditional finance ideal investor — bounded by three traits: **rationality, self-control, self-interest**. ⭐
- **Efficient Markets** (concept): The idea that asset prices reflect fundamental value and all available information.

### Key Concepts

#### Why traditional theory broke down
Traditional finance could not explain:
- Market volatility and unpredictability
- Market crashes
- Irrational investor behaviour
- Stock market bubbles
- "Why does an investor trade, how does an investor trade, what factors decide a portfolio?"

#### Two branches of behavioural finance
| Branch | Focus | Studies |
|--------|-------|---------|
| **Microbehavioural Finance** ⭐ | Individual investor | Cognitive, psychological, sociological influences; behavioural biases distinguishing them from rational economic beings; heuristics as rules of thumb leading to suboptimal decisions |
| **Macrobehavioural Finance** ⭐ | Whole market | Market anomalies that make markets inefficient; reasons behind stock bubbles, market volatility, limited arbitrage |

#### Kahneman's Nobel Prize (2002)
Behavioural finance became popular after **2002**, when **Daniel Kahneman** was awarded the Nobel Prize for psychological research into economic science — especially human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: "Cognitive bias is one of the three traits of homo economicus" → ✅ Correct: The three are **rationality, self-control, self-interest** (Check Your Progress 1 Q3 — answer is c, cognitive bias is NOT a trait).
- ❌ Mistake: "Microbehavioural finance studies bubbles and limited arbitrage" → ✅ Correct: That's **macrobehavioural** finance. **Microbehavioural** = irrational investor behaviour (Check Your Progress 1 Q4 — answer is a).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Behavioural finance = cognitive + sociological + psychological influences ⭐
> - Three traits of homo economicus: **rationality, self-control, self-interest** ⭐
> - **Micro**behavioural = individual investor; **Macro**behavioural = whole market
> - Kahneman Nobel 2002 → behavioural finance went mainstream

## Section: Difference Between Traditional and Behavioural Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
Traditional finance is built on **investor and market rationality** — investors process complete information unbiased by emotion. Behavioural finance is built on **bounded rationality** — investors are influenced by emotions, biases, and heuristics. The two are not opposites but **alternative perspectives** providing a fuller picture of markets.

### Five Key Contrasts

| Dimension | Traditional Finance | Behavioural Finance |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Investor decision basis** | Rational, unbiased information processing | Influenced by overconfidence, familiarity bias, herding, etc.; emotions and psychology may make investors irrational |
| **Information assumption** | Complete information available to all; investors examine it rationally; investors always make right decisions | **Bounded rationality** — investors don't process all info; they **satisfice instead of maximise**; even with accurate info, they err in judgment |
| **Market efficiency** | EMH — markets are efficient; price reflects fundamental value | EMH disputed; anomalies cause volatility unexplainable by EMH |
| **Self-control** | Investors have **perfect self-control**; past/personality don't influence | Self-control problems exist and affect both investor and market |
| **Arbitrage** | Any "noise" balanced out by rational traders | **Limit to arbitrage**; prices not always right; non-equilibrium can persist |

### Definitions
- **Bounded Rationality** ⭐: Investors don't process all available information; they **satisfice** (settle for "good enough") rather than maximise. ⭐
- **Limit to Arbitrage** ⭐: Behavioural finance proposition that arbitrage cannot fully correct mispricing — prices may stay in non-equilibrium for periods. ⭐
- **Satisficing**: Choosing an acceptable rather than optimal outcome.

### Examples
- **Herding example**: When buying stocks, investors follow others "because they think everyone else knows more" → herding mentality → sub-optimal decisions

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Traditional: rational + complete info + EMH + self-control + arbitrage corrects all
> - Behavioural: bounded rationality + biases + EMH-failures + self-control problems + limit to arbitrage
> - Investors **satisfice** rather than maximise
> - "Rational, irrational" is the right pair (CYP2 Q3 → answer b)
> - "Complete information available to everyone" is **NOT** behavioural — it's traditional (CYP2 Q4 → answer a)

## Section: Growth and Origin of Behavioural Finance 🟡

### Core Idea
Behavioural finance has roots in 1960s–70s cognitive psychology but really emerged in the **1980s** to explain market anomalies — driven by Kahneman and Tversky's work. It evolved in **two phases**: (i) documenting examples of anomalies, then (ii) explaining them. The late 1990s/early 2000s saw it absorbed into mainstream finance, cemented by two consecutive Nobel Prizes (2001, 2002).

### Two-Phase Evolution

| Phase | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Phase 1** | Documenting **examples of market anomalies** and deviations from standard finance assumptions |
| **Phase 2** | **Explaining** these anomalies |

### Timeline of Key Contributions

| Year | Contribution | Author(s) |
|------|--------------|-----------|
| 1981 | Market anomalies — rational factors alone can't explain stock market volatility | **Robert James Shiller** |
| 1985 | Stock market overreaction → value anomaly | **De Bondt and Richard Thaler** |
| 1980s | Concepts: **disposition effect**, **equity premium puzzle** | Various economists |
| Late 1990s/early 2000s | Behavioural finance becomes mainstream; concepts: **limit to arbitrage**, **myopic loss aversion**, **over-reactions and under-reactions** |  |
| 2000 | Book *Irrational Exuberance* — predicted dot-com crash | **Robert Shiller** |
| Around 2000 | Noise traders → limited arbitrage → market anomalies | **Andrei Shleifer** |
| **2001** | Nobel Prize "for analyses of markets with **asymmetric information**" | **George Akerlof, Michael Spence, Joseph Stiglitz** |
| **2002** | Nobel Prize "for integrating insights from **psychological research** into economic science, especially concerning human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty" | **Daniel Kahneman** |

### Critique
Behavioural finance is criticised for being "**Micro-efficient but not Macro-inefficient**" — i.e., it explains individual investor behaviour effectively but is not very useful in **predicting the overall market**. It explains market anomalies in the **long run**, but cannot reliably predict **short-run** stock-market variations.

### Definitions
- **Disposition Effect** (originated 1980s): Tendency to prematurely sell winning assets while holding losing ones (detailed in Chunk 010).
- **Equity Premium Puzzle** (originated 1980s): Empirical observation that stocks have outperformed bonds yet investors still hold bonds — contradicts pure risk-aversion models.
- **Myopic Loss Aversion**: Frequent portfolio evaluation amplifies loss aversion (detailed in Chunk 010).
- **Irrational Exuberance** (Shiller 2000): Investor enthusiasm pushing stock prices above fundamentals.
- **Noise Traders** (Shleifer): Traders whose decisions are not based on fundamentals; they cause limited arbitrage.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Behavioural finance evolved in **1980s**
> - Two phases: (1) document anomalies (2) explain them
> - **Kahneman & Tversky** are the foundational figures
> - **Shiller 1981** (volatility anomalies) and **De Bondt-Thaler 1985** (overreaction)
> - **2001 Nobel** — Akerlof/Spence/Stiglitz (asymmetric info); **2002 Nobel** — Kahneman
> - Critique: "**Micro-efficient, not Macro-inefficient**" — long-run yes, short-run no

## Section: Efficient Market Hypothesis and Its Critique 🔴

### Core Idea
The **Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)** says market prices fully reflect all available information; new information is rapidly incorporated, so current prices are the best approximation of intrinsic value. EMH is associated with the "**random walk**" — past prices cannot predict future movements, and no one can outperform the market without taking extra risk. Behavioural finance counters with **momentum** in short-run prices: investors follow trends ("bandwagon effect"), under-react to new information, and exhibit biases that produce **predictable** rather than random price movements.

### Definitions
- **Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)** ⭐: Market prices fully reflect all available information; current prices are the best approximation of a company's intrinsic value. Developed independently by **Paul A. Samuelson** and **Eugene F. Fama** in the **1960s**. ⭐
- **Random Walk Theory** ⭐: Past stock-price movements/trends cannot predict future movement; impossible to outperform market without additional risk. Logic: news is unpredictable, so today's situation cannot predict future values. ⭐
- **Bandwagon Effect / Momentum**: Investors follow market trends — rising price → more investment → further price rise.
- **Irrational Exuberance**: Investor enthusiasm pushing stock price above fundamental value.
- **Under-reaction**: Investors take time to update beliefs about a stock; full impact realised over a period → positive correlation in prices (not random).

### Key Concepts

#### EMH "currency-note-on-the-road" intuition
"If a currency note is lying on the road, it will not be there for long as it will be surely picked up by someone." Similarly, all profitable information is picked up and incorporated by market agents → no persistent free lunches.

#### Behavioural critique of randomness
1. **Bandwagon effect** — herding into rising stocks → momentum
2. **Under-reaction** — investors take time to update beliefs → news is gradually priced in → **positive serial correlation** (changes in prices are NOT random)
3. **Behavioural biases** — sociological, psychological, cognitive influences cause behavioural anomalies

### Three Core Puzzles Driven by Behavioural Biases

| Puzzle | What it says |
|--------|--------------|
| **Equity Premium Puzzle** | Stocks have earned disproportionately higher returns than bonds, yet investors are relatively unwilling to hold them |
| **Volatility Puzzle** | Stock returns are highly variable with **large dispersions** |
| **Predictability Puzzle** | Stock returns are predictable — persistent profits NOT eroded by arbitrage |

### Common Behavioural Biases (introduced)
- **Herding**, **overconfidence**, **overreaction**, **loss aversion**, **mental accounting** — these dominate investor thinking and produce the three puzzles above.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **EMH** = Samuelson & Fama, 1960s ⭐
> - EMH ↔ **Random Walk**: past prices ≠ predictor of future ⭐
> - Behavioural counter: momentum + under-reaction → positive serial correlation
> - 3 puzzles: **equity premium, volatility, predictability**
> - 5 dominant biases: herding, overconfidence, overreaction, loss aversion, mental accounting

## Section: Market Anomalies — Three Types 🔴

### Core Idea
Tversky & Kahneman (1986) defined a market anomaly as: "*a deviation from the presently accepted paradigms that are too widespread to be ignored, too systematic to be dismissed as random error and too fundamental to be accommodated by relaxing the normative system*." Anomalies fall into **three types**: **Fundamental**, **Calendar/Seasonal**, and **Technical**.

### Definitions
- **Market Anomaly** ⭐: A deviation from the predictions of EMH that is too widespread, systematic, and fundamental to be dismissed as random error. (Tversky & Kahneman 1986). ⭐

### Three Types of Anomalies

#### (i) Fundamental Anomalies
| Anomaly | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Value Anomaly** | Over-estimation of future earnings and returns of growth companies |
| **Low Price-to-Book** | Stocks with high book-to-market generate **less return** than stocks with low price-to-book |
| **High Dividend Yield** | Positive correlation between yield and stock return; high-dividend-yield stocks **outperform** the market |
| **Neglected Stocks** | Prior neglected stocks generate **more return** subsequently; prior best performers underperform the index |
| **Low Price-to-Earnings (P/E)** | Low P/E stocks tend to **outperform**; high P/E stocks tend to **underperform** |

#### (ii) Calendar/Seasonal Anomalies
Irregular patterns of stock returns across periods — day-to-day, month-to-month, year-to-year.

| Anomaly | Pattern |
|---------|---------|
| **Weekend Effect** | Monday closing < previous Friday closing |
| **Turn-of-the-Month Effect** | Stock prices rise in **last trading day** of month + **first three days** of next month |
| **Turn-of-the-Year Effect** | Prices and trading volume rise in **last week of December** + **first half of January** |
| **January Effect** | Small-company stocks generate more return than other assets in **first 2–3 weeks of January** |

#### (iii) Technical Anomalies
Techniques to forecast prices from past prices — contradicting weak-form EMH which says past prices are already incorporated.

| Anomaly | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Moving Averages** | Short-period MA crossing **above** long-period MA = **buy** signal; crossing **below** = **sell** signal |
| **Trading Range Break** | Identify support/resistance + price trends → entry/exit points; "breakout" = potential trading opportunity |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **3 anomaly types**: Fundamental, Calendar/Seasonal, Technical ⭐ (CYP3 Q1)
> - 5 fundamental: Value, Low P/B, High Div Yield, Neglected, Low P/E
> - 4 calendar: Weekend, Turn-of-Month, Turn-of-Year, January
> - 2 technical: Moving Averages, Trading Range Break
> - Tversky & Kahneman 1986 definition of "anomaly"

## Section: Market Puzzles and Behavioural Explanations 🔴
<!-- Continues in chunk 010 — Predictability puzzle (disposition effect, momentum effect) -->

### Core Idea
Behavioural finance explains three market puzzles that EMH cannot: **Equity Premium Puzzle**, **Volatility Puzzle**, and **Predictability Puzzle**. The first two are fully covered in this chunk; the predictability puzzle's behavioural explanation continues into Chunk 010.

### Puzzle (i): Equity Premium Puzzle

**The puzzle (Mehra and Prescott)**: Stocks have **greatly outperformed bonds** over the last century — earned disproportionately higher returns — yet **people still buy bonds**. The level of risk aversion required to justify this is implausibly high. Pure risk-aversion models cannot explain it.

**Behavioural explanations** (Prospect Theory):
1. **Loss aversion** — investors are more sensitive to losses than to gains
2. **Myopic loss aversion** — investors evaluate their portfolio frequently (even when saving for retirement); frequent evaluation amplifies the felt impact of short-term losses
3. **Ambiguity aversion** — preference for **known** risks over **unknown** risks; equity-return distribution is unknown, so people overweigh probability of returns from bonds

### Puzzle (ii): Volatility Puzzle

**The puzzle**: High variability of stock prices with large dispersions. Changes in stock prices are **disproportionately higher than changes in fundamentals** — contradicts EMH (which says prices represent fundamentals).

**Behavioural explanations** (cognitive, social, emotional biases):
- Investors **overweigh recent information** and **consensus beliefs**
- Investors seek **confirming evidence** → under-react or overreact in the market
- **Focal points** — investors focus more on similar pieces of information
- **Loss aversion** — overweighing of negative information leads to **steep falls** (rather than gradual upward correction)

### Puzzle (iii): Predictability Puzzle (introduced — continues in chunk 010)

**The puzzle**: Stock returns are **predictable from price-dividend ratios** — contradicts random-walk hypothesis.

**Behavioural explanations** (begun, continues into next chunk):
- **Disposition effect** — tendency to prematurely sell assets that have made financial gains while holding on to losing assets

### Definitions
- **Equity Premium Puzzle** ⭐: Stocks outperform bonds yet investors still hold bonds — unexplainable by pure risk aversion (Mehra & Prescott). ⭐
- **Loss Aversion** ⭐: Investors are more sensitive to losses than to gains. ⭐
- **Myopic Loss Aversion** ⭐: Frequent portfolio evaluation amplifies short-run loss aversion. ⭐
- **Ambiguity Aversion** ⭐: Preference for known risks over unknown risks. ⭐
- **Volatility Puzzle** ⭐: Stock-price changes far exceed fundamentals changes — high variability with large dispersions. ⭐
- **Predictability Puzzle** ⭐: Stock returns predictable from price-dividend ratios; persistent profits not eroded by arbitrage. ⭐
- **Prospect Theory**: Kahneman framework explaining loss aversion and risk attitudes around gains/losses (used to derive equity premium puzzle explanation).
