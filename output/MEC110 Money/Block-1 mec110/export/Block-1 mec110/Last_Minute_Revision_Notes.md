### The Nature of the Financial System 🔴

**Four constituents of a financial system**
1. **Financial institutions** — business organisations that mobilise and deposit savings and act as purveyors of credit/finance; also provide financial services.
2. **Financial markets** — mechanisms enabling participants to deal with financial claims and to set prices for those claims.
3. **Financial instruments** — claims against a person or institution for the payment, at a future date, of money and/or a periodic payment in the form of interest or dividend.
4. **Financial services** — services rendered by intermediaries (merchant banking, leasing, hire purchase, credit rating, etc.) that bridge the information gap between investors and increasingly sophisticated instruments.

**Two classifications of financial institutions**
| Classification | Type A | Type B |
|----------------|--------|--------|
| **By money-creation power** | Banking institutions — *creators* of credit (Sayers) | Non-banking institutions — *purveyors* of credit |
| **By source of funds** | Intermediaries — borrow from ultimate savers, lend to ultimate borrowers | Non-intermediaries — lend, but don't take funds directly from savers |
1. They participate in the economy's payments mechanism (provide transactions services).
2. Their **deposit liabilities form a major part of the national money supply**.
3. As a whole, they **can create deposits or credit** which is money.

**Sayers' distinction**
- The contrast between banking and non-banking institutions is captured by Sayers, who calls the former "**creators**" of credit and the latter "**purveyors**" of credit. ⭐
- **Financial System**: a complex, well-integrated set of sub-systems of financial institutions, markets, instruments, and services which facilitate the transfer and allocation of funds, efficiently and effectively. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial institutions**: business organisations that act as mobilisers and depositors of savings and as purveyors of credit or finance. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial markets**: a mechanism enabling participants to deal with financial claims; participants on the demand and supply sides include FIs, agents, brokers, dealers, borrowers, lenders, savers etc. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial instrument**: a claim against a person or an institution for the payment at a future date of a sum of money and/or a periodic payment in the form of interest or dividend. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial services**: services like merchant banking, leasing, hire purchase, credit rating provided by intermediaries to bridge the gap between lay investors' lack of knowledge and sophisticated instruments.
1. **Facilitating Transactions** — enabling exchange of goods and services.
2. **Mobilizing Savings** — gathering savings that would otherwise have limited investment use.
3. **Allocating Capital** — directing funds to finance productive investments.
4. **Monitoring Management** — ensuring allocated funds are used as intended.
1. *Provision of funds* — venture capital, banking services, asset financing, trade financing, credit cards.
2. *Managing investible funds* — portfolio management, merchant banking, mutual & pension funds.
3. *Risk financing* — project preparatory services, insurance, export credit guarantee.
4. *Consultancy services* — project preparation, project appraisal, business advisory, valuation, credit rating, M&A.

### Importance and Role of Financial Institutions in Economic Development 🔴
1. **Form financial resources** by mobilising savings of households, corporates, and the public sector.
2. **Channelise resources/investments** into agriculture, manufacturing, services.
3. **Meet short- and long-term financial needs.**
4. **Multiply monetary resources** through credit creation (banks specifically).
5. **Transfer surplus funds** from one part of the economy to another based on national priorities and market demand.
6. **Encourage conversion of physical assets into financial assets** to make capital available for production, trade, commerce.
7. **Provide variety of financial assets** to suit different investor needs.
8. **Risk control & uncertainty management** for stability of the financial system; reduce capital-loss risk via diversification.
9. **Encourage entrepreneurial skills** and assist new projects.

**Equilibrium condition**

**Why intermediaries are essential**
| Market | What is traded | Key feature |
|--------|----------------|-------------|
| **OTC (Over-the-Counter)** | Stocks of usually small companies | Decentralised, less regulation, customised procedures, traded cheaply. |
| **Bond market** | Bonds (govt, corporate, state, municipal) | Loan with set time and predefined interest rate. |

| **Options markets** | Options contracts | Listed and regulated; on equities, fixed-income, commodities, etc. |
| **Commodities markets** | Agri products, energy (oil/gas/carbon credits), precious metals, soft commodities | Bulk in **spot markets** (physical for money); forwards/futures/options trade OTC + listed. |
| **Forex market** | Currency pairs (buy/sell/hedge/speculate) | **Most liquid market in the world**; decentralised; banks, central banks, hedge funds, retail. |
| **Cryptocurrency markets** | Digital assets (Bitcoin, Ethereum, etc.) | Since 2009; blockchain-based; mostly centralised exchanges (hack/fraud risk); decentralised P2P exchanges also exist; futures/options on majors. |
1. **Nature of Claim** → (a) **Debt market** (bonds, debentures); (b) **Equity market** (shares).
2. **Maturity of Claim** → (a) **Money market** (short-term: CDs, T-bills, commercial paper, ≤12 months); (b) **Capital market** (medium/long term: ≥1 year).
3. **Timing of Delivery** → (a) **Cash (spot) market** (real-time settlement); (b) **Futures market** (delivery on a specified future date).
4. **Organisational Structure** → (a) **Exchange-traded** (centralised, patterned procedure); (b) **OTC** (decentralised, customised).
5. **Nature of Transaction** → (a) **Primary market** ("new issue market" — fresh capital to firms; mobilises savings); (b) **Secondary market** (already-issued securities; provides liquidity, no fresh capital directly).
6. **Regulation Status** → (a) **Unorganised** (indigenous bankers, money lenders); (b) **Organised** (under RBI / SEBI regulation).
- **Derivative**: a contract between two or more parties whose value is based on an agreed-upon underlying financial asset (or set of assets like an index). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Money market**: market trading highly liquid, short-term (<1 year) instruments with high safety and low return.
- **Forex market**: market in which participants buy, sell, hedge, or speculate on exchange rates between currency pairs; **most liquid market in the world**.
- **Cryptocurrency**: decentralized digital assets based on blockchain technology. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Primary market**: deals in new financial claims / new securities — also called "new issue market"; supplies fresh capital to business units. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Secondary market**: deals in already-issued / outstanding securities; provides liquidity but does not directly add new capital.

### Importance and Functions of Financial Markets 🔴

**Five core functions of financial markets ⭐**
1. **Create securities products** — return for surplus units (investors/lenders); access to funds for deficit units (borrowers).
2. **Mobilize savings** and trade them in the most productive uses.
3. **Assist in price discovery** of securities through investor interaction with demand/supply.
4. **Provide liquidity** to bartered/traded assets.
5. **Reduce search and information cost** — parties don't have to spend time and money finding each other; markets supply valuable information about traded securities.

**Wider economic effects**

**Quick Recall:**
- 5 functions: **create securities · mobilise savings · price discovery · liquidity · lower cost & info asymmetry**.
- Markets allocate scarce capital — without them, output and trade shrink.

### Financial Instruments — Types and Classifications 🔴

**Three primary types**
1. **Cash instruments** — value determined directly by the markets; includes (a) **securities** (readily transferable) and (b) **loans and deposits** (require borrower-lender agreement to transfer).
2. **Derivative instruments** — value derived from underlying assets (e.g., options, forward exchange contracts).
3. **Foreign exchange instruments** — neither debt nor equity; in a category of their own.

**Classification by asset class**
- **Equity-based** — claim to ownership.
- **Debt-based** — claim to repayment + interest. Sub-divided into:
  - *Short-term debt*
  - *Long-term debt*
- **Foreign exchange** — separate from debt/equity.

**Complex vs Non-Complex**
| | Complex | Non-Complex |
|---|---------|-------------|
| Examples | Derivatives — CFDs, futures, options | Shares/equity, debt securities, certain investment funds |
| Knowledge required | Specialist in-depth knowledge | Tradable without vast specialist knowledge |

**Primary vs Secondary securities**
- **Primary (direct) securities** — issued directly by the ultimate borrowers of funds to the ultimate users.
- **Secondary securities** — issued by financial intermediaries; the term is also used for securities purchased from other investors in the market.
- **Financial instrument**: an intangible asset that is a claim against a person or institution for future payment of a sum and/or a periodic payment in the form of interest or dividend; a tradable asset representing a legal agreement or contractual right. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cash instruments**: instruments whose value is determined directly by the markets (securities, loans, deposits).
- **Derivative**: a financial instrument such as options or forward exchange contracts whose value is derived from one or more underlying instruments.
- **Primary security (direct security)**: directly issued by the ultimate borrower to the ultimate user. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Secondary security**: a term used for the financial market where securities are purchased from other investors. ⭐ (exam-important)

- ❌ Confusing "primary security" with "primary market" → ✅ Primary security = directly issued by the ultimate borrower; primary market = the venue where new issues happen. They overlap but aren't synonyms.
- ❌ Treating forex instruments as a sub-type of debt or equity → ✅ Forex sits in a **separate** category, neither debt-based nor equity-based.

### Unit 2 — Financial Intermediation (Objectives, Direct vs Indirect Finance) 🔴

**Direct vs Indirect Finance — comparison**
| Feature | Direct Finance | Indirect Finance |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| Path of funds | Saver → financial markets → Borrower | Saver → Intermediary → Borrower |
| Middleman | None | Yes — a "financial intermediary" |
| Example | Buying corporate bond directly | Bank deposits → bank loans |
| What flows back | Securities issued by borrower | Intermediary's own claim (e.g., deposit account) |

**Bank as a textbook intermediary**
- Accepts **deposits** from savers.
- Provides **loans** to borrowers.
- Pays depositors interest; receives interest from borrowers.
- Earns profit on the **spread** between lending rate and deposit rate.

**Broker vs Financial Intermediary — *don't confuse these***
| | Broker | Financial Intermediary |
|---|--------|------------------------|
| What they do | Bring borrowers and lenders together | Lend to borrowers themselves |
| Issue securities? | No — just facilitate direct-finance transactions | Yes — they create and issue their own securities (e.g., bonds, deposits) |
| Compensation | Commission for matching parties | Profit on the spread between cost of funds and lending rate |
| Risk | None on their balance sheet | Holds the loans/assets on their balance sheet |

**Intermediaries vs Non-intermediaries (recap from Unit 1)**
- **Intermediaries** intermediate between savers and investors — lend money + mobilize savings; their **liabilities are towards ultimate savers**, their **assets are claims on borrowers**.
- **Non-intermediary institutions** are in the lending business but their resources are not directly obtained from savers.
- All banking institutions are intermediaries; many non-banking institutions also act as intermediaries (NBFIs).

**Generic functions of financial intermediaries (banking + non-banking)**
1. Bridge between savers and investors.
2. Reduce cost of financial services.
3. Drive development of financial services in the country.
4. Provide consultancy on risky investments — improve decision-making.
5. Profit by earning higher returns on investments than they pay for funds.
6. Asset side = loans, stocks, bonds, real estate; Liability side = deposits, insurance policies, pension payouts.
- **Direct finance**: funds are directly provided by savers to borrowers in financial markets without involvement of any intermediary. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Indirect finance**: funds are provided through a "middleman" — a third party (financial intermediary) that stands between borrowers and lenders. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial intermediary**: an entity that accumulates funds from surplus parties (savers) and makes those funds available to deficit parties (borrowers); creates and issues its own securities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Spread (banking)**: the difference between the rate of interest charged to borrowers and the rate paid to depositors — the bank's source of profit.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Treating brokers as intermediaries → ✅ Brokers don't lend or issue securities; they only facilitate transactions and earn commissions. Real intermediaries take the loan onto their own balance sheet.
- ❌ Forgetting that NBFIs can also be intermediaries → ✅ Mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds intermediate too — they are non-banking *financial intermediaries* (NBFIs).

**Quick Recall:**
- **Direct = no middleman; Indirect = middleman (FI)**.
- Banks earn the **spread** (loan rate − deposit rate).
- Broker ≠ Intermediary: broker just matches; intermediary actually lends and issues its own securities.
- All banks are intermediaries; not all non-banks are.

### An Overview of Indian Financial System 🔴

**Two segments of the Indian financial system**
| Sector | Components | Interest rates / credit conditions |
|--------|------------|----------------------------------|
| **Organised** | Commercial banks, development banks, cooperative banks, stock market, insurance, mutual funds, NBFCs | Regulated, broadly transparent |
| **Unorganised** | Informal credit market — money lenders, indigenous bankers; serves farmers, small firms, individuals | Higher / opaque rates and terms |

**A) Nationalisation of financial institutions ⭐ (memorise dates)**
| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| **1948** | RBI (founded 1935 as private central bank) nationalised. |
| **1955** | Imperial Bank of India nationalised → renamed **State Bank of India**. |
| **1956** | 245 life insurance entities (154 life insurance companies + 16 foreign + 75 provident) nationalised and merged into **LIC (Life Insurance Corporation of India)**. |
| **1969** | **14 commercial banks** nationalised. |
| **1972** | 107 general insurance entities (55 Indian + 52 other operations) nationalised and merged into **GIC (General Insurance Corporation of India)**. |
| **1980** | Another **6 commercial banks** nationalised. |

**B) Establishment of new institutions**
- **UTI (Unit Trust of India), 1964** — to strengthen Indian financial system and supply institutional credit to industries; mobilise public savings, provide credit to institutions for productive purposes. Subsidiaries: UTI Bank Ltd., UTI Investor Service Ltd., UTI Security Exchange Ltd.
| Institution | Year | Purpose |
|-------------|------|---------|
| **IFCI** (Industrial Finance Corporation of India) | 1948 | Medium/long-term finance to industrial concerns at central level. |
| **SFCs** (State Financial Corporations) under SFC Act | 1951 | Medium/long-term finance to medium and small industries at state level. SIIC also set up. |
| **ICICI** (Industrial Credit and Investment Corporation of India) | 1955 | Develop large/medium industries in private sector. *(Now merged with ICICI Bank, a private commercial bank.)* |

| **IDBI** (Industrial Development Bank of India) | 1964 | Initially wholly owned subsidiary of RBI; apex institution in development banking, coordinates other FIs. *(Ownership now with central government.)* |
| **SIDBI** (Small Industries Development Bank of India) | 1990 | Principal FI for promotion, financing, development of MSMEs. |
| **ARDC** (Agricultural Refinance and Development Corporation) | 1963 | Finance major rural development projects (minor irrigation, farm mechanisation, land development, horticulture, dairy). |
| **NABARD** (National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development) | 1982 | ARDC merged into it; whole sphere of agricultural finance entrusted to NABARD. |
| **EXIM Bank** (Export and Import Bank of India) | 1982 | Took over IDBI's International Finance Wing operations; finance to exporters/importers; principal FI for foreign-trade financing coordination. |
| **NHB** (National Housing Bank) | 1988 | Apex institution to mobilise resources for housing; refinancing, underwriting, guaranteeing facilities to housing finance institutions. |
| **SHCIL** (Stock Holding Corporation of India Ltd.) | 1987 | Tone up stock and capital markets; share-transfer, clearing, depository, MIS, development services. |

**C) Encouragement of new financial services and instruments**
- **Mutual Funds Industry** — both private and public sector encouraged to float mutual funds.
- **Venture Capital Industry** — both sectors encouraged to finance via venture capital.
- **Credit Rating Agencies** — **CRISIL** (Credit Rating and Information Services of India Ltd.), **ICRA** (Investment Information and Credit Rating Agency of India Ltd.), **CARE** (Credit Analysis and Research Ltd.) — to help investors decide and protect them from risky ventures.
- **New financial instruments** — public-sector bonds, national savings certificates, post-office savings schemes, varieties of shares/debentures, insurance schemes, varieties of bank deposits.

**Evolution and post-1991 shift**
- The structure in the late 1960s reflected growing public ownership/control aimed at directing finance to priority development areas.
- **1991 economic reforms** — system underwent a structural change with substantial role for the **private sector**.
- **Major weakness**: high overdues / **non-performing assets (NPAs)** in lenders, affecting both industrial and agricultural finance (improving in recent years).
- **Gross saving rate**: ~8% at independence → peak ~35% → ~30% in recent years.
- **Composition of saving**: physical savings were >90% of gross savings at independence → ~50% in recent years (i.e., financial assets now far more important).
- **14 + 6 = 20** commercial banks nationalised (1969 + 1980).
- **245** life insurance entities nationalised (1956).
- **Organised financial sector**: under regulatory purview of RBI, SEBI etc.; includes commercial/development/cooperative banks, stock market, NBFIs (insurance corps, mutual funds).
- **Unorganised financial sector**: informal credit market — money lenders, indigenous bankers; serves farmers, small firms, individuals.
- **Financialization (of the Indian economy)**: the increase, since 1950, in financial assets relative to physical assets in gross savings, indicating an active financial system in transferring funds from surplus to deficit sectors.
- **Non-Performing Assets (NPAs)**: high overdues that affect lenders — major weakness of the Indian financial system post-1991.

### The National Accounts 🔴

**Two flows in macro**

|---------|------------|--------------|
| **National Income** | Total income earned by a country's residents | Sum wages + salaries + profits + rental income, over a period (usually a year). |
| **National Product** | Total value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in a period | Sum value of all final goods and services, regardless of who produced them. Includes both market-traded and personal-use goods (e.g., homemade vegetables). |

| Approach | Components |
|----------|-----------|

**From GDP to NNP**
| Step | Formula | What it adds/subtracts |
|------|---------|------------------------|
| GDP at factor cost (production side) | Sum of value-added | — |

### Flow of Funds Accounts (FFA) and Goldsmith's Financial-Development Ratios 🔴

**Structure of the FFA**
- Maintained by the **Federal Reserve System of the United States** (the original source).
- Covers sectors: **households, businesses, government, banking and financial institutions, rest of the world**.
- Tracks the movement of funds between sectors via instruments — stocks, bonds, loans.
- Two sections:
  - **Asset and Liability section** — financial assets and liabilities of each sector.
  - **Transactions section** — financial transactions between the sectors.

**Information offered by FFA**
1. Variation in **net worth** of each sector.
2. **Credit outstanding** in each sector.
3. **Equity outstanding** in each sector.
4. **Flow of funds** between sectors (e.g., household savings → business / govt securities).
5. **Composition of financial assets and liabilities** of each sector — which instruments are held.

**Instrument-wise classification of financial claims**
- **Primary issues / Direct securities** — all kinds of debt issued by ultimate borrowers (marketable or otherwise).
- **Secondary issues / Indirect securities** — debt and claims issued by **financial intermediaries** to acquire primary securities.

**Goldsmith's financial-deepening ratios ⭐⭐ (named theory — high-yield)**
| Ratio | Formula | Indicates |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **Finance Ratio (FR)** | $$FR = \frac{\text{Total Financial Claims (Primary + Secondary issues)}}{\text{National Income}}$$ | Financial structure of economic growth — how large the financial sector is relative to income. |
| **Financial Inter-Relation Ratio (FIR)** | $$FIR = \frac{\text{Financial Assets (Total Issues)}}{\text{Physical Assets (Net Domestic Capital Formation)}}$$ | Relationship between financial structure and the real-asset structure of the economy; indicator of financial issues vs capital formation. |
| **New Issue Ratio (NIR)** | $$NIR = \frac{\text{Primary Issues}}{\text{Net Physical Investments (Net Domestic Capital Formation)}}$$ | How primary/direct issues to savers have financed the investment by investing sectors. |
| **Intermediation Ratio (IR)** | $$IR = \frac{\text{Total Secondary Issues}}{\text{Total Primary Issues}}$$ | Significance of financial intermediaries in channelling resources; ratio of FI-issued claims to non-FI-issued claims. |
- **Flow of Funds Accounts (FFA)**: macroeconomic tool tracking the flow of funds within an economy across sectors and instruments; provides information on generation, distribution, utilisation of funds. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Primary issues / Direct securities** (in FFA context): debt issued by ultimate borrowers.
- **Secondary issues / Indirect securities** (in FFA context): debt and claims issued by financial intermediaries to acquire primary securities.
- **Goldsmith's Financial Deepening Ratios** (1969): a set of ratios — FR, FIR, NIR, IR — that measure financial development. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Finance Ratio (FR)**: Total Financial Claims / National Income.
- **Financial Inter-Relation Ratio (FIR)**: Total Financial Issues / Net Domestic Capital Formation.
- **New Issue Ratio (NIR)**: Primary Issues / Net Domestic Capital Formation.
- **Intermediation Ratio (IR)**: Total Secondary Issues / Total Primary Issues.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes

### Exchange Rates in International Financial Markets 🔴

**Brief history of exchange-rate regimes**
1. **Pre-19th century** — barter and direct exchange of goods/services.
2. **1875**: **Gold Standard** introduced — currencies' values fixed in gold. Lasted until WWI when countries abandoned it because of financial constraints.
3. **Post-WWII**: **Bretton Woods system** — fixed values of currencies to the **US dollar**, dollar fixed to gold. **Collapsed in early 1970s** because of economic crisis and US's failure to maintain the gold standard.
4. **Today**: **Floating exchange rates** dominate — more flexible, less prone to financial losses.

**Effects of exchange-rate movements**
| Channel | When currency strengthens | When currency weakens |
|---------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| **GDP** | Exports more expensive → fewer exports → lower GDP | Exports cheaper → more exports → higher GDP |
| **Government borrowing from foreign investors** | Cheaper to borrow → lower borrowing costs → more govt spending | More expensive to borrow → higher costs → lower spending |
| **Imports/Exports** | Imports cheaper, exports dearer | Exports cheaper, imports dearer |
- Initial rate: 1 USD = 0.85 EUR.
- USD strengthens vs EUR: rate becomes $1 = €0.80 (each $1 buys fewer €).
- Effect: US exports become more expensive for European buyers → US exports fall → US GDP falls.
- Initial rate: 110 JPY = $1.
- Yen weakens against dollar: rate becomes 120 JPY = $1.
- Effect: It now costs more yen to buy each dollar → expensive for Japanese government to borrow in dollars.
- **Exchange rate**: the value of one currency expressed in terms of another. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Gold Standard** (1875–WWI): system in which currency values were fixed in gold.
- **Bretton Woods system** (post-WWII to early 1970s): system in which the US dollar was fixed to gold and other currencies were fixed to the US dollar; collapsed due to economic crisis and the US's failure to maintain the gold standard.
- **Floating exchange rate**: exchange rate determined by market forces of supply and demand.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Saying the gold standard ended in 1971 → ✅ The **gold standard** ended around **WWI** (countries abandoned it then). The **Bretton Woods system** (a *dollar*-gold system) collapsed in the early 1970s.
- ❌ Thinking a strong currency is always good for GDP → ✅ A strong currency makes **exports more expensive** and can **lower GDP** via reduced export demand.

**Quick Recall:**
- **Gold Standard 1875 → WWI**.
- **Bretton Woods → collapsed early 1970s**.
- Today = **floating exchange rates**.
- Strong currency ⇒ exports dear ⇒ GDP ↓; weak currency ⇒ exports cheap ⇒ GDP ↑.

### Rate of Interest 🔴

**Pre-money: the barter system and its limits**
- **Barter system** = exchange of goods/services for other goods/services without money.
- **Two limitations** of barter:
  1. Difficult to assess the value of goods and services.
  2. Hard to find a counterpart who wants what you have *and* has what you want (the "double coincidence of wants" problem).

**How interest works in real estate**

**Interest rates on government bonds**
- Governments borrow by issuing **promissory notes** that promise to repay principal + interest at a defined rate.
- Rates on government bonds reflect the **government's borrowing risk** as perceived by economic forces — higher perceived risk ⇒ higher interest demanded.

**Macroeconomic effect of interest rates**
| Interest rate level | Effect on borrowing & investment | Effect on economy |
|---------------------|------------------------------------|--------------------|
| High | Discouraged | Slows |
| Low | Encouraged | Stimulated |
- **Interest rate**: the cost of borrowing, used by individuals, businesses, and governments to finance investment and operations; usually expressed as a percentage of income/principal. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Barter system**: any exchange of goods and services for other goods and services with no exchange of money. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mortgage**: a cost-of-purchase loan, usually repaid with interest over 15 to 30 years.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Saying low interest rates always boost the economy → ✅ Low rates *typically* stimulate borrowing/investment, but if confidence is broken (as in deep recessions), the textbook channel weakens.
- ❌ Confusing the cost of borrowing with the cost of money → ✅ Interest is the cost of *credit* over time; not the cost of holding money.

**Quick Recall:**
- **Barter limits: (i) value assessment, (ii) finding a counterparty.**
- Mortgages run **15–30 years**.
- Post-2008 Fed cut **federal funds rate to zero**.
- **High rates → slow economy; Low rates → stimulate**.

### Government Borrowings 🔴

**Mechanics**
- **Investors** (banks, pension funds, individual investors) **buy government securities** and lend money to the government.
- **Government uses proceeds** to fund expenditure.
- **Budget deficit** = government expenditure − revenue in a year. A deficit must be financed by borrowing.

**Two macro effects of government borrowing ⭐**
| Effect | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| **Crowding out** | When govt borrows heavily, it competes with private lenders for funds → interest rates ↑ → private-sector borrowing ↓ → economy slows. |
| **Crowding in** (low borrowing) | When govt borrows less and spends more *(text)*, interest rates fall → private investment rises → economic growth stimulated. |

**Effect via households**
- Government borrowing pulls people out of the private sector — households may have **less money to invest**.
- Less household investment ⇒ slower consumption growth ⇒ slower economy.
- Alternatively, with less to invest, households may chase **risky assets** for higher returns → **asset bubbles and financial instability**.
- **Government borrowing**: the government's process of raising money by issuing bonds or other securities to investors, used to finance government expenditures (infrastructure, health services, defence). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Budget deficit**: the difference between government expenditures and revenues in a year — financed by borrowing.
- **Crowding out**: rise in interest rates and reduction in private-sector borrowing caused by heavy government borrowing competing for funds.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Treating budget deficit as borrowing itself → ✅ Deficit = expenditure − revenue; borrowing is the *means* to finance the deficit.
- ❌ Forgetting the household-side effect → ✅ Government borrowing changes households' available investment funds and risk appetite — possible asset bubbles.

**Quick Recall:**
- Heavy borrowing ⇒ rates ↑ ⇒ **crowding out**.
- Low borrowing + spending ⇒ rates ↓ ⇒ private investment ↑.
- Households squeezed → may chase risk → asset bubbles.

### Nominal and Real Interest Rates — and the Fisher Effect 🔴

**Nominal vs Real**
| | Nominal Interest Rate | Real Interest Rate |
|---|------------------------|---------------------|
| Definition | Accounting rate stated on a loan or investment | Inflation-adjusted rate; true return |
| Inflation accounted? | No | Yes |
| Example | 10% on a savings account | 6% if inflation is 4% (10% − 4%) |
| Example formula | i (stated) | R = i − h (approx) |

**Determinants of nominal rates**
- Demand and supply of money
- Actions of the central government
- **Monetary policy** of the central bank — decreases nominal rates to stimulate during recessions; increases them during inflationary phases
- Other macro factors

**Effects of real interest rates on credit markets**
- **Low real rates** → borrowers more likely to take loans → **credit demand ↑**.
- **High real rates** → borrowers less inclined → **credit demand ↓**.

**The Fisher Effect (exact equation) ⭐⭐ (named theory)**
- $i$ = Nominal interest rate
- $R$ = Real interest rate
- $h$ = Expected inflation rate

**Implication of the Fisher Effect**
- If nominal rate is **constant** and inflation **rises**, real rate **falls** ⇒ favourable to lender or borrower? *Source says: "The lower the purchase price, the better the lender."* — i.e., the lender benefits when purchasing power gain holds up.
- If both nominal rate **and** expected money supply increase **at the same rate**, real rate **does not change**.
- Nominal rate = 10%; Inflation = 4%.
- Real rate (simplified) = 10% − 4% = **6%**.
- **Nominal interest rate**: the accounting rate of interest stated on a particular loan or investment; does not account for inflation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Real interest rate**: rate adjusted for inflation; represents the true return on investment. *(Source's exact wording is "real interest rate equals inflation less the nominal interest rate" — almost certainly a typo; correct relationship is **real ≈ nominal − inflation**, consistent with the worked example.)* ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Fisher Effect**: the relationship between inflation and nominal/real interest rates given by $(1+i) = (1+R)(1+h)$. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Monetary Policy**: the set of tools used by the central bank to control money supply and promote economic growth.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Stating "real = inflation − nominal" (verbatim from source) → ✅ Correct relation is **real ≈ nominal − inflation** (or exactly via the Fisher equation). The example in the source itself confirms 10% − 4% = 6%, contradicting the literal sentence. Use the correct version in answers.
- ❌ Using the simplified Fisher equation when inflation is high → ✅ Use the full equation $(1+i) = (1+R)(1+h)$ in high-inflation contexts; the simplified $i = R + h$ assumes low inflation.

**Quick Recall:**
