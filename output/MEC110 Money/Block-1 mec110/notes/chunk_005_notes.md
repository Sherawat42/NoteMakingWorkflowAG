## Section: Unit 3 Objectives & Introduction 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 3 treats **accounting as the language of business** and the bridge between micro-level firm finances and macro-level national income. It introduces (i) financial statements (balance sheet, income statement, cash flow statement), (ii) real vs financial assets, (iii) national income accounts, (iv) flow of funds accounts, (v) the stock-flow distinction, (vi) exchange rates, (vii) interest rates (nominal and real), and (viii) government borrowings.

> **In Simple Terms:** Accounting is just bookkeeping with rules. Stocks are snapshots ("what I own right now"); flows are videos ("what's coming in and going out"). The unit lifts this idea up to the whole country (national accounts) and uses it to read the financial system.

### Mechanisms / Processes — The accounting cycle
1. **Economic activity** occurs.
2. **Document/voucher** is generated.
3. Vouchers go to **accounts department** → recorded, analysed, kept in books of accounts.
4. **Classification** — posting to ledgers.
5. **Summarising** — trial balance → trading P&L → balance sheet.
6. **Analysis & interpretation.**
7. **Communication** of essential information to users for financial decision-making.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Accounting cycle in 7 steps: **activity → voucher → record → classify → summarise → analyse → communicate**.
> - Accounting = "language of business"; users include owners, management, employees, banks.

## Section: Basic Concepts of Accounting — Stock vs Flow Statements 🔴

### Core Idea
Two **types of statements**: **stock statements** and **flow statements**. The balance sheet is the stock statement. Flow statements come in two kinds — **cash flow statement** and **income statement**. Stock = a snapshot at a point in time; flow = a movie over a period. Both apply to firms and to individuals.

### Key Concepts

| Statement Type | Statement | What it shows | Time perspective |
|----------------|-----------|---------------|------------------|
| Stock | Balance Sheet | Assets, liabilities, net worth | Point in time |
| Flow | Cash Flow Statement | Cash inflows and outflows | Over a period |
| Flow | Income Statement | Revenues recognised and expenses incurred | Over a period |

### 3.2.1 The Balance Sheet
The balance sheet discloses the firm's financial condition at a **specific point in time**. It tells us how much the firm **owns** (assets) and **owes** (liabilities). The difference is **net worth**, which reflects the value of the firm.

**Worked example: Balance Sheet (₹ millions)**

| Particulars | 2020 | 2021 |
|-------------|------|------|
| **Assets** | | |
| Cash | 70 | 90 |
| Accounts Receivable | 210 | 200 |
| Land and Building | 180 | 180 |
| Plant and Equipment | 700 | 720 |
| Inventories | 160 | 140 |
| **Total Assets** | **1320** | **1330** |
| **Liabilities** | | |
| Loan from Bank | 400 | 300 |
| Accounts Payable | 100 | 150 |
| Mortgages and Bonds | 560 | 540 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **1060** | **990** |
| **Net Worth** | **260** | **340** |

Verify: Net Worth = Total Assets − Total Liabilities → 1320 − 1060 = **260** (2020); 1330 − 990 = **340** (2021).

#### Notes on the Balance Sheet line items
- **Cash** = currency + readily available bank deposits.
- **Accounts payable** = creditors and bills payable (owed to suppliers).
- **Bank loans** = liability.
- **Accounts receivable** = debtors and bills receivable (owed by customers); may need adjustment for bad debts.
- **Land/building, plant/equipment** = at **historical cost** (per accounting convention). Some economists prefer current value; to avoid this complexity firms use **GAAP**.
- **Depreciation** = wear-and-tear loss in value; deducted from asset value, so balance sheet shows **depreciated** value.
- **Inventories** = raw materials (inputs) + finished goods + work in progress.
- **Mortgages and bonds** = shown at face/par value per GAAP. But if traded in bond market, the firm values bonds at **market price** if it is *less* than face value (because bonds can be bought back at that market price by the debtor).

### 3.2.2 The Cash Flow Statement
A **cash flow statement** records **inflows and outflows of cash** during the accounting period and groups them into three categories:

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Operating activities** | Cash receipts from sale of goods/services; cash payments to employees. |
| **Investing activities** | Purchase/sale of long-term assets; cash payments to buy fixed assets. |
| **Financing activities** | Issue of shares (preference + equity); cash dividends; changes in owner's capital and borrowings. |

### 3.2.3 The Income Statement
The cash flow statement only shows cash movements; it does **not** show revenues recognised or expenses incurred. The **income statement** does — and it follows the **accrual** principle:
- **Revenues** are recognised in the period when **goods are delivered / services offered** (cash need not have been received yet).
- **Expenses** are recognised when used to generate revenues, regardless of whether cash was paid.

The income statement is prepared **before** the cash flow statement. Therefore, **there is no direct relationship between income earned and cash flows** of a period.

### Definitions
- **Balance Sheet**: statement disclosing the financial condition of a firm at a specific point in time — assets owned, liabilities owed, and net worth. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Net Worth**: difference between assets and liabilities; reflects the value of the firm. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Depreciation**: loss in asset value because of wear and tear; deducted from asset value; balance sheet shows depreciated value.
- **GAAP** (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles): commonly followed accounting rules and standards for financial reporting; aim is to prohibit misrepresentation and provide accounting insight. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cash Flow Statement**: statement of cash inflows and outflows during the accounting period, grouped into operating/investing/financing.
- **Income Statement**: statement displaying revenues recognised and expenses incurred during a period (accrual-based, not cash-based).
- **Accounts payable**: creditors and bills payable (amount owed to suppliers).
- **Accounts receivable**: debtors and bills receivable (amount owed by customers).
- **Inventories**: raw materials, finished products, and work-in-progress held by the firm.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Treating income earned = cash received → ✅ Revenue is recognised on delivery, expenses on use; cash may flow in/out at different times. Income ≠ cash.
- ❌ Listing assets at current market value on the balance sheet → ✅ GAAP uses **historical cost less depreciation** (with explicit exceptions like bonds, where market price below face value is used).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Stock statement = Balance Sheet** (point in time). **Flow statements = Cash Flow + Income Statement** (over a period).
> - **Net Worth = Assets − Liabilities** (2020: 260; 2021: 340 in the example).
> - Cash flow has 3 buckets: **Operating · Investing · Financing**.
> - Income statement is **accrual**, cash flow is **cash basis** — they don't match.

## Section: Real Assets and Financial Assets 🔴

### Core Idea
Real and financial assets are the two fundamental kinds of assets, with different features, risk profiles, and balance-sheet treatments. Real assets are **tangible** (real estate, land, machinery, commodities) and have intrinsic value from physical properties or usefulness. Financial assets are **intangible** (stocks, bonds, mutual funds, currencies) and represent **claims** on cash flows or ownership rights. Their values are dynamically related — changes in one influence the other.

> **In Simple Terms:** A real asset is something you can touch (a building); a financial asset is a piece of paper that gives you a claim (a share in the company that owns the building). When the building's value rises, the share usually does too.

### Key Concepts

| Feature | Real Assets | Financial Assets |
|---------|-------------|------------------|
| Nature | **Tangible** | **Intangible** |
| Examples | Real estate, land, machinery, commodities | Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, currencies |
| Source of value | Intrinsic (fair) value from physical properties or usefulness | Claim on expected cash flows or ownership rights |
| Inflation behaviour | Often used as a hedge against inflation | Demand/supply forces in markets set value |
| Income generation | Can generate income or appreciate over time | Pay interest/dividend or capital gain |

#### Two-way interaction (real ↔ financial)
- **Real → Financial**: rise in real-estate value lifts **REITs** (real-estate investment trusts, a financial asset). Commodity-price swings move commodity-mutual-fund returns.
- **Financial → Real**: rising **interest rates** make borrowing expensive → demand for housing falls → real-estate values fall. Stock-market swings affect investor confidence → real-asset investment decisions.

#### Disclosure on the balance sheet ⭐
- **Real assets (PPE — Property, Plant, Equipment)**: shown as **non-current assets**. Useful life > 1 year (land, buildings, machinery, equipment). Reported at **historical cost less accumulated depreciation**. Also includes intangibles like patents, copyrights, trademarks (real value but not physical).
- **Financial assets**: classified as **current** (convertible to cash within 1 year) or **non-current** (held > 1 year), depending on liquidity. Reported at **fair market value** / market price at the balance-sheet date.

### Definitions
- **Real assets**: tangible assets — real estate, land, machinery, commodities — with intrinsic value from physical properties or usefulness; can generate income or appreciate; often used as inflation hedge. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial assets**: intangible assets — stocks, bonds, mutual funds, currencies — representing a claim to expected cash flows or certain ownership rights. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **PPE (Property, Plant, Equipment)**: long-term real assets with useful life > 1 year, reported at historical cost less accumulated depreciation.
- **REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts)**: financial assets that invest in real-estate properties.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Treating REITs as real assets → ✅ REITs are **financial assets** that hold real assets — they are claims, traded in markets.
- ❌ Reporting financial assets at historical cost → ✅ Financial assets are reported at **fair market value**, unlike PPE which uses **historical cost − depreciation**.
- ❌ Saying intangibles can't be real assets → ✅ Patents/copyrights/trademarks count as real assets even though intangible — value comes from underlying utility, not from a market claim.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **Real = tangible (with intangible exceptions like patents); Financial = claims**.
> - PPE → historical cost − depreciation (non-current). Financial assets → market value (current or non-current).
> - REIT example: bridge between real and financial.

## Section: The National Accounts 🔴

### Core Idea
National accounts are the macro counterpart of firm-level accounting. **National Income** (total income earned by residents) and **National Product** (total value of goods/services produced) are two flow concepts used to measure the country's economic performance. National Product is captured by GDP and GNP; NNP adjusts GNP for depreciation.

### Key Concepts

#### Two flows in macro

| Concept | What it is | How computed |
|---------|------------|--------------|
| **National Income** | Total income earned by a country's residents | Sum wages + salaries + profits + rental income, over a period (usually a year). |
| **National Product** | Total value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in a period | Sum value of all final goods and services, regardless of who produced them. Includes both market-traded and personal-use goods (e.g., homemade vegetables). |

#### GDP — two ways to measure

| Approach | Components |
|----------|-----------|
| **Production side** | 3 sectors: agriculture · industry · services |
| **Consumption / Expenditure side** | **Private Consumption + Investment + Government Expenditure + Net Exports** |

**Expenditure-side components (in detail):**
- **Private Consumption** — household spending on durables (cars, appliances), non-durables (food, clothing), services (healthcare, education).
- **Investment expenditure** — business spending on new capital goods (machinery, buildings) and R&D.
- **Government expenditure** — public infrastructure, defence, social programs.
- **Net exports** — exports − imports. Positive ⇒ trade surplus; negative ⇒ trade deficit.

#### From GDP to NNP

| Step | Formula | What it adds/subtracts |
|------|---------|------------------------|
| GDP at factor cost (production side) | Sum of value-added | — |
| GDP at market prices | GDP at factor cost + indirect taxes − subsidies | Aligns with expenditure on GDP. |
| GNP at factor cost | GDP at factor cost + foreign income (repatriated income, profits, royalties from abroad) | Adds factor income from abroad. |
| **NNP** | GNP − depreciation | Net production after replacing capital used up. |

### Definitions
- **National Income**: total income earned by a country's residents — wages, salaries, profits, rental income — over a year. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **National Product**: total value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year, regardless of producer; includes market-traded and personal-use goods.
- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**: broad measure of an economy's output of goods and services; compilation of value-added output. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **GNP (Gross National Product) at factor cost**: GDP at factor cost + foreign income (repatriated income, profits, royalties from abroad).
- **NNP (Net National Product)**: GNP − depreciation; net production after capital is replaced. Useful for analysing long-run capital stock dynamics.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Treating GDP and GNP as identical → ✅ GNP = GDP + foreign income earned by residents abroad.
- ❌ Forgetting depreciation when going from GNP to NNP → ✅ NNP = GNP − depreciation.
- ❌ Listing only 3 expenditure components of GDP → ✅ There are **4**: C, I, G, NX.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - **GDP (expenditure) = C + I + G + NX**.
> - **GNP = GDP at factor cost + foreign income.**
> - **NNP = GNP − Depreciation.**
> - Production-side GDP: agri + industry + services.

## Section: Flow of Funds Accounts (FFA) and Goldsmith's Financial-Development Ratios 🔴

### Core Idea
The **Flow of Funds Accounts (FFA)** is a macroeconomic tool that tracks the flow of funds within an economy across sectors and instruments. It is the financial analogue of national income accounts. From the FFA, **Goldsmith (1969)** derived a set of ratios — the **financial deepening ratios** — that quantify how developed a country's financial system is.

### Key Concepts

#### Structure of the FFA
- Maintained by the **Federal Reserve System of the United States** (the original source).
- Covers sectors: **households, businesses, government, banking and financial institutions, rest of the world**.
- Tracks the movement of funds between sectors via instruments — stocks, bonds, loans.
- Two sections:
  - **Asset and Liability section** — financial assets and liabilities of each sector.
  - **Transactions section** — financial transactions between the sectors.

#### Information offered by FFA
1. Variation in **net worth** of each sector.
2. **Credit outstanding** in each sector.
3. **Equity outstanding** in each sector.
4. **Flow of funds** between sectors (e.g., household savings → business / govt securities).
5. **Composition of financial assets and liabilities** of each sector — which instruments are held.

The FFA serves policymakers, researchers, analysts, and investors in identifying risks, monitoring investment-pattern trends, and informing monetary/fiscal policy decisions.

#### Instrument-wise classification of financial claims
- **Primary issues / Direct securities** — all kinds of debt issued by ultimate borrowers (marketable or otherwise).
- **Secondary issues / Indirect securities** — debt and claims issued by **financial intermediaries** to acquire primary securities.

**Total flow of finance** = funds raised by **Primary issues + Secondary issues**.

#### Goldsmith's financial-deepening ratios ⭐⭐ (named theory — high-yield)

| Ratio | Formula | Indicates |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **Finance Ratio (FR)** | $$FR = \frac{\text{Total Financial Claims (Primary + Secondary issues)}}{\text{National Income}}$$ | Financial structure of economic growth — how large the financial sector is relative to income. |
| **Financial Inter-Relation Ratio (FIR)** | $$FIR = \frac{\text{Financial Assets (Total Issues)}}{\text{Physical Assets (Net Domestic Capital Formation)}}$$ | Relationship between financial structure and the real-asset structure of the economy; indicator of financial issues vs capital formation. |
| **New Issue Ratio (NIR)** | $$NIR = \frac{\text{Primary Issues}}{\text{Net Physical Investments (Net Domestic Capital Formation)}}$$ | How primary/direct issues to savers have financed the investment by investing sectors. |
| **Intermediation Ratio (IR)** | $$IR = \frac{\text{Total Secondary Issues}}{\text{Total Primary Issues}}$$ | Significance of financial intermediaries in channelling resources; ratio of FI-issued claims to non-FI-issued claims. |

### Definitions
- **Flow of Funds Accounts (FFA)**: macroeconomic tool tracking the flow of funds within an economy across sectors and instruments; provides information on generation, distribution, utilisation of funds. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Primary issues / Direct securities** (in FFA context): debt issued by ultimate borrowers.
- **Secondary issues / Indirect securities** (in FFA context): debt and claims issued by financial intermediaries to acquire primary securities.
- **Goldsmith's Financial Deepening Ratios** (1969): a set of ratios — FR, FIR, NIR, IR — that measure financial development. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Finance Ratio (FR)**: Total Financial Claims / National Income.
- **Financial Inter-Relation Ratio (FIR)**: Total Financial Issues / Net Domestic Capital Formation.
- **New Issue Ratio (NIR)**: Primary Issues / Net Domestic Capital Formation.
- **Intermediation Ratio (IR)**: Total Secondary Issues / Total Primary Issues.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mixing up NIR and IR → ✅ **NIR** uses *primary issues / capital formation* (real-economy denominator); **IR** uses *secondary / primary* issues (within-financial-sector ratio).
- ❌ Saying total finance = primary issues only → ✅ **Total flow of finance = primary + secondary** issues.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - FFA originated with the **US Federal Reserve**.
> - **Goldsmith (1969)** = 4 ratios: **FR, FIR, NIR, IR**.
> - FR uses **National Income** denominator; FIR/NIR use **Net Domestic Capital Formation** denominator; IR is **secondary/primary**.
