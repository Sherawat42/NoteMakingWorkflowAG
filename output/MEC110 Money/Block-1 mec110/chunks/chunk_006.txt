3.6  THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN STOCKS 
AND FLOWS 
In accounting, stock implies the quantity or value of something that is 
measured at a specific point in time. Example: bank balance, inventory 
balance and debt owed by a company.  On the other hand, flow implies the 
speed at which something is changing over some time. Example: the balance 
comes in and out of the bank, the rate at which the inventory is bought and 
sold or the revenue generation by the company.  
Both are vital concepts from the accounting point of view. A company can 
gain insight regarding its financial position by keeping an eye on the stock of 
certain assets and liabilities. This helps the business firm or company to 
interpret how their financial position is wavering over time and can gain 
knowledge regarding the effectiveness of operations.  
Stocks and flows play an important role in the Indian financial system. In 
financial terms, stocks refer to the current value of assets, while flows refer to 
the movement of funds or money in the economy. There are certain instances 
which show how financial system is impacted by stocks and flows: 
1) Investment: Investment is highly impacted in the Indian financial system.
Stock is known as the value of shares held by investors in a company.
The high stock price of a company reflects that the company’s prospects
are good, and investors may show their readiness to invest in it. This
leads to a rise in the flow of funds into the company, which can then be
utilized for further investment.
2) Capital markets:  The Indian capital markets are heavily impacted by
stocks and flows. The stock market is the place where there exists trading
of securities, and the flow of funds into the market ascertains the value of
stocks. The heavy inflow of funds into the market enhances the demand
for stocks thereby leading to an increase in stock prices. This, in turn,
boosts the market value.
3) Banking sector: Stocks and flows also influenced the banking sector. A
high inflow of funds into the economy leads the banks to lend more
funds, ultimately increasing the supply of credit. This, in turn, leads to an
increase in investment and economic growth.
4) Foreign investment:  Stocks and flows positively attract foreign
investment to India. When a high inflow of funds is generated into the
economy, it signifies a robust economy thereby attracting foreign

3.7  EXCHANGE RATES 
Exchange rates are an important part of international financial markets. They 
express the value of one currency to another. Exchange rates have a huge 
impact on the economy, including gross domestic product (GDP) and 
government debt. In this section, we will examine the history of exchange 
rates, their role in international financial markets, and the various factors that 
affect exchange rates. The history of exchange rates goes back to the early 
days of commerce when people began to exchange goods and services. 
However, it wasn’t until the 19 th century that exchange rates began to work. 
The gold standard was introduced in 1875 and fixed its value in gold. The 
system existed until the First World War when the country began to abandon 
it due to financial constraints. 
After the Second World War, the Bretton Woods system was established to 
fix the value of US gold and other currencies to the US dollar. However, in 
the early 1970s, the system collapsed due to the economic crisis and the US’s 
failure to maintain the gold standard. 
Today, most countries have a floating exchange rate, meaning the value of 
their currencies is determined by the market forces of supply and demand. 
The system is more flexible and less susceptible to financial losses.

Exchange rates are related to international financial markets in many ways. 
For example, the exchange rate affects the value of imports and exports, 
which affects a country’s balance of payments. Exchange rates also affect the 
value of a country’s assets, such as stocks and bonds. Changes in exchange 
rates can cause significant changes in financial markets and investors should 
take this into account in their investment decisions. 
One of the most important effects of the exchange rate is on GDP. When a 
country’s currency strengthens, its exports become more expensive, resulting 
in fewer exports and lower GDP. Conversely, when a country’s currency is 
weak, its exports become cheaper, leading to higher exports and higher GDP. 
For example, let’s take the exchange rate between the US dollar and the Euro. 
The exchange rate of 1 USD = 0.85 EUR. If the dollar strengthens against the 
euro, the exchange rate will change to $1 = €0.80. This will make U.S. 
exports more expensive to European countries, which could lead to lower 
U.S. exports and lower GDP. 
Government borrowing is also affected by exchange rates. When the 
country’s currency weakens, it becomes more expensive for the government 
to borrow from foreign investors. This can lead to higher borrowing costs and 
lower government spending. Conversely, when a country’s currency is 
strong, it becomes cheaper for the government to borrow money, leading to 
lower borrowing costs and more government spending. 
For example, consider the exchange rate between the Japanese Yen and the 
US Dollar. 
Suppose the exchange rate is 110 Yen = $1. If the yen weakens against the 
dollar, the exchange rate will change to 120 yen = $1. This would make it 
more expensive for the Japanese government to borrow money. 
In summary, exchange rates play an important role in international financial 
markets. Understanding the history and factors that affect exchange rates is 
important for investors and policymakers. The exchange rate affects GDP, 
government borrowing and the financial market, and changes in the exchange 
rate have a significant impact on the economy. By monitoring exchange rates 
and taking steps to contain their effects, countries can help ensure stability 
and growth.

borrowing used by individuals, businesses and governments to finance 
investment and business. In this section, we will examine the history of 
interest, its post-exchange rate policy and its impact on the economy.

With the emergence of money, the concept of interest emerged. The interest 
rate reflects the cost of the loan and is usually expressed as a percentage of 
income. Interest rates allow individuals, businesses and governments to 
borrow money to invest and operate and provide a way for borrowers to get a

An example of the use of interest is in the real estate sector. When a person

promise to repay the money and interest at a certain rate. Interest rates on 
government bonds are determined by economic forces that reflect the 
government’s borrowing risk. If investors perceive that the government is a 
risky borrower, they will charge higher interest rates to cover the risk. 
Interest rates also affect the economy as a whole. When interest rates are 
high, it discourages lending and investment, which slows the economy. 
Conversely, when interest rates are low, it encourages lending and 
investment, which stimulates economic growth. 
As was witnessed after the 2008 financial crisis, the central banks around the 
world lowered interest rates to encourage lending and investment. For 
example, the Federal Reserve lowered the cost of borrowing for businesses 
and individuals by lowering the federal funds rate to zero. This contributes to 
the growth of the economy and prevents further declines. 
Interest rates were introduced after the barter system as a way to measure the

borrowers to get a return on their investment. Interest rates can affect the 
entire economy, and their use is monitored by policymakers and investors. 
3.9  GOVERNMENT BORROWINGS 
Government borrowing is the government’s process of raising money by 
issuing bonds or other securities to investors. These funds are used to finance 
government expenditures such as infrastructure, health services and defence. 
In this section, we will examine the government borrowing process, how it is 
used to illuminate the impact of economic policy, and the role of the family 
in the economy. 
Investors such as banks, pension funds, and individual investors purchase 
government securities and lend money to the government. The government 
then uses the proceeds of these securities to fund its expenditures.

One way to use government debt to illustrate the impact of monetary policy 
on the economy is the concept of government deficit. The government’s 
budget deficit is the difference between government expenditures and 
revenues in a year. If the government spends more than necessary, it runs a 
budget deficit that must be financed by borrowing. 
The size of the government’s budget deficit can have a significant impact on 
the economy. When the government borrows to finance its spending, they 
compete with private lenders for money. This could lead to higher interest 
rates affecting the private sector and slowing the economy. On the other 
hand, if the government borrows less and spends more, it can help lower 
interest rates and stimulate private investment, which stimulates economic 
growth. 
Another way government loans affect the economy is through the role of the 
households. When the government borrows to finance its spending, it pulls 
more people out of the private sector, which means households have less 
money to invest. This affects the wealth of households and their consumption 
patterns, which in turn affects the economy. 
For example, if households have less money to invest, it will slow economic 
growth by reducing their consumption of goods and services. Or, if 
households have less money to invest, they may choose to invest in risky 
assets to make more money. This can lead to asset bubbles and financial 
instability. 
3.10  NOMINAL AND REAL INTEREST RATES 
The nominal interest rate refers to the accounting rate of interest stated on a 
particular loan or investment. It does not take into account the impact of 
inflation on the purchasing power of money. For example, if a bank offers a 
10% interest rate on a savings account, the account holder will earn 10% 
interest on their deposits, regardless of the inflation rate. 
Nominal interest rates can be influenced by various factors, including the 
demand and supply of money, the actions of the central government, the 
monetary policy of the central bank, and many others. Central banks 
implement short-term nominal interest rates as a monetary policy. The 
nominal rate is usually decreased to stimulate economic activity during 
recessions. However, the nominal rate increases during the inflationary 
phase. 
On the other hand, the real interest rate is adjusted for inflation and represents 
the true return on investment after accounting for inflation.  The real interest 
rate equals inflation less the nominal interest rate. For example, if the 
nominal interest rate is 10% and the inflation rate is 4%, the real interest rate 
would be 6% (10% - 4%).

Real interest rates are particularly significant in financial markets as they 
provide a more accurate measure of return on investment. A high nominal 
interest rate may appear attractive, but if the inflation rate is high, it can erode 
the purchasing power of the investment. Therefore, investors often look at 
real interest rates when evaluating investment opportunities. 
In addition, real interest rates can also influence credit supply and demand in 
financial markets. For instance, when real interest rates are low, borrowers 
may be more likely to take out loans to finance their investments. This may 
lead to an increase in credit demand. Conversely, when real interest rates are 
high, borrowers may be less inclined to borrow, which can lead to a decrease 
in credit demand. 
Overall, both nominal and real interest rates are critical concepts in finance. 
Understanding their differences and their impact on financial markets is 
crucial for investors, borrowers, and lenders alike. 
Inflation Adjustment of the Nominal Interest Rate: 
The Fisher Effect explains the relationship between inflation and nominal or 
real interest rates through the equation below: 
(1 + i) = (1 + R) (1 + h) 
Where: 
i – Nominal interest rate 
R – Real interest rate 
h – Expected inflation rate 
In a stable economy that grows at a moderate pace, the inflation rate is 
usually low. With a low inflation rate, a simplified Fisher equation can be 
implemented. According to this formula, the nominal interest rate is equal to 
the real interest rate plus inflation (i = R + h). 
A bond investor, for example, expects a real interest rate of 10%, when the 
market expects inflation of 4%. For this reason, investors should search for 
bonds with stated (nominal) interest rates of 14% (10% + 4%). 
Using the formula below, you can calculate the nominal interest rate. 
i = (1 + R) (1 + h) – 1 = (1 + 10%) (1 + 4%) – 1 = 14.4% 
According to the Fisher effect, if the nominal interest rate remains constant 
and the inflation rate rises, the real interest rate falls. The lower the purchase 
price, the better the lender. If both the nominal interest rate and the expected 
money supply increase at the same rate, that is, if the nominal interest rate 
increases, the real interest rate does not change.
