1.5  FINANCIAL INSTRUMENTS 
Any asset which holds capital and can be traded in the market is referred to as 
a financial instrument. A financial instrument, an intangible asset is a claim 
against a person or an institution for the payment at a future date of a sum of 
money and/or a periodic payment in the form of interest or dividend. It is a 
tradable asset representing a legal agreement or a contractual right to 
evidence the monetary value/ownership interest of an entity. Financial 
instruments are typically traded in financial markets where the price of a 
security is arrived at based on the play of market forces. 
Key financial instruments that are traded in the stock market include shares, 
stocks, bonds, derivatives, mutual funds, cheques etc. Investing in financial 
instruments carries a financial risk. 
1.5.1 Types of Financial Instruments 
There are typically three types of financial instruments: cash instruments, 
derivative instruments, and foreign exchange instruments. Furthermore, the 
financial instruments can be classified based on the ‘asset classes’ into 
‘equity-based’ or ‘debt-based’. If the instrument is debt, then it can be further 
categorized as ‘short-term debt’ and ‘long-term debt’. Foreign exchange 
instruments and transactions are neither debt- nor equity-based and belong in 
their category.

Non-complex. Complex financial instruments require in-depth knowledge for 
traders to be successful when trading them. The most commonly traded 
complex financial instruments are derivatives. These can be contracts for

2.2  CONCEPT OF FINANCIAL INTERMEDIATION 
2.2.1  Direct and Indirect Finance 
The two primary ways through which funds are channelled from savers to 
borrowers in an economy are direct and indirect finance. These concepts 
relate to the mechanisms by which financial resources move between those 
who have excess funds (savers) and those who need funds (borrowers). In 
direct finance, funds are directly provided by savers to borrowers in the 
financial markets without the involvement of any intermediary. In indirect 
finance, there is a special kind of ‘middleman’, a third party that stands 
between borrowers and lenders. This ‘middleman’ is known as a financial 
intermediary. The role of a financial intermediary is to accumulate funds 
from parties that have excess funds (the savers) and make those funds 
available to those who have deficient funds and need those funds (the 
borrowers). While we will discuss various types of financial intermediaries 
shortly, let us discuss here one of the financial intermediaries: banks. Banks 
are financial intermediaries because they accept deposits, and then provide 
loans from these funds to borrowers.  Banks pay depositors interest on their 
deposits and receive interest payments from borrowers on the loan advanced.  
The bank is in the middle, as an intermediary and aims to earn a profit on the 
spread between the rate of interest charged to borrowers and the rate of 
interest paid to depositors. 
As mentioned, a financial intermediary is like a middleman; so is a broker 
who facilitates exchange between borrowers and lenders in financial markets 
where direct finance is involved. But there is a difference between brokers 
and financial intermediaries: a broker brings together borrowers and lenders 
but does not lend to borrowers or issue bonds. All that a broker does is 
facilitate transactions involving direct finance and charge a commission for 
his services rendered. A broker does not create any financial instrument. In 
contrast to this, financial intermediaries lend to borrowers by creating and 
issuing their securities, for example: bonds. A financial intermediary does not 
merely bring borrowers and lenders together; financial intermediaries 
themselves give loans to lenders. They earn profits by repackaging deposits 
of savers into loans, and themselves lending to borrowers. 
2.2.2  Intermediaries and Non-intermediaries 
Intermediaries intermediate between savers and investors; they lend money as 
well as mobilize savings; their liabilities are towards the ultimate savers, 
while their assets are from the investors or borrowers. Non-intermediary 
institutions do the loan business but their resources are not directly obtained 
from the savers. All banking institutions are intermediaries, many non-
banking institutions also act as intermediaries and when they do so, they are 
known as non-banking financial intermediaries.

Financial institutions as intermediaries work in tandem with each other to 
create a fully interdependent financial system. The importance of financial 
institutions lies in the fact that they provide services which lead to a smooth 
functioning of the financial system. 
They allow the exchange of funds 
between lenders, investors, and borrowers. There are three aspects to the 
process by which saving is channelled to investors through the financial 
system: (i) the amount of savings and the resulting investment that are raised 
in the economy, (ii) the question of who saves and who invests and how do 
they do that respectively, and finally, (iii) the flow of funds through financial 
institutions and markets as money passes on its way from savers to investors.

and how well the financial system and its institutions are organised to 
facilitate these stages contributes to the growth of economic activity. Some of 
these functions are as under:
