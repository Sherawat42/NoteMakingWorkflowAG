2.5 AN OVERVIEW OF INDIAN FINANCIAL 
SYSTEM 
The Indian financial system exhibits two prominent segments: the organized 
sector and the unorganized sector. The organized sector includes commercial, 
development, and cooperative banks, the stock market, and diverse non-
banking financial institutions like insurance corporations and mutual funds. 
The unorganized sector is the informal credit market which provides loans to 
farmers, small enterprises and individuals. These two sectors vary majorly in 
terms of rates of interest and credit conditions.  
The financial system was underdeveloped until independence. After 1947, 
the government started creating new financial institutions for the supply 
of finance for both industrial and agricultural purposes. For this purpose, 
several steps have been taken since independence. Some of them are: 
1)  Nationalization of financial institutions : Some important financial 
institutions at that time were nationalized. For example,  
a) In the year 1948, the Reserve Bank of India (which was established 
in the year 1935 as a private-sector central bank) was nationalized. 
b) In the year 1955, the then Imperial Bank of India was nationalized 
and renamed as State Bank of India. 
c) In the year 1956, 245 life insurance business entities (consisting 
of 154 life insurance companies, 16 foreign companies and 75 
provident companies) were nationalized and merged to form 
Life Insurance Corporation(LIC) of India.  
d) In the year 1969, 14 Commercial Banks were nationalized. 
e) In the year 1972, 107 general insurance business entities 
(consisting of 55 Indian insurance companies and 52 other general 
insurance operations of other companies) were nationalized and 
merged to form the General Insurance Corporation (GIC) of 
India.   
f) In the year 1980, another 6 Commercial Banks were nationalized. 
2)  Establishment of new institutions: 
i)  Unit Trust of India (UTI) – UTI was established in the year 1964 
to strengthen the Indian financial system and supply institutional 
credit to industries. It was entrusted with the work of 
mobilisation of the savings of the public and provision of credit 
facilities to institutions for productive purposes. It has 
established the following subsidiaries:

The structure and evolution of the Indian financial system in the late 1960s 
closely reflected a growing degree of public ownership and control of 
financial institutions in keeping with the political priorities of the time. The 
extensive control of the financial system was aimed at ensuring the flow of 
finances in the desired directions and in particular to priority areas of 
development. With the economic reforms of 1991, the financial system has 
undergone a structural change with the private sector now playing a 
substantial role. However, it suffers from one major weakness i.e., high over 
dues or non-performing assests of the lenders which affects many Indian 
financial institutions. It is a problem in both industrial and agricultural 
finance although the situation is on the mend in the recent years.   
In short, the evolution of the financial system since 1950 has increased the 
“financializaton” of the Indian economy.  From a gross saving rate of about 8 
per cent for the country at the time of independence, it has gone up to 35  
per cent at its highest, declining to about 30 per cent in the most recent years. 
Importantly, from a preponderance of physical saving at over 90 per cent in 
the gross saving rate of the country, at the time of independence, it has come 
down to about 50 per cent in the recent years. It indicates that an increased 
role of the public sector in the financial system has not retarded its 
development. Underpinning this financial deepening is an increase in the 
assets of the commercial banks. There is an increase in the importance of 
financial institutions in the economy and of financial flows relative to 
economic activity. This indicates that the financial system is playing an 
increasingly active role in the transfer of funds from the surplus to the deficit 
sectors in the economy. Financial intermediation in India’s organized sector 
is conducted by a wide range and number of financial institutions which have 
largely operated in major cities and towns but with growing presence in rural 
areas, aided by the expansion of digital infrastructure across the country, in 
recent years.

2.6  REGULATION OF FINANCIAL 
INSTITUTIONS 
The government needs to oversee and regulate banks and financial 
institutions because they play a critical role in the economy. Historically, 
bankruptcies of financial institutions have created panic and instability in the 
financial system. A well-regulated financial system inspires confidence 
among investors, depositors, and the general public. The regulation of 
financial institutions in India is overseen by several regulatory bodies. These 
organisations play a crucial role in streamlining the functioning of the 
financial system and protecting the interests of customers. The regulation 
of financial institutions in India is important for various reasons. Regulation 
helps maintain the stability of the financial system by preventing excessive 
risk-taking, fraud, and other activities that could lead to systemic crises. 
Regulations in the financial sector also aim to safeguard the interests of 
consumers especially small savers. The interest of the customers can be 
protected by operating transparently and adhering to ethical business 
practices. 
Financial institutions are subject to regulations that require them to 
implement robust risk management practices. This helps prevent 
excessive risk-taking and ensures that institutions are adequately prepared 
for adverse economic conditions. 
Another important reason for financial institutions being highly regulated is 
that most people will not be confident in investing in an unregulated 
institution. Thus, the regulation of financial institutions also aims to gain and 
retain customer confidence. Confidence in turn is crucial for the smooth 
functioning of financial markets and institutions. 
In every country, government agencies oversee the regulation of financial 
institutions, formulating rules and guidelines for the sector while also 
ensuring compliance through monitoring.

Overview 2.7 LET US SUM UP 
A Financial Institution (FI) is an entity engaged in the business of dealing 
with financial and monetary transactions such as deposits, loans, investments, 
and currency exchange. They encompass a broad range of business 
operations within the financial services sector including banks, trust 
companies, insurance companies, brokerage firms, and investment dealers. 
FIs can vary by size, scope, and geography. FIs offer a wide range of 
products and services for individual and commercial clients. The specific 
services offered vary widely between different types of financial institutions. 
When each agent and constituent functions well, the financial system as a 
whole is more stable. The health of the financial sector rests on a delicate 
balance between the various functions of financial institutions. Problems with 
one function can reflect on the other capacities of the financial system as 
well. 
The financial institutions provide the best instruments to save and invest 
money and to earn good returns from that investment. They try to help 
nations in building up economies. However, there could be some risks 
associated with the functioning of some institutions at times and when there 
is a default, in the system there could be distress and loss for economic 
agents. It is therefore important to have a robust regulation for the a stable 
functioning of a financial system. Indeed, the health of a nation’s banking 
system is a linchpin of economic stability.
