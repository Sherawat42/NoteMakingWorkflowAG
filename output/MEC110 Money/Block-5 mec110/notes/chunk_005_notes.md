## Section: Housing Finance Companies (HFCs) 🔴

### Core Idea
Housing Finance Companies are corporate entities under the Companies Act, 1956 whose principal business is providing housing loans. They hold roughly one-third of India's housing finance market — the second-largest provider after Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs). Following the 2019-20 Union Budget, the National Housing Bank Act 1987 was amended to transfer regulatory powers (including HFC registration) from NHB to RBI with effect from August 9, 2019.

> **In Simple Terms:** HFCs are non-bank lenders who specialise in home loans. Earlier the housing-sector regulator (NHB) was their parent supervisor; now the RBI itself watches them like it does banks — because if they fail, the shock could spread through the whole system.

### Key Concepts

#### Definition & Market Share of HFCs
HFCs are corporate entities operating under the Companies Act of 1956 whose primary focus is providing housing loans or finance via direct/indirect means. They command about one-third of the housing-finance market in India, second only to SCBs. To qualify as an HFC, an NBFC must hold at least 50% of its assets as housing loans, and 75% of those housing loans must be to individual homebuyers. Asset quality, profitability and credit disbursement growth all rose in 2022-23, supported by affordable-housing policy push, demographics and urbanisation. Some HFCs run Co-Lending Models (CLM) with banks for housing and MSME loans.

#### Shift of Regulator: NHB → RBI
Earlier, NHB was the regulator of HFCs. The amendment to the National Housing Bank Act, 1987 (pursuant to Union Budget 2019-20) transferred regulation, including registration, to RBI with effect from **August 9, 2019**. RBI now ensures HFCs comply with principal business criteria — Net Owned Fund (NOF) requirements, Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR), and Capital Adequacy Ratio (CRAR).

#### Scale-Based Regulation (SBR) Placement of HFCs
RBI has notified that HFCs will sit only in the Middle Layer or Upper Layer of the four-layer SBR pyramid (Base / Middle / Upper / Top). The top ten eligible NBFCs (HFCs included) by asset size always reside in the Upper Layer. RBI included **5 HFCs in the Upper Layer for 2023-24**, signalling their systemic importance. If RBI judges a particular HFC to pose significant risk, it may be moved from the Upper Layer to the Top Layer.

| Layer | HFC Placement |
|-------|---------------|
| Base Layer (BL) | HFCs NOT placed here |
| Middle Layer (ML) | Default placement for HFCs |
| Upper Layer (UL) | 5 HFCs in 2023-24 (systemically important / top-ten asset size) |
| Top Layer (TL) | Reserved for HFCs RBI deems significantly risky |

### Definitions
- **HFC (Housing Finance Company)**: A corporate entity under the Companies Act 1956 primarily providing housing finance, with ≥ 50% assets as housing loans and ≥ 75% of those to individual homebuyers. ⭐
- **NOF (Net Owned Fund)**: Principal-business yardstick used by RBI to assess HFC compliance. ⭐
- **LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio)**: Buffer of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) sufficient to survive a 30-day acute liquidity stress. ⭐
- **CRAR / CAR (Capital Adequacy Ratio)**: Minimum capital ratio HFCs must maintain on an ongoing basis. ⭐
- **LTV (Loan-To-Value) Ratio**: Cap on loan amount as a % of collateral value (50% for listed shares; 75% for gold jewellery for HFCs).
- **Co-Lending Model (CLM)**: Joint lending arrangement between HFCs and banks (housing & MSME loans).

### Mechanisms / Processes
**The 11 RBI regulatory rules for HFCs:**
1. All non-deposit-taking HFCs with asset size ≥ ₹100 crore and all deposit-taking HFCs (any asset size) must pursue **liquidity risk management**.
2. Liquidity risk management must cover gap-limit adherence, use of liquidity-risk monitoring tools, and a **stock approach to liquidity risk**.
3. HFCs must maintain a liquidity buffer in the form of **LCR** — sufficient HQLA to survive a 30-day acute liquidity stress.
4. **LTV cap** — 50% on loans against listed shares as collateral; 75% on loans against gold-jewellery collateral.
5. HFCs cannot accept/renew **public deposits** unless they have obtained a **minimum investment-grade rating** for fixed deposits from an approved credit rating agency at least once a year.
6. HFCs must keep **full cover** for public deposits at all times. If an HFC defaults on a public deposit, it cannot grant any loan/credit, make any investment, or create any other asset until the default is cured.
7. HFCs **cannot lend against their own shares**.
8. **Minimum CAR**: not less than **13% by March 2020**, **14% by March 2021**, and **15% by March 2022**.
9. **Single-borrower exposure** ≤ 15% of owned fund; **single-group exposure** ≤ 25% of owned fund.
10. **Investment in shares**: ≤ 15% of owned funds in another company; ≤ 25% of owned funds in a single group of companies.
11. **Aggregate capital-market exposure** (fund-based + non-fund-based) ≤ **40% of net worth** as on March 31 of the previous year.

### Examples
**Example: SBR placement of an HFC**
A large HFC ranks among the top-ten eligible NBFCs by asset size — it automatically resides in the Upper Layer. If RBI later deems it to carry "significant risk," the same HFC can be moved to the Top Layer for tighter scrutiny.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: HFCs sit in the Base Layer like small NBFCs → ✅ Correct: RBI explicitly places HFCs only in Middle or Upper (and possibly Top) Layer.
- ❌ Mistake: NHB still regulates HFCs → ✅ Correct: Regulatory powers shifted from NHB to RBI on **August 9, 2019**.
- ❌ Mistake: 50% of housing loans must go to individuals → ✅ Correct: 50% of *total assets* must be housing loans, and 75% of those *housing loans* must be to individual homebuyers.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The CAR schedule (13/14/15%) escalates yearly; quote the year specifically in answers.
- LTV thresholds differ by collateral type — listed shares (50%) vs. gold (75%).
- Capital-market exposure cap (40% of net worth) uses the previous March 31 net worth — not current.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - HFC qualification: **50% / 75%** rule (housing loans / individual homebuyers).
> - Regulator shift: NHB → RBI from **9 Aug 2019**.
> - SBR: ML/UL only; **5 HFCs in UL for 2023-24**.
> - CAR ladder: **13% (Mar 2020) → 14% (Mar 2021) → 15% (Mar 2022)**.
> - Capital-market exposure cap: **40%** of net worth.

## Section: All India Financial Institutions (AIFIs) 🔴

### Core Idea
AIFIs are an institutional mechanism providing **sector-specific long-term financing** to areas like agriculture, housing, infrastructure and small-scale industries. They are regulated by RBI and were brought under the Basel III Capital framework in 2023-24. There are **5 AIFIs**: EXIM Bank, NABARD, NaBFID, NHB, and SIDBI.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of AIFIs as five specialist big-ticket financiers — one each for exports, agriculture, infrastructure, housing, and small industry — that provide long-tenor money to sectors where ordinary banks find lending difficult.

### Key Concepts

#### The Five AIFIs Regulated by RBI
| AIFI | Sector Focus |
|------|--------------|
| EXIM Bank (Export-Import Bank of India) | External trade finance |
| NABARD (National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development) | Agriculture & rural |
| NaBFID (National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development) | Infrastructure |
| NHB (National Housing Bank) | Housing finance |
| SIDBI (Small Industries Development Bank of India) | Small industries / MSME |

#### Basel III Capital Framework Extension (2023-24)
RBI extended Basel III norms to AIFIs in 2023-24. AIFIs' role has evolved as the economy grows; they are now seen as key channels of direct/indirect credit. Capital adequacy must be assessed at the **group level**, fully consolidating all financial subsidiaries (except those in insurance and non-financial activities).

| Capital Component | Minimum Required | Effective Date |
|-------------------|------------------|----------------|
| Total Capital | 9% | April 2024 (NHB: July 2024 — its FY is July–June) |
| Tier-I Capital | 7% | April 2024 |
| CET-1 Capital | 5.5% | April 2024 |

**Investment limits under Basel III rules for AIFIs:**
- AIFI investment in capital instruments of banks/financial/insurance entities: capped at **10% of capital funds**.
- Fresh acquisition of bank/AIFI equity: not allowed if it pushes holding **> 5%** of investee equity.
- AIFI equity investment in a single entity: ≤ **49%** of investee equity. If acquired as **pledgee** against AIFI claims, the stake must be brought below 10% within **three years**.

### Definitions
- **AIFI (All India Financial Institution)**: RBI-regulated specialised entity providing sector-specific long-term finance. ⭐
- **Tier-I Capital**: Core capital — equity + disclosed reserves; minimum 7% under Basel III for AIFIs.
- **CET-1 (Common Equity Tier-1)**: Highest-quality capital — paid-up equity + retained earnings; minimum 5.5%.
- **NaBFID**: National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development — newest AIFI.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. RBI extends Basel III to AIFIs (2023-24).
2. AIFIs consolidate all financial subsidiaries (except insurance/non-financial) for capital-adequacy computation.
3. AIFIs maintain 9% total / 7% Tier-I / 5.5% CET-1 from April 2024 (NHB July 2024).
4. Caps on capital instrument investment (10%), fresh equity acquisition (5%), and single-entity equity (49%, with pledgee unwind in 3 years) take effect.

### Examples
**Example: NHB exception**
NHB follows a July–June accounting year, so the Basel III implementation date for NHB is **July 2024** rather than April 2024 — quote this if asked about implementation dates.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: There are 4 AIFIs → ✅ Correct: There are **5** — EXIM, NABARD, NaBFID, NHB, SIDBI.
- ❌ Mistake: AIFIs follow Basel II → ✅ Correct: RBI extended **Basel III** norms to AIFIs in 2023-24.
- ❌ Mistake: All AIFIs implement Basel III from April 2024 → ✅ Correct: NHB implements from July 2024.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The 49% equity limit is breached only when AIFI holds it as **pledgee** against claims, and even then it must be brought < 10% within 3 years.
- Insurance and non-financial subsidiaries are excluded from group-level capital consolidation.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 AIFIs: **EXIM, NABARD, NaBFID, NHB, SIDBI**.
> - Basel III ratios: **9 / 7 / 5.5** (Total / Tier-I / CET-1).
> - Caps: **10%** capital-instrument investment, **5%** fresh equity, **49%** single-entity equity.

## Section: Primary Dealers (PDs) 🟡

### Core Idea
The Primary Dealer system was introduced by RBI in **1995** to strengthen the Government Securities (G-sec) market and create a more efficient secondary-market trading system. PDs underwrite primary issues of government securities; they are the only entities (alongside RBI, which has shed this role) permitted to do so. In 2021, India had **21 PDs — 14 bank PDs and 7 standalone PDs**.

> **In Simple Terms:** PDs are the wholesale handlers and cheerleaders of government bonds — they bid in auctions, make markets, and ensure G-secs find buyers.

### Key Concepts

#### Origin & Structure of PD System
The PD system was introduced in 1995. In **2006-07**, RBI gave banks the **option to undertake PD business departmentally**, leading to two PD types — bank PDs and standalone PDs. The Government of India can refer to PDs as Merchant Bankers, since only PDs are permitted to underwrite primary issues of government securities. RBI expects PDs to actively participate in primary auctions, make markets in G-secs, predominantly invest in G-secs, achieve a minimum secondary-market turnover ratio, and maintain efficient internal controls. A PD must have a **standing arrangement with RBI**, with an undertaking and authorisation letter renewed every **three years**.

#### Standalone PD Core Activities (10 items)
Standalone PDs are permitted the following 10 core activities:
1. Dealing and underwriting in Government securities.
2. Dealing in Interest Rate Derivatives (IRD).
3. Providing broking services in Government securities.
4. Dealing and underwriting in Corporate / PSU / FI bonds and debentures.
5. Lending in Call / Notice / Term / Repo / CBLO market.
6. Investment in Commercial Papers (CPs).
7. Investment in Certificates of Deposit (CDs).
8. Investment in Security Receipts issued by Securitization / Reconstruction companies.
9. Investment in Asset-Backed Securities (ABS) and Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS).
10. Investment in debt mutual funds whose corpus is fully in debt securities.

### Definitions
- **Primary Dealer (PD)**: Entity entitled to underwrite primary issues of government securities and trade actively in G-secs.
- **Merchant Banker**: Government of India term for PDs (permitted to underwrite primary issues of G-secs).
- **G-sec (Government Security)**: Sovereign debt instrument; primary instrument PDs deal in.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. RBI introduces PD system (1995) → strengthens G-sec market.
2. PD signs standing arrangement / undertaking with RBI — renewed every **3 years**.
3. PD participates in primary auctions, market-makes in G-secs, invests predominantly in G-secs.
4. PD meets minimum secondary-market turnover ratio.
5. (2006-07) Banks given option to do PD business departmentally → emergence of bank PDs.

### Examples
**Example: 2021 PD landscape**
Of 21 PDs, 14 are bank PDs (banks running PD desks departmentally) and 7 are standalone PDs (NBFC-style entities) — the latter have access to 10 specified core activities.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: PD system started in 2006 → ✅ Correct: 1995. The 2006-07 change merely allowed banks to run PD business departmentally.
- ❌ Mistake: All 21 PDs are standalone → ✅ Correct: 14 are bank PDs and only 7 are standalone.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Standalone PD activity list excludes equity dealing; their orbit is debt and money-market instruments.
- The PD undertaking with RBI has a **3-year** renewal cycle.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - PDs introduced in **1995**; bank-departmental option from **2006-07**.
> - 2021: **21 PDs = 14 bank + 7 standalone**.
> - 10 standalone PD core activities — chiefly G-secs, IRD, bonds, money-market instruments, debt MFs.

- Connects to: G-sec market mechanics covered earlier in the block.

## Section: Issues and Concerns in NBFI Sector 🔴

### Core Idea
NBFIs contribute to the economy but bring significant inherent risks — excess leverage, pro-cyclicality, over-reliance on wholesale funding, and concentration risk. Critically, NBFIs lack two safety nets that banks enjoy: **lender of last resort** access and **deposit insurance**. The Thorat Committee (Aug 2011) recommended applying all bank-level prudential norms to NBFCs to plug regulatory arbitrage.

> **In Simple Terms:** NBFIs run a higher-octane business than banks — more leverage, riskier sectors, less stable funding — but without the central-bank parachute or deposit-insurance airbag. Regulation must therefore be tight enough to prevent crashes without choking innovation.

### Key Concepts

#### Inherent Risks of NBFIs
NBFIs carry several inherent risks: (i) **excess leverage**, (ii) **amplification of pro-cyclicality**, (iii) **over-reliance on wholesale funding**, and (iv) **concentration risk** from niche-sector exposure. Their business model is risk-prone — weaker underwriting, higher risk-taking and complexity. They are also exposed to regulatory gaps, regulatory arbitrage and contagion effects.

#### Absence of Safety Nets
Unlike banks, NBFIs are typically not allowed:
- The benefit of a **central bank as lender of last resort (LOLR)**.
- Coverage from **deposit insurance institutions**.
The only way to control these risks is through proper regulation. Striking the right balance between risks and benefits is a perpetual challenge for regulators in both developing and developed economies — too little regulation breeds crises; too much chokes growth.

#### Risk Cascade — How NBFI Stress Becomes Systemic
NBFIs are more prone to systemic risks because:
- They concentrate exposure to specific sectors.
- They depend on commercial banks for funding (direct & indirect) → **interconnectedness risk** is higher.
- Asset-liability mismatches **accentuate liquidity risk**.
- Liquidity risk → solvency risk → systemic risk in rapid sequence.
Continuous monitoring of factors like maturity-liquidity transformation and leverage is therefore essential.

#### Thorat Committee (August 2011)
The working group on Issues and Concerns in the NBFC Sector (Thorat Committee, Aug 2011) suggested:
- Reducing **clutter** and possibilities of **regulatory arbitrage**.
- Recognising regulatory priorities and the fundamental differences with banks.
- Plugging **arbitrage points** between bank and NBFC regulation.
- Making **all prudential norms applicable to banks** also applicable to NBFCs.

### Definitions
- **Pro-cyclicality**: Tendency to amplify cyclical swings — lend more in booms, cut sharply in busts. ⭐
- **Wholesale Funding**: Short-term institutional funding (CPs, CDs, bank borrowings) — riskier than retail deposits.
- **Lender of Last Resort (LOLR)**: Central-bank function of providing emergency liquidity; usually unavailable to NBFIs.
- **Asset-Liability Mismatch (ALM)**: Funding short-tenor liabilities with long-tenor assets; primary cause of NBFI liquidity stress. ⭐
- **Contagion Risk**: Risk that distress at one institution spreads through the financial system.
- **Thorat Committee (Aug 2011)**: RBI working group on Issues and Concerns in NBFC Sector. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. NBFI builds up leverage and concentrates exposure in a niche sector.
2. Wholesale funding dries up → liquidity risk emerges.
3. Asset-liability mismatch accentuates liquidity squeeze.
4. Liquidity risk → solvency risk.
5. Solvency stress + interconnectedness with banks → **systemic risk**.

### Examples
**Example: Channel of contagion**
An NBFI heavily funded by short-term bank lines (wholesale funding) suddenly faces market stress; rolling becomes costly; long-tenor housing/infra loans cannot be liquidated quickly; defaults cascade into lending banks — illustrating how interconnectedness turns NBFI distress systemic.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: NBFIs enjoy deposit insurance like banks → ✅ Correct: NBFIs are typically excluded from deposit insurance.
- ❌ Mistake: RBI is the LOLR for NBFIs → ✅ Correct: NBFIs generally do not have LOLR access.
- ❌ Mistake: Thorat Committee was set up in 2001 → ✅ Correct: **August 2011**.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The right regulatory balance is described as a "perpetual challenge" — even advanced economies struggle with it.
- Monitoring needs to focus on maturity-liquidity transformation and leverage, not just static capital ratios.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 inherent NBFI risks: leverage, pro-cyclicality, wholesale funding, concentration.
> - Missing safety nets: **LOLR + deposit insurance**.
> - Thorat Committee (**Aug 2011**) → apply bank prudential norms to NBFCs.
> - Risk cascade: ALM → liquidity → solvency → systemic.

## Section: Summary, Key Words & References 🟢

### Core Idea
NBFIs (a.k.a. shadow banks) plug gaps in financial services that banks cannot serve well — they foster a risk-management culture, broaden the spectrum of risks for investors, encourage saving and investment, and improve resilience to shocks. They are heterogeneous: NBFCs + HFCs + AIFIs + PDs. Since 2023, RBI regulates NBFIs under the four-layer **Scale-Based Regulation (SBR)** framework.

> **In Simple Terms:** This wrap-up reminds us NBFIs complement banks by offering simpler procedures, customer-oriented services and attractive deposit rates, all under RBI's tiered SBR oversight.

### Key Concepts

#### Recap of NBFI Roles
NBFIs perform financial intermediation through deposits, loans/advances, leasing and hire-purchase. They diversify products, foster a risk-management culture, encourage savings, and bolster financial system resilience. Their complementarity with banks comes from customer orientation, simplified procedures, attractive deposit rates and timely sector-specific credit.

### Definitions (All Key Words from the Unit)
- **Economies of Scale**: Large asset portfolios let financial intermediaries reap (a) risk reduction via diversification, (b) employment of efficient/professional managers, (c) low administrative cost on large loans, and (d) low costs of establishment, information and transactions. ⭐
- **Financial Intermediation**: A financial intermediary is an institution that connects surplus and deficit agents — a middleman between two parties in a financial transaction. While commercial banks are typical examples, the category also includes investment banks, insurance companies, broker-dealers, mutual funds and pension funds. Benefits include safety, liquidity and economies of scale. ⭐
- **Hire Purchase**: A legal contract in which persons agree to pay for goods in parts or by a percentage at a time. ⭐
- **Insurance Company**: A company that offers insurance policies to the public (directly or through employee-benefit plans), comprised of multiple agents. May specialise in one type (life/health/auto) or offer multiple types. ⭐
- **Investment Companies**: Privately and publicly owned business entities that manage, sell, and market funds to the public — offering portfolio management, record-keeping, custodial, legal, accounting and tax services. ⭐
- **Leasing**: A process by which a firm obtains the use of fixed assets in return for a series of contractual, periodic, tax-deductible payments. The lessee receives the asset; the lessor owns it. The relationship between tenant and landlord (tenancy) may be fixed or indefinite. The consideration is called rent. ⭐
- **Microfinance**: Provision of financial services to micro-entrepreneurs and small businesses lacking banking access (high transaction costs). Two delivery mechanisms: (1) **relationship-based banking** for individuals/small businesses, and (2) **group-based models** where entrepreneurs apply jointly. ⭐
- **Mutual Fund**: A mediator that pools money from a group of people and invests in stocks, bonds and other securities. Each investor owns shares representing a portion of the fund's holdings — offers diversified, professionally managed exposure at low cost. ⭐
- **Shadow Banking**: Entities performing banking activities (fully or partially) outside the regular banking system; in India, primarily NBFCs. ⭐
- **Systemic Risk**: Risk of collapse of an entire financial system or market — financial-system instability, potentially catastrophic, caused or worsened by idiosyncratic events at financial intermediaries. Refers to interlinkages whereby failure of one entity (or cluster) cascades to bring down the whole system. ⭐

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. NBFIs mobilise savings from surplus agents.
2. Channelise funds into investment via diverse products.
3. Improve risk distribution across investors with varying risk appetites.
4. Complement bank credit, especially in niche/under-served sectors.

### Examples
**Example: NBFI complementarity**
A small entrepreneur unable to access bank credit due to documentation gaps takes a hire-purchase loan from an NBFC for equipment, which a bank would not have offered with the same speed — illustrating how NBFIs fill bank-system gaps.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: NBFIs and banks do identical functions → ✅ Correct: NBFIs are heterogeneous; banks are a homogeneous group; NBFI lending typically takes longer-term forms.
- ❌ Mistake: All NBFIs are NBFCs → ✅ Correct: NBFIs include NBFCs + HFCs + AIFIs + PDs.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The Top Layer of SBR is "ideally expected to be empty."
- Shadow banking is a global concept; in India it is operationalised primarily through NBFCs.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - NBFIs = NBFCs + HFCs + AIFIs + PDs.
> - SBR layers: BL → ML → UL → TL (TL ideally empty).
> - All 10 Key Words are exam-critical short-answer fodder.
