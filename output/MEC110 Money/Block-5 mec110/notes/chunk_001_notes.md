## Section: Course Overview — MEC-110 Money, Financial Institutions and Markets 🟢

### Core Idea
MEC-110 is a third-semester MA Economics course that extends the macroeconomic foundation laid by MEC-101 and MEC-102 into the territory of monetary economics. It studies how money, financial institutions and financial markets together allocate resources, facilitate transactions, and transmit monetary policy. The course treats the financial system as the cornerstone of a modern economy and asks how capital flows between surplus and deficit units.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of the economy as a body — earlier courses studied muscles (real sectors) and the nervous system (macro policy). MEC-110 studies the bloodstream: money and the institutions that move it from those who have spare cash to those who need it.

### Key Concepts

#### Position of MEC-110 in the MA Economics programme
The MA Economics programme runs across four semesters. The first two semesters build theoretical foundations through Micro Economic Analysis (MEC-101), Macro Economic Analysis (MEC-102), quantitative tools (MEC-203), and growth/development. The third semester then introduces three area-specific core courses — Public Economics (MEC-106), International Trade and Development (MEC-107) and MEC-110. MEC-110 is positioned explicitly as an extension of the macro dimension because monetary topics could not be fully covered inside MEC-102.

#### Why a separate course on money, FIs and markets?
The course is justified because money, financial institutions and markets form "the cornerstone of modern economies." They are the channels through which resources are allocated, transactions facilitated, and economic prosperity achieved. Understanding their interactions is essential for policymakers, economists, investors and citizens. The course also covers asset pricing (Efficient Portfolio Frontier, CAPM, APT), derivatives, corporate finance, FDI/FPI, NPAs, foreign exchange stability and behavioural finance — the financial-stability complement to the institutional study in Block 5.

### Definitions
- **MEC-110**: Money, Financial Institutions and Markets — third-semester core course of the IGNOU MA Economics programme, presented in Volume-2 across Blocks 5–7.
- **Financial system (course framing)**: The set of money, financial institutions and markets that channel capital, allocate resources, facilitate transactions, and underpin economic stability.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Theoretical core (MEC-101, MEC-102) → Quantitative/research tools (MEC-203, MEC-109) → Applied / area-specific courses (MEC-106, MEC-107, MEC-110) → Empirical Indian policy (MEC-205).
2. Capital flow logic of the course: Savers (surplus units) → Financial intermediaries / markets → Borrowers (deficit units) → Investment, growth, stability.

## Section: Block 5 Overview — Financial Institutions 🟢

### Core Idea
Block 5 argues that financial institutions are the backbone of the financial system: they channel funds between savers and investors and shape the landscape of finance. The block has four units that together cover banks, non-bank intermediaries, the securities regulator (SEBI) and other institutions/regulations.

> **In Simple Terms:** Block 5 is a guided tour of the "buildings" of the financial system — banks, NBFIs, the stock-market referee SEBI, and miscellaneous specialised institutions — before later blocks zoom into the activity that happens inside them.

### Key Concepts

#### Four units of Block 5
| Unit | Title | Focus |
|------|-------|-------|
| 14 | Commercial Banking | Definition, functions, structure, credit creation, asset distribution, recent trends |
| 15 | Non-Bank Financial Intermediaries | NBFCs and their role beyond banks |
| 16 | Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) | Capital-markets regulator |
| 17 | Other Financial Institutions and Regulations | Remaining specialised institutions and the regulatory environment |

All four units in Block 5 are written by Prof. S.K. Singh (Retd. Prof. of Economics, IGNOU).

#### Role of FIs in channelling funds
The block's framing is that financial institutions intermediate between savers and investors — gathering small, dispersed savings and routing them into productive investment. Without them, capital allocation would be inefficient and inclusive growth impossible.

### Definitions
- **Block 5 — Financial Institutions**: The first block of Volume-2 of MEC-110, covering commercial banks, NBFIs, SEBI, and other financial institutions and their regulations.
- **Financial institution (block framing)**: An organisation that channels funds between savers and investors and forms part of the backbone of the financial system.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Savers deposit funds → Financial institutions (banks, NBFIs) → Lend / invest → Borrowers and firms.
2. Regulators (e.g., SEBI, RBI) → Set rules and supervise → Maintain trust → Enable the channelling function above.

### Examples
**Example: The four-unit logic of Block 5**
- Unit 14 introduces the dominant intermediary (commercial banks).
- Unit 15 covers everything else that intermediates without being a bank.
- Unit 16 introduces the rule-maker for securities markets.
- Unit 17 fills in the remaining institutions and regulatory gaps.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Block 5 presents institutions and regulators; it does not yet model market behaviour or pricing — that is the job of Block 6.

## Section: Preview of Blocks 6 and 7 🟢

### Core Idea
After the institutional groundwork of Block 5, Block 6 turns to the operational mechanics of financial markets (risk-return, asset-pricing models, derivatives, corporate finance, FDI/FPI), and Block 7 closes the volume with financial-stability themes (business cycles, market efficiency, NPAs, FX stability, behavioural finance). Together the three blocks deliver a complete picture: institutions, then markets, then stability.

> **In Simple Terms:** Block 5 builds the stadium, Block 6 explains the game being played inside, and Block 7 asks what happens when the crowd panics.

### Key Concepts

#### Block 6 — Operational Aspects of Financial Markets
Six units (18–23) covering Efficient Portfolio Frontier, CAPM, APT, derivative pricing, corporate finance, and FDI/FPI. The orientation is risk-return analysis and the dynamics of foreign investment.

#### Block 7 — Financial Stability
Six units (24–29) on macro-finance and business cycles, the Efficient Markets Hypothesis, financial stability issues, non-performing assets, foreign exchange stability, and behavioural finance.

### Definitions
- **Block 6 — Operational Aspects of Financial Markets**: Block focused on risk-return analysis, asset-pricing models (CAPM, APT), derivatives pricing, corporate finance, and foreign investment.
- **Block 7 — Financial Stability**: Block examining the conditions for and threats to a stable financial system, including NPAs, FX stability, and behavioural finance.

### Examples
**Example: Mapping a real-world topic to a block**
- "Why did a stock crash?" → Block 6 (asset pricing) plus Block 7 (EMH, behavioural finance).
- "Why did a bank fail?" → Block 5 (commercial banking) plus Block 7 (NPAs, financial stability).

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The blocks are interconnected; topics like monetary policy or NPAs surface across multiple blocks at different depths.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Block 6: Units 18–23, six units, markets and pricing.
> - Block 7: Units 24–29, six units, stability and crises.
> - Volume-2 = Blocks 5 + 6 + 7 = 16 units.
