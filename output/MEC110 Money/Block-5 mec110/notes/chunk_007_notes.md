## Section: Working of SEBI (16.5) 🔴

### Core Idea
SEBI's "working" is best understood as 11 concrete intervention areas across the primary market, secondary trading, post-trade settlement, governance and enforcement (Sabarinathan, 2010). Across these areas SEBI moved India from a manual, opaque, badla-driven exchange culture to a dematerialised, electronic, T+2 settlement, disclosure-rich market with a recognisable corporate governance code (Clause 49). The March 29, 2023 board meeting added an ASBA-like secondary-market mechanism, ESG norms, private equity as MF sponsors and a backstop facility for stressed debt MFs.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of SEBI as the contractor who renovated India's stock-market house room-by-room — the entrance (primary market), the bookkeeping (disclosure), the rules of behaviour (Clause 49), the trading floor (electronic + T+2), the storage room (demat), the security cameras (insider trading surveillance) and the ownership deed of the house itself (corporatisation of exchanges).

### Key Concepts

#### 1. Primary market, market access and intermediaries
SEBI regulates the primary market on three fronts: (i) issuers' access to the market, (ii) information production at the time of issue, and (iii) processes/procedures for issuance. All three have evolved considerably.

#### 2. Disclosure requirements
With corporate governance norms in 2000-01, listed firms must disclose materially significant **related-party transactions** with promoters, directors, management, subsidiaries, relatives etc. Quarterly reporting frequency was raised; finer-grained performance disclosure mandated to reduce information asymmetry.

#### 3. Corporate Governance (Clause 49) ⭐
- Initiative chain: **Kumar Mangalam Birla Committee** → two reports of the **N R Narayana Murthy Committee** → introduction of **Clause 49** in the listing agreement.
- Clause 49 is often compared to (and draws upon) the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act, 2002** of the USA in objectives and approach.

#### 4. Market for corporate control (Takeover Code) ⭐
Takeovers and acquisitions are SEBI-regulated. The **Takeover Code** has enabled an active market for corporate control to evolve in India. It is an initiative entirely attributable to SEBI.

#### 5. Trading Mechanisms — Electronic trading
SEBI pushed Indian stock exchanges toward electronic trading. Automation: (i) reduced price manipulation and concealment of audit trails; (ii) gave investors time-priority and correct prices; (iii) altered exchange economics — NSE and BSE were extended electronically to other cities from **1996-97**.

#### 6. Settlement systems — T+2 ⭐
Along with dematerialisation, SEBI moved exchanges gradually to **T+2 rolling settlement from April 2003**. Risk-management products in disguised form such as **badla** (a futures-like trading) were discontinued. Net effect: lower transaction costs.

#### 7. Dematerialisation ⭐
- Phased timeline: **Started January 1998**; made compulsory for **all IPOs in September 2001**.
- Benefits: greater liquidity; no risk of mutilation/loss of scrips; shorter book-closure for dividends/rights/bonus; eliminated transfer delays.

#### 8. Institutionalisation of Trading and Ownership
A hallmark of developed markets is institutional ownership/trading share. **FII inflows** correlate strongly with market returns (causal direction unclear). Institutional investors pressure Indian corporates to raise governance standards.

#### 9. Market Integrity and Insider Trading
- Arthur Levitt (former US SEC Chairman): insider trading "has utterly no place in any fair-minded law-abiding economy".
- SEBI requires listed firms, intermediaries and advisors to install internal anti-insider-trading systems and report compliance.
- **SEBI (Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices) Regulation, 2003** addresses manipulative practices; backed by heavy market surveillance.
- **October 2022**: trading in MF units brought into insider-trading regulation — long overdue.
- SEBI also investigates **front-running**.

#### 10. Ownership/Governance of stock exchanges — C&D ⭐
SEBI's biggest governance reform was **Corporatization & De-mutualisation (C&D)** — separation of ownership from trading rights. Requirement: **not less than 51% public ownership** of stock exchanges (i.e. by people other than trading-rights shareholders).

#### 11. Compliance Enforcement (the weak link)
SEBI has been less effective in **prosecution and penalising** non-compliers. Experts blame the **absence of specialised financial-market courts**. Related-party-transaction leakages remain a focus; SEBI keeps tweaking rules as firms find new escape hatches. "Regulations cannot plug all holes."

#### Index Providers (Saravanan & Banerjee, 2023)
Index providers (e.g., NSE Indices for Nifty50; S&P Dow Jones + BSE for Sensex) must: consider all relevant data; avoid data distortion; do due diligence on data submitters; ensure data is sourced **only from regulated entities**. Important because passive funds and their AUM are growing exponentially.

#### SEBI March 29, 2023 Board Meeting Decisions
1. **ASBA-like mechanism for secondary trades**.
2. Bonus issue of shares reform.
3. Removal of permanent positions in board seats — gives shareholders a larger say; empowers Nomination & Remuneration committees.
4. Allowing private equity firms to be MF sponsors.
5. **Backstop facility** for MFs in stress times.
6. Mechanism for preventing/detecting stock-market frauds.
7. New ESG norms.

### Definitions
- **Clause 49**: ⭐ Provision of the listing agreement laying down India's corporate-governance code; introduced after the Birla and Narayana Murthy Committee reports; comparable to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, 2002.
- **Sarbanes-Oxley Act, 2002**: ⭐ US federal law on corporate governance and audit accountability, often used as the global benchmark; Clause 49 draws upon it.
- **Takeover Code**: ⭐ SEBI regulation creating a market for corporate control by governing takeovers/acquisitions of listed firms.
- **Dematerialisation**: ⭐ Conversion of physical share certificates into electronic form held in a depository; began Jan 1998, compulsory for all IPOs from Sept 2001.
- **T+2 rolling settlement**: ⭐ Trade-day-plus-two-business-days cycle for settling securities trades; introduced from April 2003.
- **Corporatization & De-mutualisation (C&D)**: ⭐ Separation of ownership and trading rights of stock exchanges, with at least 51% public (non-trading-member) ownership.
- **Badla**: A pre-reform Indian carry-forward, futures-like trading mechanism; discontinued during the dematerialisation/T+2 transition.
- **NACAS**: National Advisory Committee on Accounting Standards — strengthens accounting rule-writing/administration.
- **Front-running**: Using insider knowledge of pending institutional orders to take a personal position before the institutional order is placed.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**SEBI's market-reform pipeline (chronology to remember):**
1. 1996-97 → NSE/BSE electronic trading extended to other cities.
2. January 1998 → dematerialisation begins.
3. September 2001 → demat compulsory for all IPOs.
4. 2000-01 → corporate governance disclosure requirements.
5. 2003 (April) → T+2 rolling settlement; badla discontinued.
6. 2003 → SEBI (Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices) Regulation.
7. 2010 onwards → C&D of stock exchanges (≥51% public ownership).
8. October 2022 → MF units brought under insider-trading regulation.
9. March 29, 2023 → Board meeting reforms (ASBA-secondary, ESG, PE sponsors, backstop).

### Examples
**Example: Clause 49 vs Sarbanes-Oxley** — Both target board independence, audit-committee strength and disclosure quality; Clause 49 was India's response to enhance investor confidence after the early-2000s governance crises.

**Example: Front-running detection** — SEBI investigates patterns where a dealer trades in a stock just before the AMC he/she works for executes a large order — a misuse of unpublished price-sensitive information.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "T+2 began in 1996-97." → ✅ Electronic trading extended in 1996-97; **T+2 settlement from April 2003**.
- ❌ "Demat compulsory from 1998." → ✅ Demat began in **January 1998**; became compulsory for **all IPOs in September 2001**.
- ❌ Confusing Clause 49 with the Companies Act. → ✅ Clause 49 is a **listing agreement** clause administered by SEBI.
- ❌ "C&D requires 100% public ownership." → ✅ **At least 51%** by non-trading-rights public.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Despite all reforms, SEBI's **enforcement/prosecution** record remains weak — primarily a judicial-infrastructure problem (no specialised courts).
- FII inflow–market return correlation is strong but **causal direction is unsure** — the textbook is explicit on this.
- Index-provider regulation is recent and motivated by the rapid growth of passive funds.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 11 working areas: Primary market | Disclosure | Corporate Governance | Takeover Code | Trading Mechanisms | Settlement | Dematerialisation | Institutionalisation | Insider Trading | C&D | Compliance Enforcement.
> - Three Clause-49 dates: 2000-01 disclosures; Birla → Narayana Murthy → Clause 49.
> - Demat: Jan 1998 (start) → Sept 2001 (all IPOs).
> - Settlement: T+2 from April 2003; badla scrapped.
> - C&D: ≥51% public ownership of exchanges.
> - March 29, 2023: ASBA-secondary, ESG norms, PE as MF sponsors, MF backstop.

### Connections

- Feeds into: Challenges (16.6) and Evaluation (16.7) — the question is whether these reforms suffice in the digital era.

### Open Questions
1. Why has India failed to set up specialised financial-markets courts despite repeated identification of this as the key enforcement weakness?
2. After the C&D reform, has the manipulation level on stock exchanges actually fallen, or has the locus of manipulation simply shifted?

## Section: Challenges Before SEBI (16.6) 🔴

### Core Idea
SEBI's three frontier challenges are: (i) finfluencers operating across Telegram, Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook, YouTube — large in size, low in qualification; (ii) the fintech revolution that turns risk into uncertainty and outpaces static regulation; and (iii) FPI/UBO opacity used for round-tripping. SEBI's response is to put fraud-detection onus on stock brokers via a 9-item monitoring list and to force DDPs to disclose ultimate beneficial owners.

> **In Simple Terms:** SEBI is fighting on three battlefields at once — social-media advice gurus with no licence, fintech innovators that move faster than rule-books, and shell-company onion-layers used to hide who really owns Indian-market money.

### Key Concepts

#### Finfluencers (Basu, 2022)
Massive presence across Telegram, Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook, YouTube; no formal qualification requirement; many disseminate self-serving advice. Honest opinion-givers should remain free; the question is **whether the influencer is doing something dishonest**. The main problem is **misleading advertising** to under-informed retail investors. Any future law would need to first define "finfluencer" precisely so that genuine market commentators are not chilled.

#### Fintech Revolution and Regulatory Lag (Nair & Sahoo, 2022)
- Regulations are **static or slow-moving**; technological change is "lightning speed".
- Fintech innovations occur at **regulatory boundaries** and are sometimes "decentralised" — putting hi-tech products beyond conventional reach.
- Conventional wisdom: **risk** can be regulated; **uncertainty** cannot — fintech pushes risk into uncertainty.
- Solution suggested: convert language-based regulations into **algorithms** ("regulatory algos") once data and text are sufficiently precise.

#### FPI / UBO / Round-tripping problem (Shah, 2023)
- Many FPIs are mere **registered vehicles** while UBOs are hidden.
- World-wide AML norms force regulators to "lift the corporate veil".
- SEBI has directed **Designated Depository Participants (DDPs)** to update lists of UBOs; threatened **FPI licence cancellation** and wind-up of positions by **March 2024** for non-compliance.
- Round-tripping: Indian money routed through **shell companies in offshore tax havens** back into Indian markets via multi-layered FPIs.

#### PMLA Rule 9, 2005 — Beneficial Owner thresholds ⭐
- **Companies** owning >**25%** of an FPI = BO.
- **Individuals, partnership firms, trusts, unincorporated associations** owning **15%** of an FPI = BO.
- If no BO can be identified through ownership/control, the **investment manager** is treated as BO and disclosed to the depository.
- Hindu Business Line (Feb 2023) recommendations: SEBI should apply a **"look-through" principle**, ban abetting intermediaries, and **reduce BO disclosure window to 3 months**.

#### Fraud-monitoring list for stock brokers (9 items)
SEBI wants brokers' systems to detect:
1. Creation of misleading appearance of trading.
2. Price manipulation.
3. Front-running.
4. Insider trading.
5. Mis-selling.
6. Unauthorised trading (incl. **'mule' accounts**).
7. Pump-and-dump, spoofing.
8. Disproportionate trading vs reported income/net worth.
9. Frequent KYC changes by clients.

### Definitions
- **Finfluencer**: Social-media personality dispensing financial/investment advice without a regulatory qualification.
- **Fintech**: ⭐ New technology that seeks to improve and automate the delivery and use of financial services.
- **Ultimate Beneficial Owner (UBO)**: ⭐ The natural person who ultimately owns or controls an investing entity, beyond layered corporate vehicles.
- **Designated Depository Participant (DDP)**: SEBI-authorised entity tasked with FPI registration and KYC, including UBO updating.
- **Beneficial Owner (BO)** under **Rule 9, PMLA Rules 2005**: ⭐ >25% ownership (companies) or 15% (individuals/firms/trusts) of an FPI; or the investment manager if neither can be identified.
- **Round-tripping**: Routing domestic money out through offshore shell companies and back as "foreign" investment.
- **Mule account**: A trading account that fronts for another, unauthorised, party.
- **Pump-and-dump / spoofing**: Manipulation tactics that inflate or fake demand to mislead other traders.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**SEBI's UBO enforcement chain:**
1. SEBI directs DDPs → 2. DDPs collect/update UBO list from FPIs → 3. PMLA Rule 9 thresholds applied → 4. Non-compliers' FPI licences cancelled and positions wound up by **March 2024**.

**Broker fraud detection:**
1. SEBI prescribes 9-item watch-list → 2. Brokers' surveillance systems flag the items → 3. Reporting/escalation to SEBI → 4. Enforcement action.

### Examples
**Example: Mule account** — Person A opens trading account in his name but it is fully operated by Person B who is unauthorised; broker's system should flag mismatched login patterns, abnormal trades vs declared income.

**Example: Round-tripping via FPI layers** — Indian promoter parks money in a Cayman shell → Cayman shell invests in a Mauritius FPI → FPI buys back Indian listed shares; Rule 9 thresholds and the look-through principle aim to reveal the Indian promoter as BO.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "PMLA threshold is 25% for everyone." → ✅ **25% for companies, 15% for individuals/partnerships/trusts.**
- ❌ "All finfluencers are illegal." → ✅ Only **dishonest/misleading** advice is the target; honest opinions are protected.
- ❌ Treating fintech disruption as just a tech matter. → ✅ It also creates **uncertainty** that defies traditional regulation.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- A fintech product can sit in a regulatory **grey zone** if it does not fit any existing definition — exactly the gap SEBI seeks to plug.
- "Look-through" is a principle SEBI **needs to apply** but is not a statutory rule yet — Hindu Business Line editorial recommends it.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 challenges: Finfluencers | Fintech | FPI-UBO/round-tripping.
> - PMLA Rule 9: **25% companies / 15% individuals/firms/trusts**.
> - 9 fraud-list items: misleading appearance, price manipulation, front-running, insider trading, mis-selling, unauthorised trading (mules), pump-and-dump/spoofing, disproportionate trading, frequent KYC changes.
> - UBO deadline for DDPs: **March 2024**.

### Connections
- Builds on: Working of SEBI (16.5) — these are the modern frontiers where 16.5's tools need upgrading.
- Forward link: Suggestions (16.8) — IT Act 2000 powers, Kotak Committee, lateral hiring all respond to these challenges.
- Cross-reference: PMLA 2002/2005 and AML framework (general financial-system regulation).

### Open Questions
1. Should "finfluencer" be a regulated category (like investment advisers) or banned outright on advertising?
2. Can decentralised finance (DeFi) ever be regulated by a national securities regulator?

## Section: Evaluation of SEBI's Performance (16.7) 🟡

### Core Idea
On balance SEBI has fared well: an autonomous, well-resourced regulator that built a safe, cost-competitive market with good disclosure and a Sarbanes-Oxley-comparable governance code, but its **enforcement powers** rank below the US SEC's. Its IOSCO-principles scorecard is reasonable, which helped Indian markets weather the 2008 GFC and the COVID meltdown.

> **In Simple Terms:** SEBI scores high on rule-making and structural design, mid on enforcement; an A-grade architect but only a B-grade police officer.

### Key Concepts

#### Strengths of SEBI
- Autonomous and suitably empowered, with the requisite knowledge and human resources.
- Designed an **operationally safe market**; among the most cost-competitive (taxes apart).
- Few payment crises since the early-mid-1990s.
- Trade execution and counterparty risks largely eliminated.
- One of the world's better **public-offering mechanisms** for price discovery and volume-handling.
- Disclosure system at listing/post-listing comparable to global best.
- Accounting strengthened via **NACAS**.
- Corporate-governance code comparable to **Sarbanes-Oxley Act**.
- Good score on the **disclosure-requirements index**.
- Strong **board structure** — policy by Board members, implementation by EDs.
- "Completely detached concerning the rentiers" — institutional independence.
- Internal-competence track record compares favourably with RBI.

#### Weaknesses (vs SEC)
- **Enforcement powers** rank "way below" the US SEC.
- Prosecution success limited; specialised courts absent.

#### IOSCO 5 Core Principles ⭐
| # | Principle |
|---|-----------|
| i | Clear and objectively stated responsibilities |
| ii | Operational independence and accountability |
| iii | Adequate power and resources |
| iv | Clear and consistent regulatory processes |
| v | Skilled and reliable staff |

IOSCO reviewed adherence by regulators of **55 countries**; SEBI scores reasonably well on all five. IOSCO also lauded SEBI for being **well-funded by fees**. SEBI officials enjoy **legal protection** for actions in good faith.

### Definitions
- **IOSCO**: ⭐ International Organisation of Securities Commissions — the global standard-setter for securities regulators; reviewed 55 country regulators against five core principles.
- **NACAS**: National Advisory Committee on Accounting Standards; institutional support for accounting rule-writing.
- **SEC**: US Securities and Exchange Commission — comparator regulator with stronger enforcement powers.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Why SEBI weathered crises (the chain):**
Strong board structure → clear policy/implementation split → IOSCO-aligned principles → safe market design → market resilience to GFC (2008) and COVID-19 meltdown.

### Examples
**Example: Sarbanes-Oxley parallel** — Clause 49 disclosures and audit-committee requirements echo SOX provisions on board independence and financial-reporting accuracy.

**Example: Crisis test** — Indian markets did not collapse during the 2008 GFC or the 2020 pandemic-led meltdown — partial validation of SEBI's design.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "SEBI is stronger than the SEC." → ✅ SEBI matches/excels on supervisory powers and autonomy but ranks **below SEC in enforcement powers**.
- ❌ "IOSCO has 4 principles." → ✅ **Five**: responsibilities, independence, power/resources, clear processes, skilled staff.
- ❌ Confusing IOSCO with IFRS/IASB. → ✅ IOSCO = securities-regulator club; IFRS = accounting standards.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The textbook concedes "some instances where the actions, or absence thereof, of the regulator have come under scrutiny" — so the picture is **net-positive but not unblemished**.
- Effectiveness depends on the Board's "detachment from rentiers" — a structural rather than personal feature.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 5 IOSCO principles → "RIPPS": Responsibilities • Independence • Power/Resources • Processes • Staff.
> - Strong on: design, disclosure, autonomy. Weak on: enforcement, prosecution.
> - 55 countries reviewed by IOSCO.

### Connections
- Builds on: Working of SEBI (16.5) — strengths flow from those reforms.
- Builds on: Challenges (16.6) — the weakness side reflects the new frontiers.
- Forward link: Suggestions (16.8) — IT Act powers, Kotak hiring boost respond to weaknesses.

### Open Questions
1. Would granting SEBI SEC-style enforcement powers compromise its accountability balance?
2. How would SEBI score on a re-run of the IOSCO review post-Adani-affair?

## Section: Suggestions for Making SEBI Effective (16.8) 🟡

### Core Idea
SEBI must reinvent itself for the digital era along three Hindu Business Line aspects (retail product evolution; social-media plug-up; redundant-regulation pruning), seek IT Act 2000 monitoring powers, fix the Kotak-Committee-flagged staffing gap, hire data-science talent laterally, and integrate ESG into corporate-governance reporting.

> **In Simple Terms:** SEBI needs new tools (digital surveillance), new people (tech and data scientists), and new rules (ESG, retail products) — and a pair of scissors to cut redundant rules.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Aspects (Editorial, The Hindu Business Line, 19 May 2022)
1. **Rising retail participation** with new products (curated stock portfolios, digital gold) sitting in a **grey zone** — regulations must keep up.
2. **Social media abuse** (WhatsApp/Twitter/YouTube + encrypting/auto-purging apps) lets rogue players spread false info on dodgy companies — SEBI must rewrite rules to plug this.
3. **Redundant regulations** must be pruned to facilitate market development; SEBI must wield surveillance/decryption powers.

#### Powers under IT Act 2000
SEBI is rightly seeking powers under the **Information Technology Act, 2000** and the **IT (Procedure and Safeguard for Monitoring and Collecting Traffic Data or Information) Rules** to access digital communication channels and carry out **interception and decryption**. To be effective it must also build internal **surveillance-mining and evidence-trail** capacity.

#### Kotak Committee on Corporate Governance — Staffing Gap ⭐
| Regulator | Listed Companies Overseen | Staff |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------|
| SEBI      | ~5,000                     | ~800   |
| US SEC    | ~equal number              | >4,500 |

Recommendation: numerical staff additions **plus** lateral hiring of **data science and tech talent** at competitive pay scales.

#### ESG Investing Trend (Dayal & Sawhney, 2022)
- Global shift from finance-centric models to **socially and environmentally responsible long-term investing**.
- ESG metrics now help assess **company valuations**.
- SEBI's 2021 amendments to listed-firm reporting were a "significant departure" but more is needed.
- Integrating ESG with corporate governance is "extremely consequential" for investment decisions.

#### Five Strengthening Steps (page 70)
India must:
1. Explain ties between business and politics.
2. Increase transparency in ownership structures.
3. Enhance independence and accountability of auditors.
4. Improve enforcement of rules.
5. Ensure regulator/policymaker actions create a **level playing field** irrespective of firm size or political connection.

### Definitions
- **Kotak Committee on Corporate Governance**: SEBI committee whose report flagged severe under-staffing of SEBI relative to the US SEC.
- **ESG investing**: ⭐ Investment approach using **Environmental, Social and Governance** non-financial factors as decision metrics, alongside or above financial returns.
- **IT Act 2000 (relevant rules)**: Indian statute and accompanying Procedure/Safeguard Rules permitting interception, monitoring and decryption of digital information — powers SEBI seeks for market surveillance.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**SEBI's effectiveness roadmap:**
Rule-rewrite (retail/digital) → IT Act powers → numeric + lateral hiring → ESG integration → 5 strengthening actions → enforce pre-emptively → fair, transparent, trustworthy markets.

### Examples
**Example: Digital gold and curated portfolios** — both are popular with retail but neither fits cleanly in existing SEBI rules; classic "grey-zone" cases needing rule rewrite.

**Example: Common MF information platform** — a single platform for all MF disclosures would ease investor search costs.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ "SEC is also under-staffed." → ✅ SEC has **>4,500 staff** for the same ~5,000 firms; SEBI has ~800.
- ❌ "ESG is just environmental." → ✅ E + S + G — **Environmental, Social and Governance**.
- ❌ Treating IT Act powers as already vested with SEBI. → ✅ SEBI is **seeking** them.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Adding staff alone is insufficient — **skill-mix** (data science, tech) matters as much as numbers.
- Pre-emptive enforcement must be balanced against over-regulation that stifles innovation.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 HBL aspects: retail-product gap • social-media plug-up • redundant-rule pruning.
> - Kotak gap: **800 SEBI vs 4,500 SEC** for ~5,000 companies.
> - 5 strengthening steps (politics-business ties, ownership transparency, audit accountability, enforcement, level playing field).
> - ESG = E + S + G.
