## Section: Unit 14 Objectives and Introduction (14.0, 14.1) 🟢

### Core Idea
Unit 14 sets out to define a commercial bank, describe its functions, explain credit creation and its limits, place banking inside economic development, and analyse recent trends in Indian banking. The introduction (14.1) frames commercial banks as the dominant species of financial intermediary — the only one with the special power to issue liabilities accepted as a means of payment.

> **In Simple Terms:** This unit answers four basic questions in order: What is a bank? What does it do? How does it create money out of deposits? And how does it shape the wider economy?

### Key Concepts

#### Learning outcomes of Unit 14
After studying the unit, the learner should be able to (i) define a commercial bank and describe its functions, (ii) illustrate the process of credit creation, (iii) indicate the limits to credit creation, (iv) highlight the role of banking in economic development, and (v) identify trends and growth drivers of Indian banking.

#### FIs as middlemen between lenders and borrowers
Financial intermediaries (FIs) are institutions that serve money and capital markets. They act as middlemen between lenders and borrowers by creating and issuing financial obligations (claims against themselves) to acquire profitable claims against others. A "chief function" of an intermediary is to provide liquidity. FIs are broadly classified into (1) commercial banks and (2) non-banking financial institutions (NBFIs). Bank deposits dominate household financial savings and bank credit dominates external finance, which is why commercial banks are described as the dominant intermediaries.

### Definitions
- **Financial intermediary (FI)**: An institution that serves money and capital markets by acting as middleman between lenders and borrowers, issuing claims against itself to acquire claims against others. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Liquidity**: The ease with which a claim can be converted into a means of payment; providing it is described as the "chief function" of an FI. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Distinguishing power of commercial banks**: Their special ability to issue liabilities (e.g., chequeable deposits) that are accepted as means of payment — this is what separates them from other FIs.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Lenders (savers) → FI issues a claim against itself (e.g., deposit) → FI uses funds to acquire claims against borrowers (e.g., loans) → Profit from rate spread + liquidity provision.

### Examples
**Example: Commercial bank vs other FI**
A mutual fund pools savings and invests, but its units are not accepted as means of payment for grocery purchases. A commercial bank's chequeable deposits are. That difference is the textbook's distinguishing test.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Acceptance of chequeable deposits is "necessary but not sufficient" to be a bank; the bank must also lend.

> - 5 unit objectives: define + functions, credit creation, limits, role in development, India trends.
> - FIs split into (1) Commercial Banks and (2) NBFIs.
> - Banks' uniqueness = liabilities that are means of payment.

## Section: Meaning and Role of Commercial Banks in Economic Development (14.2) 🔴

### Core Idea
A commercial bank is a profit-seeking institution that pays low interest to depositors and charges higher interest to borrowers, earning the spread. More fundamentally, banks are "dealers in debt": every deposit is a debt the bank owes, every banknote a promissory IOU, and every loan an act of self-created debt. Commercial banks are critical for capital formation, innovation, financial inclusion, monetary-policy transmission and overall economic activity.

> **In Simple Terms:** A commercial bank is essentially a debt shop. It buys IOUs cheap (deposits) and sells IOUs dear (loans). Along the way, it manufactures something the rest of the economy can spend — money.

### Key Concepts

#### What is a Commercial Bank? (14.2.1)
The textbook stitches together three definitional anchors. **Paul Samuelson**: "the primary economic function of commercial banks is to hold demand deposits and honour cheques drawn on them — in short, to provide us, the economy with the most important component of our money supply." **R.S. Sayers**: "banks are not merely purveyors of money but also in an important sense manufacturer of money." The textbook itself synthesises: "a bank is a financial institution that deals in debts and credits. It accepts deposits, lends money and creates money. It bridges the gap between the savers and borrowers." Acceptance of chequeable deposits is necessary but not sufficient; lending is the second necessary condition.

#### Banks as dealers in debt
Three illustrations in the source:
1. A deposit is a debt the bank owes the depositor.
2. A banknote is a promissory note — an IOU of the issuing bank to the bearer.
3. Loans and overdrafts are debts the bank creates against itself.

The concept is fluid because socio-economic conditions change — hence the recent emergence of digital banks, neo banks, post-payments banks and small finance banks (covered later in 14.7).

#### Importance and Role in Economic Development (14.2.2)
Five textbook-listed roles:

| # | Role | What it means |
|---|------|---------------|
| a | Promoting capital formation | Banks mobilise idle savings into productive investment |
| b | Encouraging innovation | They finance entrepreneurs and risk-taking ventures |
| c | Influencing economic activity | Deposits are purchasing power; their creation alters aggregate demand |
| d | Promoter of financial inclusion | A pre-requisite of inclusive growth |
| e | Facilitator of monetary policy | A well-developed banking system is essential for effective monetary policy transmission |

Banks also relieve the public of risk in safeguarding surplus income, promote banking habits, discourage unproductive hoarding, and reduce idle capital — channelling these idle funds to businessmen, industrialists and entrepreneurs.

#### Commercial banks in international business
Banks offer trade finance, project finance, payroll, foreign exchange transactions and trading, lockboxes for collecting payments, and general corporate finance. The textbook notes that "without commercial banks, the international finance and import-export industry would not have grown to the level it has today" and that global commercial banking serves as a "worldwide barometer of economic health and business trends."

### Definitions
- **Commercial Bank**: A profit-seeking financial institution that accepts deposits, lends money and creates money; a dealer in debt that bridges savers and borrowers. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Dealer in debt**: A description of banks emphasising that deposits, banknotes and loans are all debts created by or owed by the bank.
- **Banknote**: A promissory note acknowledging the issuing bank's debt to whoever bears it.
- **Bank deposit**: A liability of the bank that simultaneously serves as money for the depositor; "represents purchasing power."
- **Financial inclusion**: Bringing unbanked/underserved populations into the formal financial system; a pre-requisite of inclusive growth. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Saver hands cash to bank → Bank books it as deposit liability → Bank lends to borrower → Bank earns interest spread → Spread = (lending rate − deposit rate) × loanable funds.
2. Channel for development: Idle savings → Bank deposits → Loans to firms/entrepreneurs → Investment → Capital formation → Growth → Higher savings (loop).

### Examples
**Example: Banks as manufacturers of money (Sayers)**
When a bank grants a Rs 1,00,000 loan, it does not hand over physical currency from a vault; it credits a fresh deposit of Rs 1,00,000 to the borrower's account. That deposit is new money that did not exist before — hence "manufacturer of money."

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Saying a bank is defined solely by accepting deposits → ✅ Correct: Acceptance of chequeable deposits is necessary but not sufficient; lending is also required.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating bank deposits as merely stored cash → ✅ Correct: Deposits are debts of the bank that simultaneously circulate as money / purchasing power.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- The line between "commercial banks" and other FIs is being eroded — the textbook explicitly notes the boundary between commercial banks and FIs is "eliminated" as they move toward universal banking.
- Defining "bank" is hard because the concept changes with technology and policy (digital, neo, payments, small finance banks).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Samuelson: hold demand deposits + honour cheques = supply most important component of money supply.
> - Sayers: banks are "manufacturers of money," not mere purveyors.
> - 5 roles in development: (a) capital formation (b) innovation (c) economic activity (d) financial inclusion (e) monetary-policy facilitator.
> - Necessary AND sufficient: chequeable deposits + lending.

## Section: Functions of Commercial Banks (14.3) 🔴

### Core Idea
Commercial bank functions split into **banking functions** (the regulated core) and **non-banking functions** (profit-seeking ancillary services). The banking core is captured in one phrase — "exchanging money claims" — and rests on accepting deposits, lending, discounting bills, and transferring money. The deepest banking function is credit creation, which is also what separates banks from all other FIs. Underneath these activities, banks perform four economic transformations: liability-asset, size, maturity and risk.

> **In Simple Terms:** Banks have a day job (taking deposits, giving loans, moving money) and side hustles (collecting cheques, issuing guarantees, acting as trustees, etc.). The day job is what makes them banks; the side hustles are extras.

### Key Concepts

#### Banking Functions (14.3.1) — Exchanging money claims
The umbrella concept is "exchange of money claims," which includes (1) accepting deposits, (2) loans and advances, (3) discounting bills of exchange, and (4) transferring money. The single most important sub-function is **credit creation** — the function which "distinguishes banks from other financial institutions."

#### Four kinds of deposits
| Type | Key feature |
|------|-------------|
| Saving bank deposit | Limited withdrawal facility, modest interest |
| Current deposit | Free withdrawal, typically no interest, business use |
| Recurring / cumulative deposit | Periodic small contributions, interest paid on maturity |
| Fixed deposit | Locked-in for term, highest interest |

#### Loan vs Overdraft
| Feature | Loan account | Overdraft |
|---------|--------------|-----------|
| Mechanism | Lump sum credited to borrower's account | Borrower can draw cheques over the available credit balance |
| Interest base | On the **whole sum** for the entire period, used or not | Only on the **amount actually utilised** |
| Simplicity | Simpler | More flexible, used for short-term working capital |

#### Discounting bills of exchange
"Technically, purchasing a future claim of money." In essence it is advancing a loan against a promise of future repayment. The key difference from an ordinary loan: with a discounted bill the bank is **certain** that the money is being used for genuine trade purposes (the bill is a real-trade instrument).

#### Transfer of money (NEFT/RTGS)
Money is transferred via bank draft, cheque, or — increasingly — electronic mechanisms NEFT (National Electronic Fund Transfer) and RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement).

#### The Four Transformations of Bank Intermediation
The textbook distils all banking functions into four economic transformations:
1. **Liability-Asset transformation** — accepting deposits as liabilities and converting them into loans (assets).
2. **Size transformation** — pooling many small deposits to provide large loans.
3. **Maturity transformation** — offering depositors short-maturity claims while extending long-maturity loans to borrowers.
4. **Risk transformation** — distributing risk through diversification, lowering the risk that would prevail in direct lending.

A fifth point in the source notes that bank functions are widening and becoming more customer-centric.

#### Non-banking Functions (14.3.2)
Services rendered "not as banks but as profit-seeking firms" — outside banking proper. Nine textbook items:
1. Collection of cheques, drafts, bills, hundis and other instruments (inland and foreign).
2. Issue of performance and financial guarantees.
3. Remittance facilities (drafts, mail transfers).
4. Safe custody of deeds, securities and safe deposit vaults.
5. Purchase and sale of securities for constituents.
6. Underwriting and bankers for new issues; sometimes private placement.
7. Acting as executors and trustees.
8. Agency services — sale of foreign exchange, acceptance of income-tax payments, etc.
9. Miscellaneous — traveller's cheques, gift cheques, tax assistance, investment advice, references, business and statistical information; cards and overdrafts.

The textbook calls banks "department stores of financial services."

### Definitions
- **Demand deposit**: Deposit repayable on demand; cheques drawn on it must be honoured.
- **Bill of exchange**: A negotiable instrument that orders payment at a future date; discounting it = bank buys the future claim today at a discount. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Loan**: Lump-sum credit; interest charged on whole amount for the whole period.
- **Overdraft**: Permission to draw beyond the credit balance; interest only on amount used.
- **NEFT**: National Electronic Fund Transfer — electronic batch-based transfer system.
- **RTGS**: Real Time Gross Settlement — large-value electronic transfers settled individually in real time.
- **Liability-Asset transformation**: Converting deposit liabilities into loan assets. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Size transformation**: Aggregating many small deposits into large loans. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Maturity transformation**: Mismatching short-term deposits and long-term loans according to depositor and borrower preferences. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Risk transformation**: Reducing per-unit risk through diversification of the loan portfolio. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Underwriting**: Bank guarantees subscription to a new issue of securities, taking up the unsold portion if needed.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Banking core: Accept deposits → Pay low interest → Lend / discount → Charge higher interest → Earn spread + create credit.
2. Four transformations as a pipeline: Deposit (liability) → Bank balance sheet → Loan (asset); many small → one large; short maturity → long maturity; concentrated risk → diversified risk.
3. Discounting a bill: Trader holds bill maturing in 90 days → Bank pays discounted face value today → Bank collects full face value at maturity → Discount = interest on the implicit loan.

### Examples
**Example: Loan vs Overdraft interest cost**
Suppose Rs 1,00,000 sanctioned at 10% p.a. for 1 year, but borrower actually uses only Rs 40,000 for 6 months.
- Loan account: Interest ≈ Rs 1,00,000 × 10% × 1 = Rs 10,000 (charged on full sum, full period).
- Overdraft: Interest ≈ Rs 40,000 × 10% × 0.5 = Rs 2,000 (only on used portion, used period).

This shows why overdrafts suit fluctuating working-capital needs.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Listing "credit creation" as a non-banking function → ✅ Correct: Credit creation is the **most important** banking function and the one that distinguishes banks from other FIs.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating discounting a bill as identical to a loan → ✅ Correct: With a discounted bill the bank is certain the funds finance genuine trade; with a plain loan, end-use is less verifiable.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Non-banking functions vary widely across banks depending on size and type — they are optional add-ons, not defining features.
- Modern commercial banks also invest in securities and may even invest in issues they help take public — blurring the line with investment banking.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Banking functions = exchange of money claims = (1) deposits (2) loans/advances (3) discount bills (4) transfer money.
> - Four deposit types: savings, current, recurring/cumulative, fixed.
> - **Four transformations** (must memorise): Liability-Asset, Size, Maturity, Risk.
> - Loan = interest on whole sum; Overdraft = interest on used amount only.
> - NEFT (batch) vs RTGS (real-time, large value).
> - "Department stores of financial services."

- Feeds into: Credit Creation (14.5, Chunk 3) where the loan-creates-deposit logic is formalised; Liquidity vs Profitability (14.6, later chunk) which trades off the four transformations.

## Section: Structure of Commercial Banks (14.4) 🟡

### Core Idea
The structure of commercial banking varies across countries depending on tradition, economy, politics, public attitude and topography. Four classic structural types are branch banking, unit banking, group banking and chain banking. Beyond structural type, banks are also classified by clientele (retail vs corporate), by scope (universal vs differentiated), and within differentiated banking by design (HDBS vs VDBS as recommended by the Nachiket Mor Committee).

> **In Simple Terms:** Banks differ along three axes: how many offices they run (branch/unit/group/chain), who they serve (retail vs corporate), and how wide a menu they offer (universal vs differentiated).

### Key Concepts

#### Branch, Unit, Group and Chain Banking

| System | Defining feature | Origin / Example |
|--------|------------------|------------------|
| **Branch banking** | One bank with many branches across the country | India |
| **Unit banking** | Each bank confined to a single office | Originated in the USA |
| **Group banking** | Two or more banks owned/operated by one corporation; group organised around a "key bank" controlled by a holding company | Combines advantages of branch + unit; suffers from supervision issues and cross-failure contagion |
| **Chain banking** | Two or more banks controlled by a single person or group via stock ownership or otherwise; less formal than group banking | Developed in USA to overcome unit-banking drawbacks |

#### Retail vs Corporate Banking (14.4.1)

| Feature | Retail banking | Corporate banking |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| Other names | Consumer banking, personal banking | Business banking |
| Customers | Individual / household consumers | Firms, businesses |
| Visibility | Highly visible (branches in cities) | Less visible to public |
| Funding role | Customer deposits = important funding source for banks | Loan origination = key profit centre |
| Profitability | Depends on volume | High profit but also major source of write-downs when loans sour |

The textbook flags that banks focusing purely on retail are few; most retail business runs as a separate division.

#### Glass-Steagall Act, 1933
The term "corporate banking" was originally used in the United States to **distinguish it from investment banking after the Glass-Steagall Act of 1933 separated the two activities**. The law was repealed in the 1990s, after which corporate and investment banking services have been offered under the same umbrella by most banks in the US and elsewhere.

#### Universal Banking (14.4.2)
Universal banking is a system in which banks provide a wide variety of financial services, including both **commercial and investment** services. A universal bank may offer credit, loans, deposits, asset management, investment advisory, payment processing, securities transactions, underwriting and financial analysis. The system **allows** but does not **require** banks to offer all services — banks may still choose to specialise. Combines commercial banking + investment banking + (sometimes) insurance under one roof.

#### Pros and cons of Universal Banking

**Arguments in favour:**
- Better risk diversification.
- Lowered costs through economies of scale (larger investment volume per unit of overhead).
- Economies of scale and scope help cope with business cycles, since multi-product firms handle demand shifts better.
- Joint provision is cheaper than separate units.

**Concerns:**
- Increases systemic risk in case of failure.
- Size enables monopoly behaviour and predatory pricing against small specialist players.
- Regulatory complexity, since not all institutions do all activities and rules vary.

#### Differentiated Banking (14.4.3)
Differentiated banks operate in a **niche segment** — distinguished by capital requirement, scope of activities, or area of operations — and offer a limited range of services or work under a different regulatory regime. The textbook lists pre-existing examples: Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs), Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS), Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), Local Area Banks (LABs), Small Finance Banks (SFBs) and Payments Banks.

#### Nachiket Mor Committee — HDBS vs VDBS
The RBI set up a committee headed by **Shri Nachiket Mor** on Comprehensive Financial Services for Small Businesses and Low-Income Households to address financial inclusion. The committee proposed two banking-system designs based on the functional building blocks of **payments, deposits, and credit**:

| Design | Building blocks | Differentiator |
|--------|-----------------|----------------|
| **HDBS — Horizontally Differentiated Banking System** | Each bank is a **full-service bank** combining payments + deposits + credit | Differentiated by **size, geography or sectoral focus** |
| **VDBS — Vertically Differentiated Banking System** | Full-service bank replaced by banks **specialising in one or more** of the three building blocks | Differentiated by **function** |

The committee specifically suggested licensing of Payments Banks and wholesale banks as differentiated banks.

### Definitions
- **Branch banking**: System where one bank operates many branches across geographies.
- **Unit banking**: System where each bank is confined to a single office; originated in the USA.
- **Group banking**: Two or more banks owned/operated by a corporation, organised around a key bank under a holding company.
- **Chain banking**: Two or more banks controlled by a single person or group via stock ownership; less formal than group banking.
- **Retail banking**: The division dealing directly with individual / consumer customers; aka consumer banking, personal banking. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Corporate banking**: The division dealing with corporate / business customers; aka business banking. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Glass-Steagall Act, 1933**: US law that separated commercial banking from investment banking; repealed in the 1990s. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Universal banking**: System where banks provide a wide variety of financial services including commercial and investment services from within one entity. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Differentiated banking**: Banking system in which banks function in a niche segment, offering limited services or operating under a special regulatory regime. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **HDBS (Horizontally Differentiated Banking System)**: Nachiket Mor design where banks remain full-service but differ by size/geography/sectoral focus. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **VDBS (Vertically Differentiated Banking System)**: Nachiket Mor design where banks specialise in one or more of payments, deposits or credit. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Indian context: RBI sees financial-inclusion gap → Sets up Nachiket Mor Committee → Committee proposes HDBS and VDBS → RBI licenses Payments Banks and Small Finance Banks as differentiated banks.
2. US context: Glass-Steagall Act 1933 → Forced separation of commercial vs investment banking → Repeal in 1990s → Re-merger under universal-banking-style umbrellas.

### Examples
**Example: Indian differentiated banks (textbook list)**
UCBs, PACS, RRBs, LABs, SFBs, Payments Banks — each operates in a localised area or for an underserved segment. None is a full-service universal bank.

**Example: Universal vs Differentiated**
- A large private-sector bank that offers savings accounts, mortgages, demat trading, mutual funds, insurance and investment-banking services = universal bank.
- A Payments Bank that can take deposits up to a cap and process payments but cannot lend = differentiated bank (vertical specialisation, no credit block).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating universal banking as legally compulsory once allowed → ✅ Correct: It permits but does not require offering all services; banks may still choose to specialise.
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing HDBS with VDBS → ✅ Correct: HDBS keeps the full-service bank but varies by size/geography; VDBS splits the bank along the payment/deposit/credit axes.
- ❌ Mistake: Calling Glass-Steagall an Indian law → ✅ Correct: It is a US law of 1933, repealed in the 1990s.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Group and chain banking enjoy advantages of both branch and unit banking but suffer from contagion risk if one constituent fails.
- The textbook warns: do not confuse "universal bank" with any financial institutions that have similar names but different functions.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Four structural types: Branch, Unit, Group, Chain.
> - Retail = consumer/personal; Corporate = business.
> - Glass-Steagall (US, 1933) → separated commercial & investment banking → repealed 1990s.
> - Universal banking = commercial + investment + (sometimes) insurance under one roof.
> - Universal pros: diversification, scale, scope, cycle resilience. Cons: systemic risk, monopoly potential.
> - Differentiated banking examples in India: UCBs, PACS, RRBs, LABs, SFBs, Payments Banks.
> - **Nachiket Mor Committee** → HDBS (full-service, differ by size/geography/sector) vs VDBS (specialise in payments / deposits / credit).
