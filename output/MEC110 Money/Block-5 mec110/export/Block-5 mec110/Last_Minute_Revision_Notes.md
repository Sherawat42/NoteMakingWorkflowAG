### Meaning and Role of Commercial Banks in Economic Development (14.2) 🔴

**What is a Commercial Bank? (14.2.1)**

**Banks as dealers in debt**
1. A deposit is a debt the bank owes the depositor.
2. A banknote is a promissory note — an IOU of the issuing bank to the bearer.
3. Loans and overdrafts are debts the bank creates against itself.

**Commercial banks in international business**
- **Commercial Bank**: A profit-seeking financial institution that accepts deposits, lends money and creates money; a dealer in debt that bridges savers and borrowers. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Dealer in debt**: A description of banks emphasising that deposits, banknotes and loans are all debts created by or owed by the bank.
- **Banknote**: A promissory note acknowledging the issuing bank's debt to whoever bears it.
- **Bank deposit**: A liability of the bank that simultaneously serves as money for the depositor; "represents purchasing power."
- **Financial inclusion**: Bringing unbanked/underserved populations into the formal financial system; a pre-requisite of inclusive growth. ⭐ (exam-important)
1. Saver hands cash to bank → Bank books it as deposit liability → Bank lends to borrower → Bank earns interest spread → Spread = (lending rate − deposit rate) × loanable funds.
2. Channel for development: Idle savings → Bank deposits → Loans to firms/entrepreneurs → Investment → Capital formation → Growth → Higher savings (loop).

**Banking Functions (14.3.1) — Exchanging money claims**

**Discounting bills of exchange**

**Transfer of money (NEFT/RTGS)**

**The Four Transformations of Bank Intermediation**
1. **Liability-Asset transformation** — accepting deposits as liabilities and converting them into loans (assets).
2. **Size transformation** — pooling many small deposits to provide large loans.
3. **Maturity transformation** — offering depositors short-maturity claims while extending long-maturity loans to borrowers.
4. **Risk transformation** — distributing risk through diversification, lowering the risk that would prevail in direct lending.

**Non-banking Functions (14.3.2)**
1. Collection of cheques, drafts, bills, hundis and other instruments (inland and foreign).
2. Issue of performance and financial guarantees.
3. Remittance facilities (drafts, mail transfers).
4. Safe custody of deeds, securities and safe deposit vaults.
5. Purchase and sale of securities for constituents.
6. Underwriting and bankers for new issues; sometimes private placement.
7. Acting as executors and trustees.
8. Agency services — sale of foreign exchange, acceptance of income-tax payments, etc.
9. Miscellaneous — traveller's cheques, gift cheques, tax assistance, investment advice, references, business and statistical information; cards and overdrafts.
- **Demand deposit**: Deposit repayable on demand; cheques drawn on it must be honoured.
- **Bill of exchange**: A negotiable instrument that orders payment at a future date; discounting it = bank buys the future claim today at a discount. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Loan**: Lump-sum credit; interest charged on whole amount for the whole period.
- **Overdraft**: Permission to draw beyond the credit balance; interest only on amount used.
- **NEFT**: National Electronic Fund Transfer — electronic batch-based transfer system.
- **RTGS**: Real Time Gross Settlement — large-value electronic transfers settled individually in real time.
- **Liability-Asset transformation**: Converting deposit liabilities into loan assets. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Size transformation**: Aggregating many small deposits into large loans. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Maturity transformation**: Mismatching short-term deposits and long-term loans according to depositor and borrower preferences. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Risk transformation**: Reducing per-unit risk through diversification of the loan portfolio. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Underwriting**: Bank guarantees subscription to a new issue of securities, taking up the unsold portion if needed.
1. Banking core: Accept deposits → Pay low interest → Lend / discount → Charge higher interest → Earn spread + create credit.
2. Four transformations as a pipeline: Deposit (liability) → Bank balance sheet → Loan (asset); many small → one large; short maturity → long maturity; concentrated risk → diversified risk.
3. Discounting a bill: Trader holds bill maturing in 90 days → Bank pays discounted face value today → Bank collects full face value at maturity → Discount = interest on the implicit loan.
- Loan account: Interest ≈ Rs 1,00,000 × 10% × 1 = Rs 10,000 (charged on full sum, full period).
- Overdraft: Interest ≈ Rs 40,000 × 10% × 0.5 = Rs 2,000 (only on used portion, used period).

### Creation of Credit/Deposits (14.5) 🔴

**Primary vs Derivative Deposits**
| Aspect | Primary Deposit | Derivative Deposit |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Cash brought in by depositor | Loan sanctioned & credited by bank |
| Effect on reserves | Cash reserves of bank rise | No new cash; only book entry |
| Effect on money stock | No net change (cash → deposit) | Net increase in money supply |
| Other name | Actual deposit | Created deposit / credit creation |

**The Deposit/Money Multiplier**
- `Δd = Δa / r`  ⭐

**Banking System as a Whole vs Single Bank**
- **Primary Deposit**: A deposit account opened by a bank by accepting cash from the public. ⭐
- **Derivative Deposit**: A deposit created by the bank in the process of granting credit; the loan, once sanctioned, is credited to the borrower's account, increasing the total stock of money. Identical to "credit creation." ⭐
- **Cash Reserve Ratio (r)**: The fraction (percentage) of deposits that a bank keeps in cash form (or with the central bank). ⭐
- **Credit Creation**: The power of banks to multiply loans and advances by creating deposits.
1. Customer A deposits ₹100 cash → bank holds ₹10 reserve, lends ₹90 to Borrower B.
2. B spends ₹90 → recipient deposits ₹90 in (some) bank → that bank holds ₹9, lends ₹81.
3. Process repeats; geometric series sums to ₹100 / 0.10 = ₹1,000 in total deposits.
4. Total new credit created = ₹900 (= ₹1,000 − ₹100 initial).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing a single commercial bank can multiply deposits 10×. → ✅ Correct: Only the banking system as a whole can; an individual bank loses cash to other banks via the clearing house.
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking banks "create money out of thin air." → ✅ Correct: Crowther — "the bank does not create money out of thin air; it transmutes other forms of wealth into money." The income-yielding securities in the economy set the outer limit.
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing primary and derivative deposits. → ✅ Correct: Primary = cash in; derivative = loan out.
- The multiplier `Δd = Δa / r` is a maximum, not an automatic outcome — actual expansion depends on willingness to lend and to borrow.
- The bank cannot let its cash reserve fall below the required percentage of total deposit liabilities (textbook example: 10%).

### Limits to Credit Creation (14.5.1) 🔴

**Seven Limits to Credit Creation**
| # | Limit | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| i | Clearing House Restriction on a Single Bank | One bank cannot expand alone — it would lose cash to others through clearing; banks must move in step. |
| ii | Liquidity Ratio | Bank must keep a prudent share of assets liquid; this caps how much it is willing to lend or invest. |
| iii | Amount of Cash in Existence | Government and central bank fix the supply of legal tender; total cash sets the base on which the multiplier works. |
| iv | Cash People Prefer to Hold | If the public hoards cash instead of depositing it, deposits cannot grow; greater cheque/digital use eases this. |
| v | Collateral Security Available | Banks lend against acceptable security (the more shift-able, the better); shortage of good security limits lending. |
| vi | State of the Economy | Loans are taken only when there are profitable ventures; in a downturn, demand for credit dries up. |
| vii | Monetary Policy of the Central Bank | RBI's tools (CRR, SLR, repo, OMO etc.) can directly throttle bank credit expansion. |
- **Collateral Security**: An asset pledged by the borrower that the bank can convert to cash if the loan is not repaid; the more liquid (shift-able) the security, the more acceptable it is.
- **Legal Tender Money**: Currency whose acceptance in payment of debt cannot be refused; supply is determined by the government and central bank.
1. Crowther's qualifying observation: banks do not create money "out of thin air" — they convert (transmute) other wealth (income-yielding securities) into money.
2. The total volume of income-yielding securities sets the overall ceiling on credit creation.
3. The bank must keep a minimum cash reserve (e.g., 10%) against deposit liabilities.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Listing only the central bank's monetary policy as the limit. → ✅ Correct: Six other limits (some structural, some behavioural) are equally binding.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating "collateral security" only as a borrower's problem. → ✅ Correct: It is also a system-level limit — economy-wide shortage of good security caps total bank lending.
- ❌ Mistake: Assuming character of borrower is irrelevant once collateral is given. → ✅ Correct: Textbook is explicit — "the first consideration is the character of the borrower"; the bank prefers the loan to be repaid and collateral to be a last resort.
- Increasing public confidence in banks and wider cheque/digital use directly **relax** limit (iv).
- Limits (vi) and (vii) are pro-cyclical and policy-driven respectively — they vary in tightness over time.

**Quick Recall:**
- SEVEN limits: (i) clearing house, (ii) liquidity ratio, (iii) cash in existence, (iv) cash public prefers to hold, (v) collateral, (vi) state of economy, (vii) monetary policy of central bank
- Crowther: banks transmute, not create from thin air

### Liquidity vs Profitability of Assets (14.6.1) 🔴

**The Liquidity-Profitability Trade-off**
| Aspect | Liquidity | Profitability |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Meet depositors' cash demand on time | Maximise income for shareholders |
| Most extreme asset | Cash / money | Long-term loans, property |
| Income | Low or zero | High |
| Convertibility | Quick, without loss | Slow, possibly with loss |
| Risk to bank if neglected | Loss of public confidence, bank run | Loss of profitability, shareholder discontent |

**Anticipated Income Concept**
- **Anticipated Income Concept**: A liquidity doctrine under which loans are repaid out of the borrower's expected future income rather than by selling or shifting the asset; underpins consumer-durable and term lending.
- **Shift-ability Concept**: The doctrine that an asset is liquid if it can be transferred to another institution (especially the central bank); broader than pure cashability and enables longer-dated lending.
1. Traditional concept of liquidity → focus on cash/near-cash.
2. Replaced by shift-ability concept → liquidity through transferability to other institutions; allows longer-dated, broader lending.
3. Replaced/supplemented by anticipated income concept → liquidity through borrower's future income stream.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Saying liquidity and profitability are independent goals. → ✅ Correct: They are opposite criteria — one comes at the cost of the other.
- ❌ Mistake: Treating shift-ability and anticipated income as the same thing. → ✅ Correct: Shift-ability = transfer to another institution; anticipated income = repayment out of future earnings.
- The "secret of success" depends on the macro environment; in stress, what was shift-able may suddenly become illiquid.
- For consumer-durable loans, only the anticipated income concept rationalises calling them "liquid."

### Difference Between Commercial Banks and NBFIs (15.2.1) 🔴

**Seven Points of Difference**
| # | Dimension | Commercial Banks | NBFIs |
|---|---|---|---|
| i | Composition | Homogeneous group, all doing banking business | Heterogeneous; vary widely (development banks → mutual savings societies) |
| ii | Specialisation | Broad-based banking | Niche; RBI classifies and regulates each type **differently** |
| iii | Loan tenor (traditional) | Confined mainly to short-term in money market | Span short, medium and long-term credit (commercial banks now also do all three) |
| iv | Credit creation | Generate **multiple expansion of credit**; driven by excess reserves and CRR; rate of interest has little significance | Only **mobilise savings for investment**; operations governed by rate-of-interest structure; pay high interest to attract funds |
| v | Speed of lending | Credit creation involves **less time** | Lending takes **longer time** |
| vi | Macroeconomic effect | Less volatile via credit creation | Can influence liquidity and create economic destabilisation when portfolio composition shifts or claims on NBFIs grow at the cost of banks' demand deposits |
| vii | Operational edge | Standardised, payment-system embedded | Lower transaction costs, quicker decision-making, customer orientation, prompt service, expertise in niche segments |

**Sayers' "Creators vs Purveyors" of Credit**
- **Creators of Credit**: Banks — generate new deposits and credit through the multiple-expansion mechanism, by virtue of being part of the payment system. ⭐
- **Purveyors of Credit**: NBFIs — distribute (lend) credit but cannot create new deposits; lend only out of resources placed with them. ⭐
1. Bank receives a primary deposit → creates derivative deposits via lending → multiple expansion (banks are creators).
2. NBFI receives savings/funds at high interest → lends those exact resources to borrowers → no multiple expansion (NBFIs are purveyors).
3. NBFI portfolio shifts or claim growth at the expense of bank demand deposits → can spill into liquidity instability.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Saying NBFIs also "create" credit. → ✅ Correct: They only **purvey**; only banks create credit (Sayers).
- ❌ Mistake: Listing "tenor" as a hard distinction. → ✅ Correct: The textbook itself notes that **commercial banks also now provide short, medium and long-term credit** to all sectors and segments.
- ❌ Mistake: Confusing speed of decision-making with speed of credit creation. → ✅ Correct: NBFIs are quicker decision-makers (point vii), but banks are quicker credit-creators (point v).
- Point (vi) — NBFIs can destabilise — is the textbook hook to the post-2008 shadow-banking concern and India's IL&FS-style episodes.

### Functions and Importance of NBFIs (15.3) 🔴

**Five Core Services**
1. **Mobilisation of savings.**
2. **Channelisation of funds into investment.**
3. **Brokers of loanable funds.**
4. **Stabilising the capital market.**
5. **Providing liquidity.**

**Liquidity, Divisibility, Risk Pooling**
| Quality | Definition | NBFI's role |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | The ease with which an asset's full market value can be realised once a decision to sell has been made | NBFIs enhance liquidity through specialisation and scale |
| Divisibility | The extent to which an asset can be traded in small denominations | NBFIs break up large (lumpy) claims and aggregate small claims to meet community preferences |
| Risk pooling | The extent to which an asset spreads default risk by pooling | NBFIs pool many small claims, achieving scope for risk-pooling far beyond what individuals can |

**Importance for the Indian Economy**
- **Broaden the spectrum of risks** available to investors.
- **Improve efficiency** of investment and savings.
- **Foster a risk-management culture** by attracting customers who are least able to bear risks.
- **Fill gaps** that arise in bank-based systems.
- **(a) Reach out to inaccessible areas**, and
- **(b) Act as substitutes (not just complements) to commercial banks** when banks face stricter regulatory constraints.
- **Liquidity (NBFI sense)**: The ease with which an asset's full market value can be realised after a decision to sell.
- **Divisibility**: The extent to which an asset can be traded in small denominations; achieved by NBFIs by aggregating/breaking-up claims.
- **Risk Pooling**: Spreading default risk by pooling many underlying promises; NBFIs have far greater scope for this than individuals. ⭐
1. Many small savers → NBFI aggregates funds.
2. NBFI breaks/aggregates claims into preferred denominations (divisibility).
3. NBFI pools risk across many borrowers/instruments.
4. NBFI lends/invests across niche segments banks cannot cost-effectively serve.
5. When banks are constrained, NBFIs step up — backup intermediation (Greenspan).

### Regulatory Mechanism for NBFCs — Scale Based Regulation (15.5.3) 🔴

**Statutory Origins**
- **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934**.
- **Chapter III B added in 1963** to deal with regulation of Non-Banking Financial Institutions.
- Recognised that non-banking financial activity is integral to the financial system and needs **vigilance and due diligence**.
- Most regulatory change since has been **incremental**.
- **2023**: RBI released the detailed SBR regulatory structure.

**The Four Layers — Composition**
1. Non-deposit-taking NBFCs **below ₹1,000 crore asset size**, **PLUS**
2. NBFCs undertaking these activities (regardless of size):
   - NBFC-Peer-to-Peer Lending Platform (**NBFC-P2P**)
   - NBFC-Account Aggregator (**NBFC-AA**)
   - Non-Operative Financial Holding Company (**NOFHC**)
   - NBFC **not availing public funds and not having any customer interface**
1. **All deposit-taking NBFCs (NBFCs-D)**, irrespective of asset size, **PLUS**
2. **Non-deposit-taking NBFCs with asset size ≥ ₹1,000 crore**, **PLUS**
3. NBFCs undertaking these activities:
   - Standalone Primary Dealer (**SPD**)
   - Infrastructure Debt Fund-NBFC (**IDF-NBFC**)
   - Core Investment Company (**CIC**)
   - Housing Finance Company (**HFC**)
   - NBFC-Infrastructure Finance Company (**NBFC-IFC**)
- NBFCs **specifically identified by RBI** as warranting enhanced regulatory requirements based on a set of parameters and a scoring methodology.
- The **top ten** eligible NBFCs by asset size **always reside in UL**, irrespective of any other factor.
- Currently **15 NBFCs (including 5 HFCs)** are in UL.
- **Ideally remains empty.**
- Populated only if RBI sees a **substantial increase in potential systemic risk** from specific NBFCs in the Upper Layer.
- Such NBFCs are then moved from UL to TL.

**Activity-Based Layer Rules (always apply, regardless of size)**
| Activity | Always sits in |
|---|---|
| NBFC-P2P, NBFC-AA, NOFHC, NBFC-without-public-funds-without-customer-interface | **Base Layer** (only) |
| NBFC-D, NBFC-CIC, NBFC-IFC, HFC | **Middle or Upper** (NEVER Base) |
| SPD, IDF-NBFC | **Middle Layer** (always) |
| NBFC-ICC, NBFC-MFI, NBFC-Factor, MGC | **Any layer** (per SBR parameters) |
| Government-owned NBFCs | **Base or Middle** (only) |

**Group Consolidation Rule**
- NBFCs that are part of a common Group (or floated by a common set of promoters) are **NOT viewed standalone**.
- Total assets of all NBFCs in the Group are **consolidated** to determine threshold for Middle Layer classification.
- If consolidated asset size of the Group's NBFCs is **₹1,000 crore and above**, then each **NBFC-ICC, NBFC-MFI, NBFC-Factor and NBFC-MGC** in the Group is classified as Middle Layer (and ML regulations apply).
- An NBFC-D within the Group is also governed under the Non-Banking [continues into chunk 5...]
- **NBFC-BL (Base Layer)**: Smallest, lowest-risk layer; non-deposit-taking NBFCs <₹1,000 cr + P2P/AA/NOFHC/no-public-funds-no-customer-interface NBFCs. ⭐
- **NBFC-ML (Middle Layer)**: All NBFCs-D (any size) + non-deposit NBFCs ≥₹1,000 cr + SPD/IDF-NBFC/CIC/HFC/NBFC-IFC. ⭐
- **NBFC-UL (Upper Layer)**: RBI-identified, enhanced regulatory oversight; **top-10 by asset size always here**; currently 15 NBFCs (incl. 5 HFCs). ⭐
- **NBFC-TL (Top Layer)**: Ideally empty; only populated if RBI sees substantial systemic risk from a UL NBFC. ⭐
- **Chapter III B of RBI Act 1934**: The statutory anchor (added 1963) for regulation of NBFIs in India.
1. NBFC registers under Companies Act + RBI licence.
2. Classified into one of 11 activity types (Table 15.2).
3. Slotted into one of the 4 SBR layers using:
   - Activity-based rules (some types are pinned to a fixed layer).
   - Size threshold (₹1,000 cr for non-deposit NBFCs).
   - Deposit-taking status (NBFCs-D ⇒ ML at minimum).
   - Group consolidation (≥ ₹1,000 cr Group → ML for ICC/MFI/Factor/MGC).
   - Top-10 by assets ⇒ UL automatically.
   - RBI scoring methodology ⇒ UL identification.
4. UL with substantial systemic risk ⇒ moved to TL.

**Definition & Market Share of HFCs**

- **HFC (Housing Finance Company)**: A corporate entity under the Companies Act 1956 primarily providing housing finance, with ≥ 50% assets as housing loans and ≥ 75% of those to individual homebuyers. ⭐
- **NOF (Net Owned Fund)**: Principal-business yardstick used by RBI to assess HFC compliance. ⭐
- **LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio)**: Buffer of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) sufficient to survive a 30-day acute liquidity stress. ⭐
- **CRAR / CAR (Capital Adequacy Ratio)**: Minimum capital ratio HFCs must maintain on an ongoing basis. ⭐
- **LTV (Loan-To-Value) Ratio**: Cap on loan amount as a % of collateral value (50% for listed shares; 75% for gold jewellery for HFCs).
- **Co-Lending Model (CLM)**: Joint lending arrangement between HFCs and banks (housing & MSME loans).
1. All non-deposit-taking HFCs with asset size ≥ ₹100 crore and all deposit-taking HFCs (any asset size) must pursue **liquidity risk management**.
2. Liquidity risk management must cover gap-limit adherence, use of liquidity-risk monitoring tools, and a **stock approach to liquidity risk**.
3. HFCs must maintain a liquidity buffer in the form of **LCR** — sufficient HQLA to survive a 30-day acute liquidity stress.
4. **LTV cap** — 50% on loans against listed shares as collateral; 75% on loans against gold-jewellery collateral.
5. HFCs cannot accept/renew **public deposits** unless they have obtained a **minimum investment-grade rating** for fixed deposits from an approved credit rating agency at least once a year.
6. HFCs must keep **full cover** for public deposits at all times. If an HFC defaults on a public deposit, it cannot grant any loan/credit, make any investment, or create any other asset until the default is cured.
7. HFCs **cannot lend against their own shares**.
8. **Minimum CAR**: not less than **13% by March 2020**, **14% by March 2021**, and **15% by March 2022**.
9. **Single-borrower exposure** ≤ 15% of owned fund; **single-group exposure** ≤ 25% of owned fund.

### Issues and Concerns in NBFI Sector 🔴

**Inherent Risks of NBFIs**

**Absence of Safety Nets**
- The benefit of a **central bank as lender of last resort (LOLR)**.
- Coverage from **deposit insurance institutions**.

**Risk Cascade — How NBFI Stress Becomes Systemic**
- They concentrate exposure to specific sectors.
- They depend on commercial banks for funding (direct & indirect) → **interconnectedness risk** is higher.
- Asset-liability mismatches **accentuate liquidity risk**.
- Liquidity risk → solvency risk → systemic risk in rapid sequence.

**Thorat Committee (August 2011)**
- Reducing **clutter** and possibilities of **regulatory arbitrage**.
- Recognising regulatory priorities and the fundamental differences with banks.
- Plugging **arbitrage points** between bank and NBFC regulation.
- Making **all prudential norms applicable to banks** also applicable to NBFCs.
- **Pro-cyclicality**: Tendency to amplify cyclical swings — lend more in booms, cut sharply in busts. ⭐
- **Wholesale Funding**: Short-term institutional funding (CPs, CDs, bank borrowings) — riskier than retail deposits.
- **Lender of Last Resort (LOLR)**: Central-bank function of providing emergency liquidity; usually unavailable to NBFIs.
- **Asset-Liability Mismatch (ALM)**: Funding short-tenor liabilities with long-tenor assets; primary cause of NBFI liquidity stress. ⭐
- **Contagion Risk**: Risk that distress at one institution spreads through the financial system.
- **Thorat Committee (Aug 2011)**: RBI working group on Issues and Concerns in NBFC Sector. ⭐
1. NBFI builds up leverage and concentrates exposure in a niche sector.
2. Wholesale funding dries up → liquidity risk emerges.
3. Asset-liability mismatch accentuates liquidity squeeze.
4. Liquidity risk → solvency risk.
5. Solvency stress + interconnectedness with banks → **systemic risk**.

### Functions of SEBI (Protective / Regulatory / Developmental) 🔴

**Protective Functions**
- (i) **Prohibits insider trading** — buying/selling by directors, employees, promoters; SEBI bars companies from purchasing their own shares from the secondary market.
- (ii) **Checks price rigging** — unnatural price fluctuations causing investor losses; SEBI maintains close watch.
- (iii) **Promotes fair practices** — prohibits insider trading and unfair trade practices in securities.
- (iv) **Financial education** — online/offline sessions on market insights and money management; helps investors evaluate options.
- (v) **Regulates operations of depositories, participants, custodians of securities, foreign portfolio investors and credit rating agencies**.

**Regulatory Functions**
- (i) Defines rules, regulations, **guidelines and codes of conduct** for corporates and intermediaries.
- (ii) Monitors **substantial acquisitions of shares and take-overs** of companies.
- (iii) Conducts **inquiries and audits** of stock exchanges.
- (iv) Regulates the working of **stockbrokers, merchant bankers, share transfer agents, trustees, registrars** etc.
- (v) Regulates and registers **mutual funds**.

**Developmental Functions**
- (i) **Training of intermediaries** in the security market.
- (ii) Introduction of **trading through electronic means / internet** with the help of registered brokers.
- (iii) Making **underwriting an optional system** to reduce the cost of issue.
- (iv) **Promotes the development** of securities market and business.
- (v) Enables a **competitive professional market** for intermediaries.

|--------|------------|------------|---------------|

**Credit Functions**
- Frames policy/guidelines for rural financial institutions; prepares **annual credit plans for all districts** to identify credit potential.

- (iv) Provides **funds to State governments** for rural developmental/promotional activities; specifically refinances **RRBs** in backward regions.
- (v) **Long-term loans** to institutions for agriculture **against State government guarantee**.
- (vi) Finances **research and development** of agricultural and rural industries.

- Suited for investors looking for **debt-plus returns** with **higher risk than fixed-income** schemes.

**MFO & Regulation**
- **MFOs** (Mutual Fund Organisations) employ specialists to analyse investments.
- Buy/sell decisions taken in capital market based on specialist appraisal.
- **Regulator:** SEBI — regulates, monitors and inspects mutual funds.
- **Mutual Fund**: Professionally managed investment scheme run by an AMC that pools investor money into stocks/bonds/securities. ⭐
- **AMC**: Asset Management Company — manages the mutual fund. ⭐
- **MFO**: Mutual Fund Organisation — pools resources of public and runs an expert team for buy/sell decisions. ⭐
- **SEBI**: Securities and Exchange Board of India — regulator of mutual funds (and securities markets). ⭐
- **Equity Mutual Fund**: Mutual fund investing principally in stocks; aka stock fund. ⭐
- **Debt Mutual Fund**: Mutual fund investing in fixed-income securities (T-bills, G-secs, corporate bonds, money-market). ⭐
- **Hybrid Mutual Fund**: Mutual fund combining stocks and bonds; aka asset allocation fund. ⭐
- **Solution-oriented Mutual Fund**: Open-ended scheme for long-term planning, mixing equity and bonds, debt-plus returns with higher risk. ⭐
- **Index Fund**: Passively managed equity fund tracking an index.
1. **Pooling:** Investor buys MF units → money pooled at AMC → MFO experts analyse opportunities → AMC executes buy/sell in capital market → returns flow back to investor as NAV change/dividend.
2. **SEBI oversight:** AMC registers → SEBI inspects → ongoing disclosure compliance.

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Equity funds are always actively managed." → ✅ Can be **active or passive (index fund)**.
- ❌ "Debt funds give higher returns than equity funds." → ✅ Equity funds **generate higher returns** than debt funds or fixed deposits.
- ❌ "Solution-oriented funds are low-risk fixed income." → ✅ They mix equity + bonds and carry **higher risk than fixed-income** schemes.
- ❌ "Mutual funds are regulated by RBI." → ✅ Regulated by **SEBI**.
- The textbook gives **four broad categories**; SEBI's 2017 product-categorisation actually uses 5 groups (the 5th being "Other Schemes" — index/ETFs/FoF). Stick to the textbook's four for exam answers.
- Solution-oriented funds typically include retirement and children's-gift schemes (not detailed in textbook).

**Quick Recall:**

- Equity = stocks; categorised by size/style/geography.
- Debt = T-bills, G-secs, corporate bonds, money market.
- Hybrid = stocks + bonds (asset allocation).
- Solution-oriented = open-ended, long-term, equity + bonds, debt-plus returns + higher risk.

- **Credit Rating Agency**: Company that assigns credit ratings to a debtor's ability to repay debt and likelihood of default. Indian: **CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, ONICRA, FITCH, SMERA**. ⭐
- **CRISIL**: Indian credit-rating agency (Credit Rating Information Services of India Ltd). ⭐
- **ICRA**: Investment Information and Credit Rating Agency. ⭐
- **CARE**: Credit Analysis and Research Ltd. ⭐
- **ONICRA**: Indian credit-rating agency (small businesses + individuals). ⭐
- **FITCH**: Fitch Ratings (international, India presence). ⭐
- **SMERA**: SME Rating Agency of India Ltd. ⭐
- **Customized loans**: Credit strategy designed for the needs of a particular firm or business. ⭐
- **Direct finance**: Funds raised by borrower directly from the financial market without an intermediary. ⭐
- **Indirect finance**: Funds raised through a financial intermediary. ⭐
- **Institutional investors**: Entities pooling others' funds to invest across instruments and asset classes (e.g., insurers, mutual funds, pension funds). ⭐
- **Microfinance**: Loans, savings and insurance services to small entrepreneurs lacking access to traditional capital; aka microcredit. ⭐

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ "Direct finance always means equity." → ✅ Direct finance = borrowing directly from market (could be commercial paper, bonds, equity) without intermediary.
- ❌ "Institutional investors only invest in equities." → ✅ They invest across **a variety of financial instruments and asset classes**.
- ❌ "Microfinance only lends." → ✅ Includes **loans, savings AND insurance** services.
