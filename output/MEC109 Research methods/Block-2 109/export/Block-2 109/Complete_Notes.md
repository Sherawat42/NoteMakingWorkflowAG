## Section: Introduction to Research Design and Methods 🟢

### Core Idea
Social science research has traditionally relied on two distinct approaches — quantitative (QN) and qualitative (QL). Confusion between *research design* and *research methods* has been common; the unit aims to clarify this distinction. In recent years, *mixed methods research*, combining both QN and QL in a single project, has gained growing prominence, especially in economics.

> **In Simple Terms:** Think of research design as the blueprint of a building (deciding *what* to build and *why*) and research methods as the tools and materials used to construct it (deciding *how* to build it). Confusing the two is like judging a blueprint by the quality of the bricks.

## Section: Types of Research 🔴

### Core Idea
Research can be classified in several ways depending on its nature, purpose, and field. Each classification is based on a dichotomous distinction, though real-world research often overlaps categories. Understanding these types is foundational to choosing the appropriate research design.

> **In Simple Terms:** Just like how roads can be classified as highways or local streets, research can be classified by its purpose (theoretical vs. applied), its method (quantitative vs. qualitative), or its approach (descriptive vs. explanatory). The categories are tools for understanding, not rigid boxes.

### Key Concepts

#### Theoretical and Applied Research
**Theoretical (Fundamental) Research** aims to formulate or verify theory, with knowledge as an end in itself. It explores logical processes underlying phenomena without concern for practical use. It may verify old theory or establish a new one. Fundamental research in economics includes developing economic theories or evolving quantitative techniques for measuring parameters like multiplier, elasticity of demand, etc. It is essentially **positive** in nature.

**Applied Research** aims to find solutions to specific real-world problems. It applies principles discovered by basic research to practical situations. It also tests theories empirically and improves tools of measurement. Applied research in economics includes poverty measurement, employment, rural development, and environmental analysis.

| Aspect | Theoretical Research | Applied Research |
|--------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Goal | Knowledge for its own sake | Solving a real problem |
| Output | Theories, laws, principles | Solutions, policy recommendations |
| Nature | Positive | Normative/Practical |
| Example (Economics) | Elasticity models, multiplier theory | Poverty indices, employment surveys |

#### Descriptive and Explanatory Research
**Descriptive Research** describes a situation, event, or social system as it exists. Researchers have no control over variables — they can only report what has happened or is happening. Surveys, comparative studies, and fact-finding enquiries fall here. It addresses questions like *"How does X vary with Y?"*

**Explanatory Research** aims to establish cause-and-effect relationships. It uses available facts/information to critically evaluate whether one variable explains another. Example: *"Is the increase in agricultural productivity explained by improved rural roads?"*

#### Quantitative and Qualitative Research
**Quantitative Research** systematically investigates quantitative properties and their relationships. It aims to develop mathematical models and test hypotheses.

Key attributes of QN research:
- Belief in a single reality
- Pursuit of universal laws
- Separation of researcher from researched
- Value-free research possible
- Large, representative samples
- Deductive approach with falsifiable hypotheses
- Formal hypothesis testing

**Qualitative Research** captures meaning and interpretation. It stresses the socially constructed nature of reality and the interdependence between researcher and subject.

Key attributes of QL research:
- Belief in multiple, constructed realities
- Interdependence of knower and known
- Researcher cannot be separated from research subject
- Heavy role of context
- Cannot generalise beyond immediate context
- Non-separation of cause and effect
- Inductive, exploratory approaches
- Small, purposively chosen samples
- Holistic system analysis

| Attribute | Quantitative (QN) | Qualitative (QL) |
|-----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Reality | Single | Multiple/Constructed |
| Researcher-subject | Separate | Interdependent |
| Sample size | Large, representative | Small, purposive |
| Logic | Deductive | Inductive |
| Hypothesis | Pre-formed, tested | Emerges during study |
| Generalisation | Universal | Context-specific |
| Statistical tools | Yes | Rarely |

#### Conceptual and Empirical Research
**Conceptual Research** deals with abstract ideas or theory. Used by philosophers and thinkers to develop new concepts or re-interpret existing theories.

**Empirical Research** relies on experience or observation. It is data-based and subject to verification by observation and experiment. Particularly useful when validation or verification of an aspect is required.

#### Other Types of Research
- **Exploratory Research**: Develops hypotheses rather than testing them. Open-ended.
- **Formalised Research**: Structured; tests specific hypotheses within a definitive framework.
- **Historical Research**: Uses existing documents to study past events.
- **Experimental Research**: Identifies causal factors through controlled experiments.
- **Evaluative Research**: Examines cost-effectiveness of a programme; guides policy decisions on continuation or modification.
- **Action Research**: Deals with real-world problems; gathers feedback to generate ideas for improvement. (Details in Unit 20)

### Definitions
- **Quantitative Variable**: A variable whose values can be measured and expressed in numerical terms (e.g., yield, height, weight).
- **Qualitative Variable**: A variable expressed as categories or choices based on attributes (e.g., gender, opinion — yes/no, agreeing/disagreeing).
- **Theoretical Research**: Research aimed at formulating or verifying theory; knowledge as an end in itself.
- **Applied Research**: Research aimed at finding solutions to practical problems by applying principles and laws.
- **Descriptive Research**: Research that describes situations or events as they exist, without researcher control over variables.
- **Explanatory Research**: Research aimed at establishing cause-and-effect relationships.
- **Empirical Research**: Data-based research subject to verification by observation and experiment.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Theoretical = knowledge for its own sake; Applied = solving real problems
> - Descriptive = describes; Explanatory = cause & effect
> - QN = deductive, single reality, large sample; QL = inductive, multiple realities, small sample
> - Conceptual = abstract/theory; Empirical = data-based, verifiable
> - Other types: exploratory, formalised, historical, experimental, evaluative, action

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating qualitative research as "less rigorous" than quantitative → ✅ Correct: Both have their own internal rigour; the choice depends on the research question.
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking descriptive and explanatory research are mutually exclusive → ✅ Correct: A study can describe first and then explain; they often complement each other.

## Section: Research Design 🔴

### Core Idea
Research design is the **logical structure of an enquiry** — it specifies what kind of evidence is needed to answer a research question. It is not about *how* data will be collected (that's research methods), but about *what* logical structure will ensure unambiguous conclusions. The function of research design is to make sure the evidence obtained enables us to answer initial research questions as clearly as possible.

> **In Simple Terms:** Research design is like a building blueprint. Just as you must decide whether you need a factory, a school, or a house before drawing plans, a researcher must clarify the research question before designing the study. The blueprint specifies what you need to build — not the tools you'll use to build it.

### Key Concepts

#### Components of Research Design
Research design comprises four components:
1. **Sampling Design**: Type of sampling method (random or non-random sampling)
2. **Statistical Design**: Sample size and the method of analysis to be adopted
3. **Observational Design**: The instrument of data collection (e.g., questionnaire, observation)
4. **Operational Design**: Specific details of how sampling, statistical, and observational designs are to be carried out

### Definitions
- **Research Design**: A logical structure of an enquiry; specifies the type of evidence needed to answer research questions and eliminate alternative explanations. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Research design = logical problem (not logistical)
> - 4 components: Sampling design, Statistical design, Observational design, Operational design
> - Purpose: ensure evidence answers research questions **unambiguously** and **eliminates rival explanations**
> - Guided by: the research question

## Section: Research Design vs. Research Methods 🟡

### Core Idea
Research design and research methods are fundamentally different and must not be confused. Research design is a *logical structure*; research methods are *tools and techniques* of data collection and analysis. No research design inherently requires a specific method of data collection.

> **In Simple Terms:** Research design is the architectural plan; research methods are the construction tools. A well-designed plan can be executed with timber, concrete, or steel — similarly, a research design can use questionnaires, observations, or interviews.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Equating cross-sectional designs with questionnaires only, or case studies with participant observation → ✅ Correct: Research designs should be evaluated by their ability to draw unambiguous conclusions, not by the strengths/weaknesses of the data collection method.

## Section: Research Methods 🔴

### Core Idea
Research methods refer to tools and techniques of data collection and data analysis. They fall into two broad categories: Mono Methods (either quantitative or qualitative alone) and Mixed Methods (combining at least one QN and one QL component).

> **In Simple Terms:** If research design is the blueprint, research methods are the specific tools — hammers, drills, or cranes. You can use one type of tool (mono method) or a combination (mixed methods).

### Key Concepts

#### Quantitative Methods
The researcher tests theory through empirical observations, using structured questionnaires and interviews. The focus is on measurable variables. Descriptive, analytical, and inferential statistical techniques are used. Experimental research design structures the study. Generalisation and replication are emphasised.

#### Qualitative Methods
The researcher starts with observation to generate theory, concentrating on the meaning of observations in naturalistic settings. Open-ended questionnaires, key informant interviews, group discussions, and unstructured interviews are used. Content analysis, case study, action research, participatory methods, and cluster/factor analysis are tools for analysis. (Details in Units 13–20)

#### Mixed Methods (Introduction)
The combination of **at least one qualitative and at least one quantitative** component — related to measurement scale, tools of data collection, or data analysis technique — in a **single research study** is called **Mixed Methods Research**. Examples:
- Using more than one measurement procedure (e.g., different ways to measure job satisfaction)
- Using two or more methods of data collection (e.g., observation + interview + questionnaire for studying decent work)
- Using two or more data analysis techniques (e.g., content analysis + factor analysis)

- **Research Methods**: Tools and techniques of data collection and data analysis. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mono Methods**: Research using either quantitative or qualitative method alone.
- **Mixed Methods Research**: Research combining at least one qualitative and one quantitative component in a single study. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: Research Methods — Mixed Methods (Philosophical Compatibility) 🔴
<!-- See Chunk 001 for start of this section -->

### Core Idea
The apparent incompatibility between QN and QL methods on philosophical grounds is not a valid barrier to mixed methods research. This is because (i) both QL and QN are large heterogeneous families with internally varying attributes; (ii) the "single reality" proposition in QN is inconsistent with actual research; (iii) sample sizes are not rigidly tied to the method. What matters most is embedding the chosen method within the research question, data needs, and theoretical grounding.

> **In Simple Terms:** The dispute between QN and QL methods is like arguing that electric cars and petrol cars can never share the same road. In practice, both coexist and can even work together. Mixing methods is not philosophically dishonest — it is methodologically pragmatic.

### Key Concepts

#### Three Reasons Why QN-QL Incompatibility Argument Fails (Bergman, 2008)
1. **Heterogeneity within families**: QL and QN are large, internally varied families. No single set of qualities uniquely defines one family versus the other. Hence the boundary between them is blurry.
2. **Single reality is inconsistent with QN in practice**: The emergent structures in QN research (questionnaire design, item selection, statistical interpretation) involve co-construction between researcher and respondent — a fundamentally social process, contradicting the "single reality" claim.
3. **Sample size is not tied to method**: Small QN studies and large QL studies both exist. Sample size is a design choice, not a defining feature of the method.

#### Three Methodological Guidelines (to avoid mistakes in research design)
a) Do not argue for or against QL or QN as an abstract matter. Justify method selection based on research questions, data needs, theoretical grounding, and research design.
b) Differentiate between data *collection* methods (unstructured narrative interview vs. structured survey) and data *analysis* methods (qualitative content analysis vs. quantitative content analysis).
c) Be more aware of the inductive and deductive analytic phases in your research.

## Section: The Rationale for Mixed Methods Research 🔴

### Core Idea
Mixed methods research has gained tremendous popularity in social, behavioural, and related sciences. The core argument: combining QN and QL offsets the weaknesses of each and draws on the strengths of both to produce a more complete, credible, and comprehensive account of the research question.

> **In Simple Terms:** Neither a hammer alone nor a screwdriver alone can build furniture. Using both tools together gets the job done better. Similarly, mixing quantitative and qualitative methods gives a fuller, richer picture than either alone.

### Key Concepts

#### Nine Justifications for Using Mixed Methods
1. **Strengths/weaknesses offset**: Each method has strengths and weaknesses; combining offsets weaknesses and capitalises on strengths.
2. **Comprehensive account**: Causal explanation → quantitative; reason/process explanation → qualitative. Together, a fuller account.
3. **Different questions**: QN and QL can answer different but complementary aspects of the same phenomenon.
4. **Sequential development**: Results from one strand generate questions or hypotheses for the next (sequential mixed methods).
5. **Credibility assessment**: One strand assesses the credibility of inferences from the other (exploratory + confirmatory).
6. **Divergent/comparative pictures**: Mixed methods can generate divergent findings that are compared and contrasted for richer insights.
7. **Structures vs. process**: QN captures social structures; QL captures social processes. Both needed together.
8. **Hypothesis generation and testing**: QL generates hypothesis; QN tests it — within a single project.
9. **Diversity of views**: Uncovering relationships between variables (QN) + revealing meaning among participants (QL) = comprehensive diversity of perspectives.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 9 rationales for mixed methods: offset weaknesses, comprehensive account, different questions, sequential development, credibility, divergence, structures + process, generate + test hypothesis, diversity of views
> - Core logic: exploit the best of both QN and QL in a single design

## Section: Forms of Mixed Methods Research Designs 🔴

### Core Idea
Mixed methods designs are classified into 4 forms based on **sequencing** (which method comes first) and **prioritisation** (which method is the core / dominant one). The forms use a notation where UPPERCASE = core/dominant method and lowercase = supplementary method.

> **In Simple Terms:** Mixed methods designs are like recipes. You can start with a base of quantitative work and season it with qualitative insights, or the reverse. The capital letters in the notation tell you which is the main ingredient.

### Key Concepts

#### Form 1: qual-QUANT (Preliminary Qualitative → Core Quantitative)
Qualitative methods are used *first* as preliminary input to a *core quantitative* design. The QL inputs help generate hypotheses, develop questionnaire content, or interpret quantitative data. QL strengths are used to strengthen the QN core.

#### Form 2: QUANT-qual (Core Quantitative → Supplementary Qualitative)
Quantitative study is the core. A supplementary qualitative follow-up extends or explains QN results — demonstrating bases for results, examining reasons for failed hypotheses, exploring theoretical significance of outliers.

#### Form 3: quan-QUAL (Preliminary Quantitative → Core Qualitative)
A qualitative study is the core, but preliminary quantitative inputs guide it — locating major differences between sub-groups, guiding purposive sampling, establishing results to pursue.

#### Form 4: QUAL-quan (Core Qualitative → Supplementary Quantitative)
Qualitative study is core. Supplementary quantitative methods develop measures of key concepts and test relationships across different samples.

| Form | Notation | Core | Supplementary | Use case |
|------|----------|------|---------------|---------|
| 1 | qual-QUANT | Quantitative | Qualitative (preliminary) | QL inputs strengthen Q design |
| 2 | QUANT-qual | Quantitative | Qualitative (follow-up) | Explain/extend QN results |
| 3 | quan-QUAL | Qualitative | Quantitative (preliminary) | QN guides purposive sampling |
| 4 | QUAL-quan | Qualitative | Quantitative (follow-up) | QN measures key QL concepts |

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 4 forms: qual-QUANT, QUANT-qual, quan-QUAL, QUAL-quan
> - UPPERCASE = dominant/core method; lowercase = supplementary
> - Sequencing + prioritisation = the two axes defining all 4 forms

## Section: Case Studies of Mixed Methods Research Design 🟡

### Core Idea
Three real-world doctoral research studies from IGNOU illustrate how mixed methods are applied in economics research. They demonstrate the three types of mixed method integration: (i) QN-dominant with QL data quantified, (ii) QL-dominant using QN analysis tools, and (iii) QN and QL used together for diversity of views.

> **In Simple Terms:** These case studies show that mixed methods is not an abstract idea — it is a practical toolkit used in actual PhD research to get richer and more reliable answers.

### Examples

**Case 1 — Decent Work Study (Nausheen Nizami, IGNOU)**
- Framework: Post-positivist paradigm, inductive logic of inquiry (QN dominant)
- Topic: Decent Work — concept, measurement, status in IT industry
- Mixed strategy: QL data (qualitative survey responses) → converted into quantitative variables and analysed using quantitative techniques (Composite Index, Factor Analysis, Phi Coefficient, Chi-square test)
- Key finding: 94% of IT employees showed deficit in decent work; higher earnings not sufficient for well-being; age and social class influence decent work status.
- Form: **qual-QUANT** (QL data quantified for QN analysis)

**Case 2 — Gender and Climate Change in Agriculture (Mamta Mehar, IGNOU)**
- Framework: Interpretative paradigm, abductive logic (QL dominant)
- Topic: Gender-specific impacts of climate variation in agriculture, Bihar
- Mixed strategy: QL approach (interpretative, case study, abduction) + QN tools (survey questionnaire, multivariate probit model)
- Key findings: Gender and climate variation interlinked; male farmers have higher adaptation probability; women face mobility, access, and resource constraints.
- Form: **quan-QUAL** (QN survey data analysed within QL interpretative framework)

**Case 3 — Quality of Education (Charu Jain, IGNOU)**
- Framework: Both QN and QL applied
- Topic: Quality of secondary education and its determinants in Delhi schools
- Mixed strategy: QN (structured questionnaires, SPSS/STATA) + QL (direct observation, key informant interviews, open-ended questions)
- Key finding: Secondary education boosts income, health outcomes, savings; school resources and teacher quality are key determinants.
- Form: **QUANT + qual** (diversity of views — QN uncovers relationships, QL reveals meaning)

## Section: Methods of Data Collection 🔴

### Core Idea
Quantitative research requires the systematic measurement of variables. The quality of data directly determines the validity of research conclusions. There are three broad methods of data collection: the Census and Survey Method, the Observation Method, and the Experimental Method.

> **In Simple Terms:** Collecting good data is like cooking with fresh ingredients — if your inputs are bad, even the most sophisticated recipe (analysis) will produce poor results. The three methods are just different ways of gathering those ingredients.

### Key Concepts

#### Census and Survey Method
Involves a carefully planned study to collect data from the subject units. Two approaches:
- **Complete enumeration (Census)**: Data collected from *all* units in the population. Example: Census of Population (2011) — collected demographic, economic, social, and cultural data for all persons in India.
- **Sample Survey**: Data collected from a *suitably selected part* (sample) of the population. Example: National Sample Survey Organisation (NSSO) household surveys on socio-economic characteristics.

#### Observation Method
Records data as things occur, using an appropriate measurement method. Examples:
- Recording a patient's body temperature every hour
- Daily recording of maximum/minimum temperatures at a location
- Recording rainfall during monsoon seasons

#### Experimental Method
Collects data through well-designed, controlled statistical experiments to isolate the effect of one variable on another. Example: Determining the optimal rate of manure application for maximum crop yield — all other variables (water, soil quality, seeds, insecticides) are controlled so only manure varies. Related branches: *Design and Analysis of Experiments* and *Analysis of Variance*.

| Method | Key Feature | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| Census & Survey | Planned study; complete or partial coverage | Population Census, NSSO surveys |
| Observation | Records as events occur | Temperature recording, patient vitals |
| Experimental | Controlled experiment to isolate one variable | Manure-yield experiment |

### Definitions
- **Census**: Complete enumeration — data collected from all units in the population. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sample Survey**: Data collected from a selected part (sample) of the population.
- **Respondent units / Informants**: Units from which data are collected in a survey.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 methods: Census/Survey, Observation, Experimental
> - Census = all units; Sample survey = selected units
> - Observation = as-it-happens recording; Experimental = controlled variable testing

## Section: Tools of Data Collection 🔴

### Core Idea
Data collection tools translate research requirements into a collection format. The two primary tools are the **questionnaire** and the **schedule**. Modern technology has expanded the range of data collection modalities. Data can be primary (collected fresh) or secondary (already collected by others).

> **In Simple Terms:** Just as a doctor uses different instruments (stethoscope, thermometer, or MRI) depending on what they need to measure, a researcher uses different tools (questionnaire, schedule, electronic device) depending on the research design.

### Key Concepts

#### Questionnaire
- A set of pre-specified questions answered directly by respondents, without investigator guidance on interpretation.
- Assumes the respondent can understand and answer questions independently.
- Respondent-bias minimised by keeping questions simple and direct.
- Responses often in the form of "yes/no/can't say" or graded (good/satisfactory/unsatisfactory).

#### Schedule
- Detailed questions where the investigator (not the respondent) reads and explains the questions.
- The investigator's training and instructions guide elicitation of reliable information.
- Intensive investigator training is required to avoid investigator-bias.

| Feature | Questionnaire | Schedule |
|---------|--------------|---------|
| Who fills it | Respondent | Investigator |
| Complexity | Simple, direct | Detailed, explained |
| Bias risk | Respondent-bias | Investigator-bias (if poorly trained) |
| Use case | Literate, self-sufficient sample | General population, complex concepts |

#### Data Collection Modes
- Personal contact with respondents
- Telephone interviews
- E-mail / internet ("chatting")
- Mail method (postal questionnaire with reply envelope)
- SMS / social media

#### Mechanical / Electronic Devices
- Time-recording machines for factory workers
- Electronic Data Transfer (EDT) — e.g., customs houses supplying trade data to DGCI&S

#### Primary vs. Secondary Data
- **Primary data**: Collected afresh by the research agency for the specific study.
- **Secondary data**: Data already collected by another agency; available in publications, compact discs, or websites. Must be carefully evaluated for suitability before use.

#### Errors in Data
- **Sampling errors**: Arise from confining data collection to a sample instead of the whole population.
- **Non-sampling errors**: Arise from faulty measurement, unclear definitions, inaccurate measurement, investigator bias, non-response, or omissions.
- **Total survey error** = Sampling errors + Non-sampling errors; must be minimised for data quality.

### Definitions
- **Questionnaire**: A pre-specified set of questions answered by respondents independently.
- **Schedule**: A data collection format where the investigator elicits answers through guided questioning.
- **Primary Data**: Data collected fresh by the conducting agency for the specific enquiry. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Secondary Data**: Data previously collected by another agency. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sampling Error**: Error arising from using a sample instead of the full population.
- **Non-sampling Error**: Error from faulty measurement, bias, non-response, or omissions.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Questionnaire = respondent fills; Schedule = investigator elicits
> - Primary = collected fresh; Secondary = published/existing data from other agencies
> - Total survey error = sampling error + non-sampling error

## Section: Sampling Design — Population and Sample Aggregates and Inference 🔴

### Core Idea
Sampling design is about how to select a sample from a population. Before choosing a method, key statistical terminology must be clear: the distinction between *parameter* (population characteristic) and *statistic* (sample characteristic), and the process of *inference* — using sample statistics to estimate population parameters.

> **In Simple Terms:** A population is the entire group you want to study (e.g., all students in a university), and a sample is the group you actually measure. A parameter is a fact about the whole population (which you often don't know), and a statistic is the corresponding fact from your sample (which you compute). Inference is the process of using the known sample statistic to estimate the unknown population parameter.

### Key Concepts

#### Population and Sample Notation
- Population: N units, denoted U_i (i = 1 to N); variable value = Y_i; **upper case** = population
- Sample: n units, denoted u_i (i = 1 to n); variable value = y_i; **lower case** = sample

#### Key Formulae
| Parameter | Formula | Notes |
|-----------|---------|-------|
| Population total | Y = ΣY_i | Sum over all N units |
| Population mean | M = (1/N)ΣY_i | ⭐ |
| Population variance | σ² = (1/N)ΣY_i² – M² | |
| Sample mean | m = (1/n)Σy_i | ⭐ |
| Sample variance | s² = (1/n)Σy_i² – m² | |
| Population proportion | P = N₁/N | N₁ = units with specified characteristic |
| Sample proportion | p | Fraction in sample with characteristic |

#### Process of Inference
1. Draw a sample from the population
2. Compute the **estimator** (a sample statistic that estimates a parameter). Example: sample mean *m* estimates population mean *µ*
3. The computed value for a specific sample is the **estimate**
4. The difference between the estimate and the true parameter = **sampling error** (assuming no non-sampling errors)
5. Different samples → different estimates → a *distribution* of estimates

### Definitions
- **Parameter**: Any function of population values Y_i (e.g., population mean M). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Statistic**: Any function of sample observations y_i (e.g., sample mean m). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Estimator (Point Estimator)**: A sample statistic used to estimate a population parameter. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Estimate (Point Estimate)**: The value of the estimator computed from a specific sample. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Inference**: The process of drawing conclusions about a population from sample results. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sampling Error**: Error in using a sample estimate in place of the true population parameter.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Population → parameter (UPPER CASE); Sample → statistic (lower case)
> - m (sample mean) = estimator of M (population mean)
> - Inference = using sample to learn about population
> - Sampling error = m – M (for a given sample)

## Section: Non-Random Sampling 🟡

### Core Idea
Non-random (non-probability) sampling methods select units without giving each population unit a pre-specified known chance of selection. This makes the relationship between sample and parent population unclear and introduces subjective bias. Despite this, non-random sampling has useful applications in exploratory and preliminary research.

> **In Simple Terms:** Non-random sampling is like picking fruit by hand — you tend to pick the ones that look best (or are easiest to reach), not necessarily a true cross-section. Random sampling is like using a machine to pick at fixed intervals from all parts of the orchard.

### Key Concepts

#### Types of Non-Random Sampling

| Type | Description | Use Case |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Judgment Sampling** | Expert selects units based on knowledge of population | Auditor selects transactions to examine |
| **Convenience Sampling** | Selecting units easiest to reach | People leaving a cinema; mall shoppers |
| **Purposive Sampling** | Group specially picked for a specific purpose | Similar to judgment sampling; preliminary research |
| **Quota Sampling** | Strata identified; convenience/judgment sample selected from each | Ensures sub-group representation, but without randomness |
| **Heterogeneity Sampling** | Units chosen to include all opinions or views | Capturing diverse perspectives |
| **Snowball Sampling** | Initial respondents refer additional respondents; used for rare characteristics | Tracing rare genetic trait; invisible/vulnerable social groups |

#### Limitations
- Relationship between sample and parent population is *unclear*
- Selection seems subjective and discretionary → reflects researcher/investigator bias
- Not helpful in drawing **valid conclusions** about the parent population
- Non-random samples are **not representative** samples

#### Uses (despite limitations)
- **Inexpensive and quick** preliminary insight into a variable under study
- Useful for designing a more rigorous scientific enquiry later
- Valuable in **exploratory research**

### Definitions
- **Representative Sample**: A sample that contains the relevant characteristics of the population *in the same proportion* as in the population. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Snowball Sampling**: Using referrals from initial respondents to identify additional respondents; used for rare characteristics or invisible/vulnerable groups.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 6 types: Judgment, Convenience, Purposive, Quota, Heterogeneity, Snowball
> - Non-random ≠ representative → cannot draw valid population-level conclusions
> - But useful: exploratory, preliminary, cheap, quick

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes

## Section: Precision of Estimates — Standard Errors and Confidence Intervals 🔴

### Core Idea
When a random sample is drawn from a population, the sample mean (an estimate of the population mean) will vary from sample to sample. The *sampling distribution* of the sample mean describes how these estimates are distributed across all possible samples. The *standard error (SE)* measures this variability and quantifies the precision of the estimate. Confidence intervals use the SE to give a range within which the true population parameter likely falls.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine you weigh 30 apples from a bag and calculate their average weight. If you repeated this with another 30 apples from the same bag, you'd get a slightly different average. The standard error tells you how much your average weight is likely to vary — the smaller the SE, the more confident you can be that your sample average is close to the true average for all apples.

### Key Concepts

#### Sampling Distribution of Sample Mean
- The sample mean *m* computed from a random sample is a random variable.
- All possible values of *m* from repeated samples form a **sampling distribution** of the sample mean.
- If parent population is N(µ, σ²), then sampling distribution of *m* is **N(µ, σ²/n)**.
- The variability of *m* (σ²/n) is much smaller than the population variance (σ²) and decreases as *n* increases → precision increases with sample size.

#### Unbiased Estimator
- Estimator "t" of parameter "θ" is **unbiased** if E(t) = θ (the mean of its sampling distribution equals the true parameter).
- The sample mean *m* is an unbiased estimator of the population mean M (under SRSWR/SRSWOR).
- If E(t) = Kθ or K+θ, bias can be corrected by using t/K or (t–K) as the estimator.

#### Standard Error (SE) and Coefficient of Variation (CV/RSE)
- **Standard Error (SE)**: Standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator. Measures the precision of the estimate.
  - SE(m) = σ/√n
- **Coefficient of Variation (CV) = Relative Standard Error (RSE)**: SE divided by mean — dimensionless measure of relative precision.
  - CV or RSE = C = σ/M = SE(m)/ m̄

#### Properties of the Normal Distribution (for Confidence Intervals)
| Coverage | SD from mean | Z-value |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| ~68% | ±1 SD | 1.00 |
| ~95% | ±1.96 SD | 1.96 |
| ~99% | ±2.576 SD | 2.576 |

#### Confidence Intervals (Known σ²)
A confidence interval for the population mean M with confidence coefficient 99%:
```
Lower limit = m – 2.576 × (σ/√n)
Upper limit = m + 2.576 × (σ/√n)
```
**General Rule**: Estimate ± (z-value × Standard Error of estimate)

#### Confidence Intervals (Unknown σ² — Student's t-distribution)
When population variance σ² is not known:
- Use unbiased estimate of variance: **v(y) = [ss]²/(n–1)** where [ss]² = Σ(yᵢ – m)²
- Unbiased estimate of sampling variance of m: **v(m) = v(y)/n**
- The statistic t = (m – M) / √v(m) follows **Student's t-distribution** with (n–1) degrees of freedom.
- Confidence interval: **[m – tα × √v(m) < M < m + tα × √v(m)]**

#### Central Limit Theorem (Non-Normal Parent Population)
Even if the population is **not** normally distributed:
- The sampling distribution of m has mean = population mean M, regardless of sample size.
- As sample size n increases, the sampling distribution of m approaches **normal distribution**.
- For n ≥ 30: use normal approximation (or t ≈ z). Proceed as in the known-σ² case.

### Definitions
- **Sampling Distribution**: The probability distribution of a statistic over all possible samples of a given size from a population. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Unbiased Estimator**: An estimator whose sampling distribution has a mean equal to the true parameter value. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Standard Error (SE)**: Standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator; measures precision. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Coefficient of Variation (CV) / Relative Standard Error (RSE)**: SE divided by mean; dimensionless. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Confidence Interval**: A range within which the population parameter lies with a stated probability (confidence coefficient). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sampling Variance**: Variance of the sampling distribution of an estimator.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - SE(m) = σ/√n → precision ↑ as n ↑, but not proportionally (need 4× sample for 2× precision)
> - 99% CI: m ± 2.576 SE; 95% CI: m ± 1.96 SE
> - Unknown σ²: use t-distribution with v(m) = v(y)/n
> - Central Limit Theorem: for n ≥ 30, sampling dist. is approximately normal regardless of parent distribution

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: SE decreases proportionally with sample size → ✅ Correct: SE = σ/√n; it decreases in proportion to √n, not n. To halve the SE, you need to quadruple the sample size.
- ❌ Mistake: SRSWOR gives no advantage over SRSWR when sampling fraction is very small → ✅ Correct: When n/N < 0.05, the finite population correction ≈ 1, and the advantage of SRSWOR disappears.

## Section: Determination of Sample Size 🟡

### Core Idea
Random sampling allows us to determine the required sample size in advance — based on either a desired precision level (CV or permissible error) or a sanctioned budget. This is a major advantage over non-random sampling.

> **In Simple Terms:** Before cooking a feast, you decide how many guests you're hosting and plan your ingredients accordingly. Sample size determination is the same — you decide what level of accuracy you need, then calculate how large a sample that requires.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**Method 1: Based on desired CV**
```
C(m) = C/√n  →  n = [C/C(m)]²
```
Where C = population CV (from prior data), C(m) = desired CV of sample mean.

**Method 2: Based on permissible error (e)**
```
n = [2.576 × C / e]²
```
Where e = permissible relative error = (m – M)/M.

**Method 3: Budget-constrained (cost function)**
- Linear cost: F = F₀ + F₁n → n = (F – F₀)/F₁
- With loss from imprecision: minimise total cost L(n) → **n = [(ℓ/2)(C/F₁)]^(2/3)**

## Section: Simple Random Sampling with Replacement (SRSWR) 🔴

### Core Idea
SRSWR is the simplest random sampling method. Every unit has an **equal probability** of selection at each draw, and selected units are **placed back** before the next draw. Units can appear more than once in the sample. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean.

> **In Simple Terms:** SRSWR is like drawing a name from a hat, noting it, and putting it back before the next draw. The same name could come up again.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Operational Procedure** (using Random Number Tables):
1. Number all N population units from 1 to N.
2. Turn to a random page of the random number table.
3. Select random numbers between 1 and N; record the corresponding units.
4. If a number > N, reject it. If a number repeats, include that unit again (with replacement).
5. Repeat until n units are selected.

### Key Formulae (SRSWR)
| Quantity | Formula | Formula Number |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| Estimator of M | m_srswr = (1/n)Σyᵢ | 2.25 |
| Sampling Variance of m | V(m_srswr) = σ²/n | 2.26 |
| Standard Error of m | SE(m_srswr) = σ/√n | 2.27 |
| CV/RSE of m | C(m_srswr) = C(y)/√n | 2.28 |
| Unbiased estimate of σ² | v(y) = [ss]²/(n–1) | 2.29 |
| Unbiased estimate of V(m) | v(m_srswr) = v(y)/n | 2.30 |
| Estimator of population total Y | Y* = Nm | 2.31 |
| V(Y*) | N²(σ²/n) | 2.32 |
| Sample proportion estimator of P | p_srswr (unbiased) | 2.34 |
| V(p_srswr) | PQ/n | 2.35 |

### Definitions
- **SRSWR**: Random sampling where each unit has equal probability of selection at every draw with replacement; units can repeat. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - SRSWR: equal probability, with replacement, units can repeat
> - m_srswr = unbiased estimator of M
> - V(m_srswr) = σ²/n; SE = σ/√n
> - Precision ↑ as n ↑, but SE reduction not proportional to n increase

## Section: Simple Random Sampling without Replacement (SRSWOR) 🔴

### Core Idea
SRSWOR selects n **distinct** units — once selected, a unit is not placed back. This method is more efficient than SRSWR because it reduces the variance by a *finite population correction* factor. However, this advantage diminishes when the sampling fraction n/N is small (< 0.05).

> **In Simple Terms:** SRSWOR is like a lottery where each ticket can only be drawn once. Since you can never pick the same person twice, you get a better cross-section of the population, making your estimate more precise.

### Key Concepts

#### Finite Population Correction (FPC)
- V(m_srswor) = [(N–n)/(N–1)] × (σ²/n)
- The factor **(N–n)/(N–1)** is the FPC (also called finite population multiplier).
- FPC < 1 for n > 1 → V(m_srswor) < V(m_srswr) → SRSWOR is **more efficient**.
- When sampling fraction n/N < 0.05: FPC ≈ 1 → advantage of SRSWOR disappears.

### Key Formulae (SRSWOR)
| Quantity | Formula | Formula Number |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| Estimator of M | m_srswor = (1/n)Σyᵢ (unbiased) | 2.38 |
| Sampling Variance of m | V(m_srswor) = [(N–n)/(N–1)] × σ²/n | 2.39 |
| Comparison | V(m_srswor) < V(m_srswr) for n > 1 | 2.40 |
| Unbiased estimate of V(m) | v(m_srswor) = [(N–n)/N] × (1/n) × [ss]²/(n–1) | 2.41 |
| Estimator of Y | Y*_srswor = N × m_srswor (unbiased) | 2.42 |
| V(p) (sample proportion) | [(N–n)/(N–1)] × PQ/n | 2.47 |

### Definitions
- **SRSWOR**: Random sampling where each unit has equal probability of selection; selected units are NOT replaced — each unit appears at most once. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Finite Population Correction (FPC)**: The factor (N–n)/(N–1) applied to the variance formula in SRSWOR; accounts for the finite population size. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - SRSWOR: n distinct units; more efficient than SRSWR
> - FPC = (N–n)/(N–1); V(m_srswor) < V(m_srswr)
> - FPC ≈ 1 when n/N < 0.05 → SRSWOR advantage disappears
> - Both m_srswr and m_srswor are unbiased for M; but m_srswor is more efficient

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: SRSWOR is always better than SRSWR → ✅ Correct: Only when sampling fraction > 0.05; for very small fractions (n/N < 0.05), FPC ≈ 1, so the advantage is negligible.
- ❌ Mistake: V(Y*) = N × V(m) → ✅ Correct: V(Y*) = N² × V(m) (since Y* = Nm, not Nm).

## Section: Interpenetrating Sub-Samples (I-PSS) 🟡

### Core Idea
I-PSS involves splitting the sample into *h* independent sub-samples, each drawn by the same method, each providing a valid estimate of the population parameter. The key advantage of **independent** I-PSS: provides a simple, unbiased estimate of V(t) even when the sampling design or estimator is complex.

> **In Simple Terms:** I-PSS is like asking h different teams to independently survey the same city using the same method. Each team gives its own estimate; the spread among those estimates tells you how reliable the overall estimate is.

### Key Formulae (I-PSS)
| Quantity | Formula | Number |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Combined estimate of θ | t = (1/h)Σtᵢ (unbiased) | 2.50 |
| Unbiased estimate of V(t) | v(t) = [1/h(h–1)] Σ(tᵢ – t)² | 2.51 |
| Confidence interval probability | Pr[Min{t} < θ < Max{t}] = [1 – (1/2)^(h–1)] | 2.52 |

- Probability increases rapidly with h: 0.5 (h=2) → 0.875 (h=4)

### Definitions
- **Interpenetrating Sub-Samples (I-PSS)**: Two or more sub-samples drawn by the same method from the same population, each providing a valid estimate of the parameter. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: Systematic Sampling 🔴

### Core Idea
Select a random start *r* (from 1 to k, where k = [N/n]), then select every k-th unit. Only one random number is needed. Operationally very convenient. The sampling variance depends on how units are arranged in the population, which is both an advantage and a disadvantage.

> **In Simple Terms:** Systematic sampling is like checking every 10th student on the attendance register starting from a random initial position. Very easy to do — but if students are listed in height order and you want to estimate height, every 10th student might give biased results.

### Key Concepts

#### Systematic Sampling with Random Start
- Choose random start r (1 ≤ r ≤ k); k = [N/n] = sampling interval
- Select units: U_r, U_{r+k}, U_{r+2k}, ...
- If N = nk: all k systematic samples have size n; m_sys* = m (sample mean, unbiased for M)
- If N ≠ nk: sample sizes vary; m_sys* = (k/N)Σyᵢ ≠ m (bias in using m directly)

#### Circular Systematic Sampling (CSS) — Advantage
Overcomes the N ≠ nk problem:
- If r+jk > N, select unit U_{r+jk–N}
- All samples have equal size; m_r is unbiased for M.

#### Efficiency of Systematic Sampling
- V(m_sys) = σ_b² (between-sample variance)
- V(m_sys) = V(y) – σ_w² (total variance – within-sample variance)
- V(m_sys) ↓ when within-sample variance σ_w² ↑ (units within a sample are heterogeneous → arrange population to maximize heterogeneity within samples)

### Definitions
- **Systematic Sampling**: Selecting every k-th unit from the population after a random start r. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sampling Interval (k)**: The integer nearest to N/n; the gap between selected units.
- **Circular Systematic Sampling (CSS)**: Modification ensuring all samples have equal size n by wrapping around.

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- **Periodic/Cyclic trends**: Systematic sampling is NOT recommended when the population has periodic or cyclic variations, as the sample might consistently hit peaks or troughs. (Workaround: if the period is known, adjust accordingly.)
- **N not a multiple of n**: Causes unequal sample sizes and bias in using sample mean; overcome by CSS.
- **Unbiased estimate of V(m_sys)**: Not directly possible from a single systematic sample. (Workaround: use I-PSS technique.)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Systematic: random start r, then every k-th unit (k = [N/n])
> - Very operationally convenient; only 1 random number needed
> - CSS: wraps around to ensure equal sample size and unbiased m
> - NOT recommended for periodic populations
> - V(m_sys) ↓ when within-sample heterogeneity ↑

## Section: Sampling with Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) 🔴
<!-- Continues in Chunk 005 -->

### Core Idea
In PPS, the **probability of selecting a unit is proportional to its size** (a related auxiliary variable). If the size variable X is proportional to the study variable Y, then PPS is more efficient than SRSWR. Each selected unit's observation is weighted by 1/pᵢ to produce unbiased estimates.

> **In Simple Terms:** PPS sampling gives larger factories a higher chance of selection (since they contribute more to total employment). But we then weight their observations accordingly, so the overall estimate remains unbiased.

### Mechanisms / Processes

**For a Sample of Size 1 (pps):**
- Pᵢ = Xᵢ/X (probability of selecting unit i)
- Unbiased estimator of population total Y: Y*(1)_pps = y₁/p₁ (Formula 2.58)
- Unbiased estimator of M: m*(1)_pps = (1/N)(y₁/p₁) (Formula 2.59)
- V[Y*(1)_pps] = Σ(Yᵢ²/Pᵢ) – Y² (Formula 2.60)
- Variance is small when Pᵢ are proportional to Yᵢ (i.e., X linearly related to Y through origin)

**Operational Procedure (PPS with replacement):**
1. Cumulate the sizes X₁, X₂, ..., Xₙ → cumulative totals T₁, T₂, ..., Tₙ
2. Select a random number R between 1 and Tₙ = X (total)
3. Select unit Uᵢ if Tᵢ₋₁ < R ≤ Tᵢ → P(Uᵢ) = Xᵢ/X = Pᵢ
4. Repeat n times for a sample of size n (pps-wr)

## Section: Stratified Sampling 🔴

### Core Idea
The population is divided into **k strata** (groups) of N₁, N₂, ..., Nₖ units. A separate random sample is drawn from each stratum. Estimates from individual strata are combined with weights (Wₛ = Nₛ/N) to estimate the population mean. Stratification reduces variance if strata are internally **homogeneous** (minimising within-stratum variation).

> **In Simple Terms:** Stratified sampling is like dividing a mixed bag of apples (red, green, yellow) into separate piles, sampling each pile independently, and then combining results with appropriate weights. The result is more precise because you're not letting one colour crowd out the others.

### Key Concepts

#### Formation of Strata
- **Key rule**: Units within a stratum should be as **homogeneous** as possible.
- This minimises within-stratum variance → minimises V(m_st).
- Equivalently, inter-strata (between-strata) variance should be maximised.

#### Estimators from Stratified Sampling
| Quantity | Formula | Number |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Stratified estimator of M | m_st = Σ Wₛmₛ = (1/N)Σ Nₛmₛ | 2.66 |
| V(m_st) | Σ Wₛ² V(mₛ) | 2.67 |
| Covariance between strata | Cov(mₛ, mᵣ) = 0 for s≠r | 2.68 |
| Estimator of Y | Y*_st = Σ Y*ₛ | 2.69 |
| V(Y*_st) | Σ V(Y*ₛ) | 2.70 |

#### Allocation of Sample Size Among Strata

**Proportional Allocation**: Allocate sample to each stratum in proportion to stratum size.
```
nₛ = n × (Nₛ/N)
```
- Appropriate when no additional information about strata is available.

**Optimum Allocation (Neyman / budget-constrained)**:
With fixed budget F = F₀ + Σnₛ Fₛ, the optimum stratum sample size is:
```
nₛ = [(F – F₀) × Wₛ√(Vₛ/Fₛ)] / [Σ Wₛ√(VₛFₛ)]   (Formula 2.71)
```
Where Vₛ = within-stratum variance, Fₛ = per-unit cost in stratum s.

Minimum variance achieved with optimum nₛ:
```
Min V(m_st) = [Σ Wₛ√(VₛFₛ)]² / (F – F₀)   (Formula 2.72)
```

### Definitions
- **Stratified Sampling**: Dividing the population into strata and drawing independent random samples from each stratum. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Stratum**: A subgroup of the population; strata are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.
- **Proportional Allocation**: Allocating sample size to strata in proportion to their size (Nₛ).
- **Optimum Allocation**: Allocating sample to minimise V(m_st) subject to a budget constraint, proportional to Wₛ√(Vₛ/Fₛ).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Stratified: k strata; independent samples from each; combine with weights Wₛ = Nₛ/N
> - m_st = Σ Wₛmₛ (unbiased for M)
> - V(m_st) ↓ when within-stratum homogeneity ↑
> - Proportional allocation: nₛ ∝ Nₛ; Optimum: nₛ ∝ Wₛ√(Vₛ/Fₛ)

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Strata should be heterogeneous internally → ✅ Correct: Strata should be internally homogeneous to minimise V(m_st). Between-strata differences should be maximised.
- ❌ Mistake: Proportional allocation is always optimal → ✅ Correct: Optimum allocation considers both variance and cost per unit (Vₛ and Fₛ). Proportional is only optimal when all strata have equal variance and cost.

## Section: Cluster Sampling 🔴

### Core Idea
Instead of selecting individual units, cluster sampling selects **groups (clusters)** of units and surveys **all units** within the selected clusters. Clusters are groups of neighbouring or conveniently co-located units. While operationally convenient (especially when a list of individuals is unavailable), cluster sampling is **less efficient** than direct individual sampling because units within clusters tend to be homogeneous.

> **In Simple Terms:** Cluster sampling is like randomly picking a few city blocks and then surveying every household in those blocks, instead of randomly picking individual households from the entire city. It's easier (you avoid travelling all over), but less precise (households in the same block tend to be similar).

### Key Concepts

#### Estimates from Cluster Sampling (Clusters of Equal Size K)
- Population of NK units divided into N clusters of K units each.
- Sample: one cluster selected by SRS.
- Cluster mean m_c-srs is unbiased estimator of M.
- **V(m_c-srs) = σ_b²** (variance *between* clusters)

#### Total variance decomposition:
```
σ² = σ_w² + σ_b²  (within-cluster + between-cluster variance)
```

#### Efficiency of Cluster Sampling vs. SRSWR
```
E_c/srswr > 1  if  σ_w² > (K–1)σ_b²
```
- Cluster sampling more efficient than SRSWR only if within-cluster variance > (K–1) × between-cluster variance.
- **In practice**: clusters are usually internally homogeneous → σ_b² > σ_w² → cluster sampling is generally **less efficient** than SRSWR.

### Definitions
- **Cluster Sampling**: Random selection of groups (clusters) of units; all units within selected clusters are surveyed. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Within-cluster variance (σ_w²)**: Variance of units within the same cluster.
- **Between-cluster variance (σ_b²)**: Variance of cluster means around the overall mean = V(m_c-srs).

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Cluster sampling: sample clusters → survey all units in selected clusters
> - V(m_c-srs) = σ_b² (between-cluster variance)
> - Generally less efficient than SRSWR (within-cluster homogeneity → high σ_b²)
> - Operationally convenient when individual frame unavailable

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Clusters overlapping (non-mutually exclusive) are possible but add complexity.
- Cluster sampling can use SRSWR, SRSWOR, systematic, pps, or stratified methods for selecting clusters.

## Section: Multi-Stage Sampling 🟡

### Core Idea
Multi-stage sampling resolves the efficiency-convenience trade-off of cluster sampling by introducing additional selection stages. First, select clusters (primary stage units); then within each selected cluster, select a sub-sample of units (secondary stage units). This is more efficient than pure cluster sampling and more operationally convenient than pure individual sampling.

> **In Simple Terms:** Multi-stage is like breaking data collection into a relay race: first pick districts, then tehsils within those districts, then villages, then households. Each stage refines the selection and utilises available information.

### Examples

**Two-Stage Sampling**:
- Stage 1 (FSU = clusters): Select a random sample of clusters (primary stage units).
- Stage 2 (SSU = individuals): Select a random sub-sample of individual units from each selected cluster.
- Efficiency: Better than cluster sampling, worse than direct individual sampling.

**Multi-Stage Rural Household Survey**:
- Stage 1: Districts (FSU)
- Stage 2: Tehsils/Taluks
- Stage 3: Villages
- Stage 4: Households (USU — Ultimate Stage Units)

Multi-stage estimates are built up stage by stage, using the sampling method appropriate to each stage.

### Definitions
- **Multi-Stage Sampling**: Sampling in two or more stages, selecting sub-samples from already-selected units at each stage.
- **Primary Stage Units (PSU/FSU)**: Clusters selected at the first stage (e.g., districts, factories).
- **Ultimate Stage Units (USU)**: The final units surveyed (e.g., households, workers).

## Section: Choice of Appropriate Sampling Method 🔴

### Core Idea
The choice of sampling design depends on four key considerations: (1) a priori information about the population, (2) precision of estimates, (3) operational convenience, and (4) cost. A structured decision framework exists.

> **In Simple Terms:** Choosing a sampling method is like choosing transportation: if you're going a short distance with no luggage, walk (SRSWR/SRSWOR). If you have luggage and stops to make, take a bus (stratified). If roads are bad and you need to be efficient, charter a helicopter (pps). The right choice depends on your destination, budget, and what you know about the route.

### Key Concepts

#### Decision Framework: 7 Situations

| Situation | Recommended Method | Reason |
|-----------|-------------------|--------|
| 1. No a priori information | SRSWOR preferred over SRSWR | V(m_srswor) < V(m_srswr); only when sampling fraction not too small |
| 2. No prior info, operational ease needed | Systematic Sampling | Only 1 random number; but avoid periodic populations |
| 3. Auxiliary variable X related to Y available | PPS | V(Y*) small when Pᵢ ∝ Yᵢ; pps-wr practical for large surveys |
| 4. Sub-population estimates needed, or heterogeneous universe | Stratified Sampling | Sub-universe estimates; freedom to choose design per stratum |
| 5. Frame of clusters available (not of individuals) | Cluster Sampling | Operational convenience; less efficient but unavoidable |
| 6. Clusters at multiple levels available | Two-Stage / Multi-Stage | Balance efficiency and convenience |
| 7. Complex design with variance estimation needed | Add I-PSS to any design | Unbiased V(m) for any design; also monitors fieldwork quality |

#### Most Recommended Set of Designs
- **SRSWOR** (baseline)
- **Stratified sampling with SRSWOR**
- **PPS-WR** (when auxiliary variable available)
- **Stratified sampling with PPS-WR**
- **+ I-PSS** (where possible for variance estimation)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - No info → SRSWOR > SRSWR; avoid systematic if periodicity
> - Auxiliary X ~ Y → PPS (pps-wr in practice)
> - Sub-groups needed → Stratified
> - No individual frame → Cluster → then upgrade to multi-stage
> - Complex design → add I-PSS for variance estimation

## Section: Concept of Measurement 🔴

### Core Idea
Measurement is the process of assigning numbers (or symbols) to the characteristics of people, objects, or concepts according to defined rules. It bridges the conceptual (abstract) and the empirical (observable) levels of research — transforming theoretical constructs into measurable quantities. Different rules of assignment yield different scales (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio).

> **In Simple Terms:** Measuring something is like translating a book from one language to another. You're converting the real world (attributes of people or objects) into the language of numbers, following strict grammar rules. Get the grammar wrong, and the meaning is lost.

### Definitions
- **Measurement (Tyler, 1963)**: "Assignment of numerals according to rules." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Measurement (Nunally, 1970)**: "Consists of rules for assigning numbers to objects in such a way to represent quantities of attributes." (Emphasises both *rules* and *manner* of assignment.) ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Measurement (Campbell)**: "The assignment of numerals to objects or events according to rules." ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Measurement = assigning numbers to characteristics according to rules
> - All three definitions agree: numbers + rules + characteristics/objects
> - The specific *rules* determine the *type of scale* (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio)
> - Must-know: one-to-one correspondence between numbers and characteristics being measured

## Section: Measurement Issues in Research 🟡

### Core Idea
Researchers face several concerns when measuring concepts. Choosing the right scale, the right number of indicators, valid and reliable measures, and avoiding errors are all critical. As a general rule: aim for the **highest possible scale** (ratio > interval > ordinal > nominal) because it enables more powerful statistical analysis.

> **In Simple Terms:** Just as using a precise digital scale gives you more useful information than a rough visual estimate of weight, choosing a higher-level measurement scale gives you more analytical power.

### Key Concepts

#### Seven Measurement Issues
1. **Ordering vs. Categorising**: Does the concept allow rank ordering (ordinal) or only categorisation (nominal)?
2. **Discrete vs. Continuous**: Are the features of the concept discrete or continuous? Determines finest possible gradation.
3. **Number of indicators**: Simple concepts → one indicator; abstract concepts → multiple indicators. Researcher must decide how many indicators adequately capture the concept.
4. **Source of valid and reliable measures**: Considerable attention required to identify measures that are both valid and reliable for the study.
5. **Freedom from measurement errors**: Measurement should be free from invalidity error and unreliability.
6. **Measurement validity ≠ research validity**: Measurement validity (does it measure what it claims to?) is distinct from internal validity and external validity (which are research design issues).
7. **Proper use of available data**: Researcher must be aware of all available data sources and types.

## Section: Scales of Measurement 🔴

### Core Idea
Stanley S. Stevens (1946) classified scales of measurement into four types: Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, and Ratio. Each successive scale has all the properties of the previous one plus an additional property. The scale type determines which statistical operations are permissible.

> **In Simple Terms:** Scales of measurement are like measuring tools: a label (nominal) just names; a ruler with only rank marks (ordinal) shows who is bigger but not by how much; a proper ruler with equal intervals (interval) shows distance; a scale with a true zero (ratio) tells you both distance and the true absence of the thing.

### Key Concepts

#### Nominal Scale
- Numbers used only to **name, identify, or classify** (e.g., gender, industry type, student roll numbers).
- No order, no direction, no meaningful zero.
- Numbers assigned are arbitrary labels.
- Statistical operations: **Frequency, percentage, proportion, mode, coefficient of contingency, chi-square test**
- Mathematical operations (addition, subtraction, etc.) are **NOT possible**.

#### Ordinal Scale
- Numbers denote **rank order** — arranged from highest to lowest or vice versa.
- Distance between ranks is NOT equal (i.e., intervals are not uniform).
- No absolute zero point.
- Example: Students ranked 1st, 2nd, 3rd in academic achievement.
- Statistical operations: **Median, percentiles, rank correlation coefficient, range**

#### Interval Scale
- Has all properties of nominal + ordinal + **equal intervals**.
- Equal numerical distances represent equal differences in the attribute.
- **No absolute zero** (zero is arbitrary, not the absence of the attribute). E.g., temperature in °C.
- Coefficient of variation **cannot** be applied.
- Statistical operations: **Arithmetic mean, standard deviation, Karl Pearson's correlation, t-test, f-test**

#### Ratio Scale
- Has all properties of nominal + ordinal + interval + **absolute zero** (zero = total absence of the attribute).
- Most common in physical sciences (height, weight, distance, salary).
- All statistical operations possible, including **geometric mean, harmonic mean, coefficient of variation**.

### Summary Table: Properties of Scales

| Property | Nominal | Ordinal | Interval | Ratio |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|-------|
| Indicates Difference | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Indicates Direction | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Indicates Amount of Difference | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Absolute Zero | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |

### Appropriate Statistics by Scale

| Scale | Examples | Permitted Statistics |
|-------|---------|---------------------|
| Nominal | Gender, roll numbers, yes/no | Frequency, %, mode, chi-square |
| Ordinal | Rank in class, SES | Median, percentile, rank correlation |
| Interval | Student grades, temperature, rating scales | Mean, SD, correlation, t-test, f-test |
| Ratio | Height, weight, salary, income | All of above + geometric/harmonic mean, CV |

### Definitions
- **Nominal Scale**: A qualitative scale without order; numbers used only to classify or name. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ordinal Scale**: A qualitative scale with rank order; intervals between ranks are NOT equal. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Interval Scale**: Equal intervals on the scale represent equal differences in the attribute; no absolute zero. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ratio Scale**: Has all interval properties plus a conceptually meaningful absolute zero. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Nominal: name only; Ordinal: name + rank; Interval: name + rank + equal intervals; Ratio: all + absolute zero
> - Interval + Ratio = parametric data; Nominal + Ordinal = non-parametric data
> - Only Ratio scale has all 4 properties: difference, direction, amount of difference, absolute zero

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Interval scale has a true zero → ✅ Correct: Interval scale has an arbitrary zero (e.g., 0°C is not the absence of temperature). Only Ratio scale has an absolute zero.
- ❌ Mistake: You can apply CV to interval data → ✅ Correct: CV requires a meaningful zero; thus CV is applicable only to Ratio scale data.

## Section: Criteria for Good Measurement 🔴

### Core Idea
A measurement instrument must satisfy three criteria: **reliability** (consistency), **validity** (measuring what it claims to measure), and **practicality** (economical and interpretable). These three criteria are the standard for evaluating any measurement tool in research.

> **In Simple Terms:** A good weighing scale is: (1) reliable — gives the same reading each time you step on it; (2) valid — actually measures weight, not something else; and (3) practical — affordable, easy to use, and readable.

### Key Concepts

#### Reliability
- The degree to which the measurement/scale is **consistent or dependable**.
- If the same construct is used repeatedly, it yields the same conclusion.
- Reliability = consistency in drawing conclusions.

#### Validity
- The extent to which an instrument **measures what it intends to measure** — i.e., differences found reflect true differences.
- **Content Validity**: Does the measure cover all relevant aspects/issues under study?
- **Criterion Validity**: How well the measure relates to one or more external criteria based on empirical observation?
- **Construct Validity**: Explains variation observed across individuals; closely linked to factor analysis.

#### Reliability vs. Validity (illustration)
| Scenario | Reliable? | Valid? |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Same score each time, measuring the right thing | ✓ | ✓ |
| Same score each time, measuring the wrong thing | ✓ | ✗ |
| Different scores, sometimes right | ✗ | ✗ |

#### Practicality
- The measure must be **economical** (not too costly), **convenient** (easy to administer), and **interpretable** (results understandable).
- Should not be too lengthy or difficult.
- May require highly specialised personnel.

### Definitions
- **Reliability**: Degree of consistency or dependability of a measurement. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Validity**: Degree to which an instrument measures what it claims to measure. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Validity**: Coverage of all relevant aspects of the concept under study.
- **Criterion Validity**: Relationship of the measure to external empirical criteria.
- **Construct Validity**: Explanation of observed variation; linked to factor analysis.
- **Practicality**: The measure is economical, convenient, and interpretable.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 criteria: Reliability (consistency), Validity (measures what it claims), Practicality (economical + convenient)
> - 3 types of validity: Content, Criterion, Construct
> - Reliability without validity is possible (consistently wrong); validity implies reliability

## Section: Errors in Measurement 🟡

### Core Idea
Measurement should be precise and unambiguous to ensure research validity and reliability. Two main types of errors: **measurement invalidity** (measures the wrong thing) and **unreliability** (inconsistent results). Four sources of error exist: respondent, situation, measurer, and instrument.

> **In Simple Terms:** Errors in measurement are like GPS navigation errors. A GPS that always points north even when you're heading east is invalid (wrong direction). A GPS that gives you a different route every time you enter the same address is unreliable (inconsistent).

### Key Concepts

#### Types of Errors
- **Measurement Invalidity**: The measure incorrectly captures the concept — it is not measuring what it is supposed to.
- **Unreliability**: Inconsistency in what the measure produces under repeated use.

#### Four Sources of Error
| Source | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Respondent** | Respondent not expressing true (especially strong negative) feelings |
| **Situation** | Poor rapport between interviewer and respondent; environmental factors |
| **Measurer** | Incorrect coding, faulty tabulation, wrong statistical calculations, interviewer attitude/bias |
| **Instrument** | Defective measuring instrument (ambiguous questions, poor scale) |

### Definitions
- **Measurement Invalidity**: Error arising when the measure does not correctly capture the concept being measured.
- **Unreliability**: Error from inconsistent measurement results under repeated use.

## Section: Scaling Techniques — Comparative Scaling 🔴

### Core Idea
**Scaling** is the process of locating measured objects on a continuum. All scaling techniques are classified into two categories: **Comparative** (objects compared directly with each other → ordinal/rank data) and **Non-Comparative** (objects evaluated independently → interval data). Comparative techniques produce *relative* measures.

> **In Simple Terms:** Comparative scaling is asking "Do you prefer A or B?" — you evaluate by direct comparison. Non-comparative scaling is asking "How much do you like A on a scale of 1–5?" — you evaluate independently.

### Key Concepts

#### Paired Comparison
- The most commonly used comparative scaling technique.
- Binary choice: respondent picks one stimulus from each pair (greater magnitude on a specified dimension).
- Data: **ordinal** in nature.
- Use case: when researcher needs to distinguish between exactly **two objects**.
- With n objects, total comparisons = **n(n–1)/2**.
- Example: "Which milk product do you prefer — Amul or Mother Dairy?" (asked on taste, packaging, price).

#### Rank Order
- Respondents rank *all* objects in the list simultaneously from most to least preferred.
- With n objects, only **(n–1)** decisions are required.
- Data: **ordinal**.
- Less time-consuming than paired comparison; easy to understand.
- Example: Rank 10 mobile phone brands by preference (Nokia, Samsung, Sony, etc.)

#### Constant Sum
- Respondents allocate a fixed total (usually 100 points) among objects based on pre-defined criteria.
- If an object is unimportant → allocate 0; most important → allocate maximum.
- Data: considered **ordinal** (some treat as ratio-like, but technically ordinal).
- Advantages: fine discrimination, fast.
- Limitations: confusing with many objects; not suitable for illiterate respondents or children.
- Example: Allocate 100 points across movie criteria (Content = 20, Music = 20, Sound = 15, Story = 30, Presentation = 15).

### Summary: Comparative Scaling Techniques

| Technique | Number of Decisions | Data Type | Best For |
|-----------|--------------------|-----------|---------| 
| Paired Comparison | n(n–1)/2 | Ordinal | Comparing 2 objects exactly |
| Rank Order | (n–1) | Ordinal | Ranking all objects — less time |
| Constant Sum | 1 allocation task | Ordinal | Fine-grained relative importance |

### Definitions
- **Scaling**: The process of generating a continuum upon which measured objects are located. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Comparative Scaling**: Scaling technique involving direct comparison of objects with each other; data is ordinal. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Paired Comparison**: Respondent selects one object from each pair; n(n–1)/2 comparisons for n objects. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Rank Order**: Respondents rank all objects simultaneously; (n–1) decisions for n objects.
- **Constant Sum**: Respondents allocate a fixed total (e.g., 100 points) among objects.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Comparative: direct comparison; ordinal data
> - 3 types: Paired Comparison [n(n–1)/2 decisions] → Rank Order [(n–1) decisions] → Constant Sum [1 allocation task]
> - Use paired comparison: 2 objects; rank order: all objects ranked; constant sum: importance weights

## Section: Non-Comparative Scaling Techniques 🔴
<!-- See Chunk 006 for Comparative Scaling -->

### Core Idea
Non-comparative scaling evaluates each object **independently** against no specific reference standard. Respondents rate one object at a time. Data generated is usually **interval scale** (sometimes numeric/metric). This approach is more widely used in social science research than comparative scaling.

> **In Simple Terms:** Non-comparative scaling is like asking a student to rate their own performance out of 10 — independently, without comparing themselves to classmates. Comparative scaling is asking: "Are you better or worse than classmate B?"

### Key Concepts

#### i) Continuous Rating Scale (Graphic Rating Scale)
- Respondents mark their rating by placing a mark at the appropriate position on a **continuous line**.
- Line labeled at both ends: one extreme criterion to the other (e.g., "Most" to "Least").
- The line may contain points 0, 10, 20, ..., 100.
- Data can be treated as **numeric/interval**.
- A large number of ratings are possible if respondents are literate.
- Example: Rate a magazine's content coverage by marking on a line from "Most" to "Least".

#### ii) Itemised Rating Scale
Respondents select from a set of **categories with descriptive labels**. Sub-types:

**a) Likert Scale (Summated Rating Scale)** — proposed by Likert (1932)
- A series of statements expressing either favourable or unfavourable attitude towards an object.
- Respondents indicate agreement on a **5-point scale**: Strongly Agree (5), Agree (4), Undecided (3), Disagree (2), Strongly Disagree (1).
- Also called "summated scale" — individual item scores are added (summed) for a total score.
- Data: **Interval scale**.

**Advantages of Likert Scale:**
1. Relatively easy to construct (no panel of judges needed)
2. More reliable — respondents answer every statement
3. Can be used in both respondent-centred and stimulus-centred studies
4. Takes less time to construct

**Limitations of Likert Scale:**
1. Only shows direction of attitude (favourable/unfavourable) — not the degree of difference
2. Does not rise above ordinal scale structure
3. A given total score can arise from different answer patterns (ambiguity)

**b) Semantic Differential Scale** — developed by Osgood, Suci & Tannenbaum (1957)
- **7-point scale** (vs. Likert's 5-point) with bipolar adjective pairs at each end (e.g., "Easy to read ↔ Hard to read").
- Scored as –3 to +3 or 1 to 7.
- Based on proposition that an object can have several implied/suggestive meanings.
- Results further analysed by **factor analysis**.
- Data: Can be treated as **numeric/interval** in some cases.

**Advantages of Semantic Differential Scale:**
1. Efficient and easy way to gather attitude from large samples
2. Comprehensive picture of meaning (both direction and total set of responses)
3. Standardised; can be easily repeated
4. Avoids many response distortion problems of direct methods

**c) Stapel Scale** — developed by John Stapel
- Single criterion in the centre; **10 categories numbered –5 to +5** (no neutral zero).
- Scale presented **vertically**.
- Positive rating = criterion accurately describes the object; Negative = inaccurately describes.
- Data: **Interval data**. Can be collected via telephonic interview.
- Analysed same way as Semantic Differential Scale.
- Example: Rate a departmental store on "Services" and "Products" using –5 to +5.

#### iii) Category Scale
- Objects grouped into a **predetermined number of categories** based on perceived strength along a dimension.
- A **dichotomous scale** (typically "Yes/No").
- Useful for **socio-demographic questions**.
- Data: **Nominal or Ordinal**.
- Simple and quick; many options can be included.
- Types: Single Category (binary: Yes/No) and Multiple Category (income brackets, age groups).
- Example: "Do you own a house? Yes / No"

#### iv) Cumulative Scale (Guttman Scale / Scalogram Analysis) — Guttman
- Series of statements in **cumulative sequence** — if you agree to item 3, you necessarily agree to items 1 and 2.
- A person's total score allows you to reproduce their responses to individual items.
- Example (Bogardus Social Distance Scale):
  1. Willing to permit immigrants to live in your country?
  2. Willing to permit immigrants to live in your community?
  3. Willing to permit in your neighbourhood?
  4. Willing to permit next door?
  5. Would you permit your child to marry an immigrant?

**Advantages of Guttman Scale:**
1. Assures only a **single dimension** of attitude is measured (unidimensionality)
2. No subjective judgment by researcher in scale development

**Limitations of Guttman Scale:**
1. Perfectly cumulative (unidimensional) scales are **very rarely found** in practice — approximations are used.
2. Developmental procedure is **cumbersome** compared to other methods.

### Summary: Non-Comparative Scaling Techniques

| Technique | Type | Scale of Data | Key Feature |
|-----------|------|--------------|-------------|
| Continuous Rating Scale | Non-comparative | Interval/Numeric | Mark on a continuous line |
| Likert Scale | Itemised | Interval | 5-point agree-disagree; summated |
| Semantic Differential | Itemised | Interval/Numeric | 7-point bipolar adjectives; factor analysis |
| Stapel Scale | Itemised | Interval | –5 to +5; single criterion; no zero |
| Category Scale | Itemised | Nominal/Ordinal | Dichotomous or multi-category |
| Guttman Scale | Cumulative | Ordinal | Cumulative hierarchy; unidimensional |

### Definitions
- **Non-comparative Scaling**: Scaling technique where each object is evaluated independently against no specific standard; usually produces interval data. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Likert Scale**: Summated rating scale using 5-point agree-disagree responses across multiple statements. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Semantic Differential Scale**: 7-point scale with bipolar adjective pairs; developed by Osgood, Suci & Tannenbaum (1957). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Stapel Scale**: Rating scale with 10 categories (–5 to +5) centred on a single criterion; no neutral point. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Guttman Scale (Scalogram)**: Cumulative scale where agreeing to higher items implies agreement with all lower items; measures a single attitude dimension. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Category Scale**: Dichotomous or multi-category scale for grouping responses by perceived strength. Used for socio-demographic questions.
- **Continuous Rating Scale**: Respondents mark position on a continuous line from one extreme to another.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Non-comparative: each object rated independently; usually interval data
> - Likert = 5-point, summated, interval
> - Semantic Differential = 7-point, bipolar adjectives, factor-analysed, interval
> - Stapel = –5 to +5, no zero, single criterion, interval
> - Category = Yes/No or multi-category, nominal/ordinal
> - Guttman = cumulative hierarchy, ordinal, unidimensional (but hard to achieve in practice)

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Likert scale generates ratio data → ✅ Correct: Likert generates interval (at best) or ordinal data; many argue it only achieves ordinal structure since total scores can arise from different patterns.
- ❌ Mistake: Semantic Differential and Stapel are the same → ✅ Correct: Semantic Differential uses bipolar adjective *pairs* on 7 points (–3 to +3); Stapel uses a *single* criterion rated on 10 points (–5 to +5) with no neutral zero.
- ❌ Mistake: Guttman scale is easy and commonly used → ✅ Correct: Perfectly unidimensional (cumulative) scales are very rare; the method is cumbersome to develop.
