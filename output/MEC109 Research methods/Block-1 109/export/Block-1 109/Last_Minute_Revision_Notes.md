### Research Methodology and its Constituents 🔴

**Definition of Research**

**Three Constituents of Research Methodology**
1. **Theoretical perspectives / orientation** — the philosophical lens guiding research
2. **Tools and techniques of data collection** — how data is gathered
3. **Methods of data analysis** — how data is interpreted

**Research Methods vs Methodology vs Tools**
| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| **Research Methodology** | Broader term; includes philosophy + methods + tools |
| **Research Methods** | Techniques and tools for data collection and analysis |
| **Research Techniques** | Practical aspects of data collection and organisation |
| **Research Tools** | Instruments: questionnaires, schedules, diaries, maps, photos |
- **Research**: A scientific and systematic enquiry to discover new facts or verify old facts, their sequences, interrelationships and causal explanations
- **Research Methodology**: Consists of theoretical perspectives, tools/techniques of data collection, and methods of data analysis
- Quantitative data: collected via census, surveys, structured questionnaires
- Qualitative data: via participant observation, semi-structured interviews, life histories, experiments, pilot studies

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing "methodology" with "methods" — methodology is the broader philosophy; methods are the specific tools
- Assuming research = just data collection. Data collection is only one part of the research process.
- Theoretical Perspectives → leads to Unit 2 (Positivism) and Unit 5 (Interpretivism, Critical Theory)

### Approaches to Social Enquiry 🔴

**Quantitative vs Qualitative Approach**
| Characteristic | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| Typical data collection | Participant obs., semi-structured interviews, focus groups | Laboratory, questionnaire, structured interviews |
| Questions | Open-ended, two-way exchange | Closed, prepared answer categories |
| Respondent selection | Information maximization; each may be unique | Representativeness; random sampling |
| Timing of analysis | Parallel with data collection | After data collection |
| Analysis methods | Descriptive; mixed methods | Standard statistical methods |
| Role of theory | Used as departure; new theory emerges | A priori theories tested deductively |
| Associated paradigm | Critical theory, Interpretivism | Positivism, Post-positivism |

**Sub-types of Quantitative Approach**
1. **Inferential**: Database via survey; inferences about variable relationships
2. **Experimental**: Controlled environment; variables manipulated to observe effects
3. **Simulation**: Numerical model of a dynamic process; artificial environment with relevant data generation

**Mixed Methods**
- Combines at least one quantitative and one qualitative method
- Used to strengthen validity of research findings
- **Quantitative Research**: Purposes and procedures that are deductive, objective and generalized; follows well-controlled procedures with large number of cases
- **Qualitative Research**: Purposes and procedures integrating inductive, subjective and contextual approaches; evolves meaning through close researcher-participant contact
- **Mixed Methods**: Combining quantitative and qualitative methods in measurement, collection or analysis of data

### Common Mistakes
- Assuming qualitative = less rigorous. Qualitative research has its own rigorous standards
- Equating Quantitative with Positivism exclusively — post-positivism also uses quantitative methods

### Research Strategies 🔴

**Four Research Strategies**
| Paradigm | Strategy | Starting Point |
|---|---|---|
| Positivism | **Induction** | Data → Generalisation → Explanation |
| Post-positivism / Realism | **Deduction** | Theory → Test via data collection |
| Critical Realism | **Retroduction** | Hypothetical mechanism → Explain phenomenon |
| Interpretivism | **Abduction** | Meanings in social accounts → Research problem |

**Detailed Descriptions**
1. **Inductive strategy**: Begins with data collection; generalisation is made as an elementary explanation
2. **Deductive strategy**: Starts with a theory providing a possible answer; tests theory via relevant data
3. **Retroductive strategy**: Starts with a hypothetical model of a mechanism that could explain a phenomenon under investigation
4. **Abductive strategy**: Starts with the concepts and meanings in social actors' accounts of activities related to the research problem
- **Induction**: Moving from particular observations to general laws
- **Deduction**: Deriving conclusions from general principles to specific instances
- **Retroduction**: Hypothetical modelling of underlying mechanisms to explain observations
- **Abduction**: Interpreting the meanings social actors assign to their activities

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing Retroduction with Abduction: Retroduction = hypothetical mechanism; Abduction = social meanings and interpretations
| Strategy | Starts with |
|---|---|
| Induction | Observations/Data |
| Deduction | Theory |
| Retroduction | Hypothetical mechanism |
| Abduction | Social meanings/accounts |

### Research Process 🔴

**The 7 Steps of the Research Process**
1. **Defining the Research Problem**
2. **Review of Literature**
3. **Formulation of Hypothesis** (Objectives + Research Questions)
4. **Research Design**
5. **Collection of Data**
6. **Analysis of Data**
7. **Report Writing**

**Step 1: Defining the Research Problem**
- This is the **foremost step** — the problem must be defined categorically
- Start broad, narrow down operationally
- Key elements to state clearly:
  - Rationale behind the problem
  - Aims and objectives (determines data, hypothesis, techniques)
  - Research questions grounded in theory
  - Ideally revisit and rephrase after literature review
- Technical terms must be explicitly defined; basic assumptions stated

**Step 2: Review of Literature**
- Two types: **(i) Conceptual/Theoretical literature** and **(ii) Empirical literature** (quantitative findings)
- Sources: journals, conference proceedings, government reports, books, internet

**Step 3: Formulation of Hypothesis**
- A **tentative statement** that needs testing for logical and empirical confirmation
- Stated as a proposition about cause-effect or relationship between variables
- Must be **testable** (capable of being accepted or rejected)
- Helps focus research and identify the method of analysis
- Exploratory or descriptive research can proceed **without a hypothesis**

**Step 4: Research Design**
- Logical conceptual structure within which research is conducted
- Blueprint for collection, measurement and analysis of data

**Step 5: Collection of Data**
- Primary Data: collected by researcher (survey, observation, interview, experiment)
- Secondary Data: already published data from agencies (CSO, NSSO, RBI, IMF)

**Step 6: Analysis of Data**
- **Uni-dimensional**: Measures of central tendency, dispersion, skewness, ANOVA, time series
- **Bivariate**: Simple regression, correlation, association of attributes
- **Multivariate**: Multiple regression, factor analysis, cluster analysis, canonical analysis

**Step 7: Report Writing**
- Two components: **Preliminary pages** + **Main text**
- Preliminary pages: Title, researcher name, institution, month/year, preface with key findings
- Main text: Introduction (objectives, rationale, literature summary, hypotheses, methodology, limitations) → Subsequent chapters → Conclusions
- An **Executive Summary** is placed before introductory chapter
- **Research Process**: Different steps involved in a desired sequence in carrying out research

### Common Mistakes
- Treating the process as strictly linear — steps overlap and are not mutually exclusive
- Neglecting the literature review — this step can refine the problem statement itself

### Hypothesis — Types and Sources 🔴

**Definition of Hypothesis**

**Two Methods of Hypothesis Generation**
| Method | Flow | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | Theory → Hypothesis → Observation → Confirmation | Starts from theory |
| **Inductive** | Observation → Pattern → Tentative Hypothesis → Theory | Starts from observation |

**Research Hypothesis (vs. General Hypothesis)**
- A **Research Hypothesis** is a **predictive statement** relating an independent variable to a dependent variable
- The relationship must be testable/objectively verifiable — otherwise it does not qualify as a research hypothesis

**Null and Alternative Hypothesis (implicit)**
- Hypotheses tested in quantitative research establish **statistical significance** of findings
- Qualitative research: hypothesis may be about social settings having certain features, confirmed or falsified via observation
- **Hypothesis**: A tentative statement about a phenomenon or cause-effect relationship, formulated to be tested
- **Research Hypothesis**: A predictive statement relating an independent variable to a dependent variable, meant to be tested objectively

### Common Mistakes
- Stating a hypothesis that is not testable — it must be verifiable/falsifiable
- Assuming all research needs a hypothesis — exploratory and descriptive research can proceed without one

### Measurement Scales of Variables 🔴

**Four Measurement Scales**
| Scale | Description | Statistical Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | Categorical, no order or distance (e.g., yes/no, gender) | Mode, chi-square, cross-tabulation, logistic regression, factor analysis |
| **Ordinal** | Ranked/ordered, but no measurable distance between ranks (e.g., preference ranking: 1st, 2nd, 3rd) | Median, mode, rank order correlation, non-parametric methods |
| **Interval** | Ordered with measurable distances, but **no true zero** (e.g., temperature in °C, satisfaction scales 1–7) | Mean, SD, correlation, regression, ANOVA, factor analysis |
| **Ratio** | All properties of interval scale + **true zero** exists; ratios are meaningful (e.g., income, height, weight) | All interval scale techniques; additionally ratios (X1/X2) meaningful |

**Properties Summary**
| Property | Nominal | Ordinal | Interval | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Named categories | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Ordered | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Equal intervals | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |
| True zero | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
- **Measurement**: Process of observing and recording collected observations
- **Nominal Scale**: Data recorded into unordered categories (yes/no, religion, gender)
- **Ordinal Scale**: Ranked data where order matters but distance between ranks is unknown
- **Interval Scale**: Ordered data with equal, measurable intervals but no absolute zero
- **Ratio Scale**: Highest level of measurement with true zero; both distance and ratio comparisons are meaningful

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing Interval and Ratio: Temperature in °C = Interval (0°C is not "no temperature"); Income = Ratio (₹0 means no income)
- Applying parametric tests (ANOVA, regression) to nominal or ordinal data without transformation

### Positivist Philosophy of Science 🔴

**Central Tenets of Positivism**
| Tenet | Description |
|---|---|
| **Scientism** | Science is qualitatively superior to all other areas of human endeavour |
| **Methodologism** | Science's superiority comes from its possession of *a method* |
| **Methodological Monism** | One method is common to all sciences regardless of subject matter |
| **Inductivitism** | Scientific knowledge is generated by arriving at generalisations from pure observations using the principle of induction |
| **Pure Observation** | Observations are theory-neutral / theory-independent — they are the unbiased foundation of knowledge |
| **Value Neutrality** | Science is objective and value-free |
| **Verifiability** | Scientific statements are systematically verifiable (the hallmark/criterion of science) |
| **Deductive-Nomologism** | All scientific explanations must follow the Deductive-Nomological (D-N) pattern |
| **Progress** | Science is cumulatively progressive — new theories build on and add to old theories ('old theory + epsilon') |
| **Objectivity** | Science is objective because it is based on pure, theory-neutral observations |
| **Rationality** | Science is rational because the principle of induction can be rationally justified |

**Baconian vs Cartesian Models**
- **Baconian (Inductive)**: Start from pure observations → generalise → law
- **Cartesian (Deductive/Hypothetico-Deductive)**: Start from hypothesis → deduce consequences → test
- **Positivism**: A movement in the philosophy of science insisting that knowledge must be based on empirically verifiable, theory-neutral observations
- **Inductivism**: Scientific knowledge is generated by arriving at generalisations from pure observations using the principle of induction
- **Methodological Monism**: The view that there is only one method common to all sciences
- **Scientism**: The belief that science is qualitatively superior to all other areas of human endeavour
- **Metaphysics**: Statements about unobservable entities, not verifiable — rejected by positivists as meaningless

### Common Mistakes
- Assuming Positivism = all science. It is a philosophical *position* about how science should work
- Confusing Positivism with Post-Positivism. Positivism is the original strict view; Post-Positivism includes Popper who critiqued it heavily

### Attack on the Positivist Philosophy of Science 🔴

**Grounds for Rejecting Pure Observation**
1. **Theory precedes observation**: What we look for in an experiment is guided by existing theory
2. **Observation is theory-dependent**: The instruments used, the framing of questions, and the interpretation of results all depend on prior theoretical commitments
3. **Observation statements are theory-laden**: Even a simple statement like "this is red" requires concepts (colour, vision, categories) that come from existing knowledge
- If pure observation is impossible, the foundation of the inductive model collapses
- This opened the door to Popper's Falsificationism and Kuhn's Paradigm theory

### Karl Popper's Philosophy of Science (Part 1 — Falsifiability & H-D Model) 🔴

**The Problem of Demarcation**

**Why Verifiability Fails (Positivist Criterion)**
- You can verify a million instances of "all swans are white," but one black swan disproves it
- Verification can never be complete; it suffers from the **problem of induction**
- Popper's alternative: we can never confirm a theory but we can falsify it

**Falsifiability vs Verifiability**
| | Positivism | Popper |
|---|---|---|
| Criterion | **Verifiability** | **Falsifiability** |
| Method | Induction | Hypothetico-Deduction |
| Progress | Cumulative (old + epsilon) | Discontinuous (new theory replaces old) |
| Observation | Pure, theory-neutral | Theory-dependent |
| Science | Objective via neutral observation | Objective via inter-subjective testability |

**The Hypothetico-Deductive (H-D) Model**
1. **Problem recognition** — observing an anomaly or gap
2. **Tentative solution** — proposing a hypothesis/theory (a conjecture)
3. **Deductive test** — deriving testable consequences and subjecting them to empirical test
4. **Elimination/Corroboration** — if falsified, discard; if it survives, it is *corroborated* (but never *confirmed*)

**Key Theses of Popper (Shared and Differing from Positivism)**
- Pure observation → Observations are **theory-dependent**
- Inductive derivation → Theories are **pure inventions of human mind** (conjectures)
- One-way observation-theory relationship → they are **interdependent**
- Value neutrality → Science is **not value-neutral**; but values can be critically discussed
- Verifiability → **Falsifiability** is the hallmark of science
- **Falsifiability**: The property of a scientific statement whereby it can be shown to be false by an empirical observation or experiment; Popper's demarcation criterion
- **Corroboration**: A theory that survives falsification attempts is corroborated (tentatively accepted) but never confirmed as absolutely true
- **Verisimilitude**: "Truth-nearness" — successive scientific theories exhibit increasing approximation to reality
- **Hypothetico-Deductive Model**: The scientific method proposed by Popper: conjecture → deduce consequences → attempt falsification → corroborate or reject

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing *corroboration* with *confirmation* — for Popper, a theory is corroborated (survived tests), never *confirmed* as permanently true
- Thinking Popper is anti-science — he is *pro-science* but against the *inductive* interpretation of science

### Thomas Kuhn's Philosophy of Science 🔴

**The Two Stages in the Life of Every Major Science**
1. **Pre-paradigmatic stage**: Multiple competing schools; no consensus; like art, philosophy, medicine today
2. **Paradigmatic stage**: One dominant paradigm; consensus on fundamentals; "mature science"

**What is a Paradigm?**
1. Specifies the **ultimate constituents** of the sphere of reality science is investigating
2. Identifies the **model problems**
3. Specifies the **range of possible solutions**
4. Provides **strategies and techniques** for solving problems
5. Provides **exemplary examples** of how to solve certain problems

**Normal Science**
- **Tradition-bound**: accepts paradigm; does not question it
- **Convergent thinking**: not radical; builds precision and extends existing theory
- **Puzzle-solving**: solves specific puzzles within the paradigm's framework
- **Goals**: (1) Increase precision of existing theory, (2) Extend theory to new areas
- Education in normal science: dogmatic initiation into pre-established tradition

**Anomalies and Crisis**
- **Anomaly**: A puzzle that resists resolution within the paradigm
- One or two anomalies ≠ crisis; a **large accumulation of major anomalies** → crisis
- No clear/objective criterion to define how many anomalies = crisis → decided by **community judgment/peer review**
- Crisis-ridden paradigm is not abandoned until a new one is ready

**Scientific Revolution**
1. Crisis deepens
2. Search for alternative paradigm begins
3. Radical scientific debates emerge
4. New paradigm is chosen (based on value judgments of scientific community, not purely logic/experiment)
5. Old paradigm is replaced by new paradigm

**Paradigm Choice — Not Purely Logical**
- Evaluation procedures of normal science are useless during paradigm choice (they depend on the paradigm under dispute)
- The choice is determined by **consensus of the relevant scientific community**
- The ultimate explanation of theory choice is **sociological, not methodological**
- Kuhn: "Scientific communities must first be recognised as having an independent existence"

**Incommensurability of Successive Paradigms**
- Two successive paradigms **cut the world differently** and speak **different languages**
- Even shared terms have different meanings across paradigms
- Cannot translate from one paradigm's language to another
- Therefore successive paradigms are **incommensurable** — cannot be directly compared
- Kuhn compares paradigm shift to a **gestalt shift** (sudden perceptual reorganisation)
- This implies: **truth is intra-paradigmatic** (relative to a paradigm), not inter-paradigmatic absolute truth
- Kuhn's view appears to be a form of **relativism**
- **Paradigm**: A disciplinary matrix comprising the ultimate view of reality, model problems, range of solutions, strategies, and techniques, shared by a scientific community
- **Normal Science**: Tradition-bound, puzzle-solving research within an accepted paradigm; does not question the paradigm
- **Anomaly**: A puzzle that resists resolution within the current paradigm's framework
- **Scientific Revolution**: The process of replacing a crisis-ridden paradigm with a new one
- **Incommensurability**: The state of two paradigms being so different that they cannot be meaningfully compared

### Common Mistakes
- Thinking paradigm shift = gradual reform. It is a **sudden, radical replacement** of the entire framework
- Confusing anomaly with crisis — anomalies are normal; only an **accumulation of major anomalies** triggers crisis

### Popper Versus Kuhn 🔴
| Dimension | Popper | Kuhn |
|---|---|---|
| **Essence of science** | **Divergent thinking** and fundamental disagreements; critical thinking challenges everything | **Convergent thinking** and consensus; tradition-bound paradigm guides practice |
| **Locus of scientific activity** | The **individual scientist** — science is the sum total of individual scientists working by method | The **scientific community** — community enforces consensus; institutions (e.g., peer review) handle disputes |
| **Explanation of theory change** | Via **methodological norms** followed by all scientists; rational | Via **value judgments of the community**; sociological (Kuhn) calls Popper's method rational; Popper calls Kuhn an **irrationalist** |
- Both (like Positivists) believe science is **unique** among human endeavours
- But they differ on *what* makes it unique:
  - **Positivists**: Systematic *verifiability*
  - **Popper**: Systematic *falsifiability*
  - **Kuhn**: *Consensus* enforced by a scientific community
- Kuhn, like Positivists and Popper, does NOT question whether science is really unique — he only provides an **alternative account** of how it acquired that status
- In this sense, Kuhn's position is "conservative"
| School | Hallmark of Science |
|---|---|
| Positivists | Systematic **verifiability** |
| Popper | Systematic **falsifiability** |
| Kuhn | **Consensus** of the scientific community |
- **Positivist recommendation**: Collect data, extract generalisation, verify, formulate law
- **Popperian recommendation**: Formulate problem, propose tentative solution, try to falsify; if survived = corroborated theory
- **Kuhnian recommendation**: Arrive at paradigms in social sciences to overcome ideological differences

### Unified View of Rules of Positivism (Kolakowski) 🔴

**Why Positivism Still Dominates (Despite Critique)**
1. Its propositions are **rigorous and prescriptive** — simple and attractive
2. Alternative approaches are **open-ended and speculative**
3. Many economists still treat Economics as a "Positive Science"

**Kolakowski's Four Rules of Positivism**
| Rule | Description |
|---|---|
| **K1 — Phenomenalism** | Knowledge is based only on phenomena (sensory experience). Facts and objective facts alone form the basis of knowledge. It is existence, not essence; facts not noumena (things-in-themselves). |
| **K2 — Nominalism (Nouminalism)** | Any insight formulated in general terms has no real reference other than individual facts. Abstract science only abridges experience; no independent knowledge beyond what is observed. |
| **K3 — Value-free Statements** | Knowledge is value-free. Value judgements and normative statements cannot be called knowledge. Science has no room for ethical judgements ("good/bad"). |
| **K4 — Unity of Science** | There is only one method of scientific knowledge. All laws are ultimately derived from a single fundamental form. |
- **Phenomenalism (K1)**: Knowledge is based on what appears in experience — phenomena, not noumena
- **Nominalism/Nouminalism (K2)**: General terms have no real reference beyond individual observable facts
- **Noumena**: Kant's term for "things as they are in themselves" — beyond sensory experience; rejected by Positivists
| Stage | Criterion | Proponent | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Positivism | **Verification** — complete verification by observational evidence required | Logical Positivists | Too strict; problem of induction |
| Popperian | **Falsification** — a statement is scientific if falsifiable | Karl Popper | Too strict; few theories meet the standard perfectly |
| Later Positivism | **Confirmation** — repeated positive tests increase degree of confirmation; theories ranked by relative degree of confirmation | Rudolf Carnap | More flexible but still fact/testing-based |

### Rules of Logic / Correct Reasoning 🔴

**3.4.1 Categorical Syllogism**
- **Example** (valid): All As are B; C is an A → Therefore, C is B
- **Example** (real): All Politicians are corrupt; Blogg is a politician → Therefore, Blogg is corrupt *(logically valid, may not be factually true)*
- **Fallacy of Enthymeme**: A conclusion without stated premises ("Blogg is a politician, therefore corrupt" — missing major premise)

**3.4.2 Hypothetical Syllogism (Modus Ponens)**
- Takes the form: "If A is true, then B is true; A is true → Therefore, B is true"
- Also called **affirming the antecedent**

**3.4.3 Logical Truth vs Material Truth**
- A statement can be **logically valid** but **factually false**
- Example: "All politicians have two tongues; Blogg is a politician → Blogg has two tongues" — logically true, materially false

**3.4.4 Deductive Fallacies**
| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical (Formal) Fallacy** — Affirming the Consequent | Incorrect: If A then B; B is true → Therefore A is true (wrong!) | Should be Modus Ponens, not affirming consequent |
| **Verbal Fallacy** — Fallacy of Composition | What is true of a part is made true of the whole | "If one person stands on tiptoe, they see better → If all stand on tiptoe, all see better" |
| **Material Fallacy** | Various others: | |
| — *Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc* | Because B follows A, B is caused by A | |
| — *Argument by Analogy* | A is similar to B; what's true of A must be true of B | "India = Singapore → open economy will work the same way" |
| — *Appeal to Authority* | Truth asserted by citing an authority | "India is shining because Milton Friedman said so" |
- **Categorical Syllogism**: Two categorical premises → necessary conclusion
- **Hypothetical Syllogism (Modus Ponens / 'Affirming the Antecedent')**: If-then form; affirms antecedent to conclude consequent
- **Logical Truth**: A statement that is true by the rules of logic/formal validity
- **Material Truth**: A statement that is factually true about the world
- **Enthymeme**: A fallacy where a conclusion is drawn without stating the premises

### Positivist Models of Scientific Explanation 🔴
- Propositions in a deductive system arranged in **levels**:
  - **Higher-level hypotheses**: Refer to theoretical entities; need not all be directly tested
  - **Lower-level hypotheses**: Describe observable phenomena; tested against reality
- Theories as a whole are tested by comparing their **deduced consequences (predictions)** with data
- Not every theoretical term needs a direct empirical counterpart — indirect confirmation through the theory suffices
- Defuses the **Realist vs Instrumentalist controversy**:
  - Realists say: All theoretical terms must refer to real entities
  - Instrumentalists say: Theories are only instruments for prediction
  - H-D accommodates both without resolving the debate philosophically
1. Allow **generality** in specification of scientific laws
2. Possess **formal simplicity** allowing elegant mathematical machinery
3. Allow discovery of **interdependencies among observables**
4. Serve as **heuristic devices** with explanatory function

**3.5.2.1 Deductive-Nomological (D-N) Model**
| Condition | Description |
|---|---|
| **R1** (Logical) | Explanandum must be a logical consequence of the Explanans |
| **R2** (Logical) | Explanans must contain at least one general law actually required for deriving explanandum |
| **R3** (Logical) | Explanans must have empirical content — capable of test by experiment/observation |
| **R4** (Empirical) | The sentences in the Explanans must be **true** |

**3.5.2.2 Inductive-Probabilistic (I-P) Model**
- Extension of D-N by Hempel (1960s)
- Explanans includes **statistical laws** (not just universal laws)
- Confers **high inductive probability** on the explanandum
- Also a covering-law model; the symmetry between explanation and prediction holds
- **Explanandum**: The sentence describing the phenomenon to be explained (not the phenomenon itself)
- **Explanans**: The set of sentences adduced to account for the phenomenon; includes antecedent conditions and general laws
- **Deductive-Nomological (D-N) Model**: A model where the explanandum is logically deduced from the explanans (antecedent conditions + general laws)
- **Inductive-Probabilistic (I-P) Model**: A model where the explanans includes statistical laws and confers high inductive probability on the explanandum
- **Symmetry Thesis**: Explanation and prediction have the same logical structure; only temporal difference

### Common Mistakes
- Explanandum = the **description of the phenomenon**, not the phenomenon itself
- Symmetry thesis holds D-N just as well for prediction as for explanation
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Deduction** | Process of deriving particular statements from general statements |
| **Empiricism** | All knowledge comes from the senses / sense experience |
| **Falsification** | Popper's criterion: a theory is scientific if it can potentially be proven false by empirical observation; theories are never proven true, only corroborated |
| **Induction** | Logical process of moving from particular statements to general statements; used in social sciences to produce theory from data |
| **Logical Positivism** | All meaningful statements are either empirical (testable by observation) or logical (tautologies); no fundamental difference between natural and social sciences |
| **Modelling** | Creating a simplified representation of a system or phenomenon with hypotheses to describe/explain it |
| **Theory** | A set of general laws related in networks generated by inductive or deductive logic; a set of propositions of different levels of generality |
| **Verification** | Use of empirical data, observation, test or experiment to confirm truth or rational justification of a hypothesis |

### Classical Political Economy and Ricardo's Method 🔴
- **Adam Smith**: His methodology was enigmatic — sometimes deductive science, sometimes inductive historical; overall moral/ethical → not clearly positivist
- Primary focus shifts to **David Ricardo**
- Ricardo's *Principles of Political Economy* showed abstract-deductive thinking
- Came to be called the **hypothetico-deductive model of explanation** in economics
- "It vigorously denies that facts can speak for themselves and contends that it is theory that can make sense"
- Ricardo claimed political economy was as accurate as the **laws of gravity**
- Distinguished **pure/strictly positive science** from **art of economics** (normative)
- Further reduced political economy to **four general propositions**:
  1. Every person desires to maximise wealth with as little sacrifice as possible
  2. Population tends to increase faster than means of subsistence
  3. Labour working with machines produces a positive net product
  4. Agriculture is subject to diminishing returns
- Conclusions hold only in absence of "particular disturbing causes"
- **First introduced the concept of 'Economic Man'**
- Elaborated Senior's 'economic man': "political economy proceeds under the supposition that man…prefers a greater proportion of wealth to a smaller in all cases"
- 'Economic man' is a **fictional man** to facilitate propositions — he acknowledged this
- Treated political economy as an **"inexact science"** and **"separate science"** using *a priori* method
- Defended deductive method; used **ceteris paribus** clauses because of "tendency laws" and "disturbing causes"
- Political economy is **hypothetical and deductive**
- Its conclusions correspond with facts "only in the absence of disturbing causes" — they represent "not positive but hypothetical truths"
- Argued that deductive reasoning was **preceded by concrete observations**
- Deductive reasoning comes at a later stage drawing on individual experiences  
- Famous observation (read as a manifesto of methodological pluralism):
- **Abstract-Deductive Method (Ricardo)**: A method of economic enquiry that starts with a few fundamental postulates and derives conclusions by deductive reasoning, with empirical testing secondary
- **Economic Man**: A fictional human being who always rationally prefers more wealth to less — a theoretical construct to simplify economic analysis
- **Ceteris Paribus**: "All other things being equal" — a clause used to isolate the effect of one variable by assuming others remain constant
- **Methodological Pluralism**: The view that different methodological approaches are appropriate depending on the subject matter, stage of investigation, and research objective (J.N. Keynes)
| Economist | Key Contribution |
|---|---|
| Ricardo | Abstract-deductive; hypothetico-deductive method; political economy as science |
| Senior | Pure positive science vs normative art; 4 propositions; introduced Economic Man |
| J.S. Mill | Defended deductive method; ceteris paribus; economic man is fictional |
| Cairnes | Hypothetical-deductive; hypothetical truths not positive truths |
| J.N. Keynes | Methodological pluralism; deduction preceded by observation; comprehensive reconciliation |

### Robbins, Positivism and Apriorism in Economics 🔴
- **Context**: Counter criticism from historical/institutional schools that economic propositions were not quantifiable/testable
- **Solution**: Economics is based on deductions from a series of **postulates** universal to human experience — these are *a priori* truths requiring no testing

**Rationality in Robbins**
- **Rationality** does not mean maximising behaviour alone — means **consistency of choice**
- If A > B and B > C, then A > C (transitivity)
- Rationality and perfect foresight are **expository devices** — not realistic assumptions

**Role of Empirical Studies (Robbins — Three Uses)**
1. Check **applicability** of theoretical constructions to particular concrete situations
2. Suggest **auxiliary postulates** to use with fundamental generalisations
3. Bring to light areas where **pure theory can be reformulated or extended**

**Why Robbins is close to **Apriorism** (Austrian School)**
- Economic laws are derived from *a priori* truths of experience
- No need for testing such laws
- Claims Economics is purely positive (value-free) because economic laws are value-free
1. **Emptiness**: Propositions of pure theory are analytically empty — no empirical content → borderline "pseudo-science"
2. **Unrealistic**: Assumption of perfect expectations is baseless; rationality postulate is unrealistic
3. **Need for empirical techniques**: Economics must be testable & use more empirical investigation
4. **Psychological method**: Basing postulates on introspection is methodologically problematic
- **Apriorism**: The view that economic laws are derived from self-evident postulates based on a priori truths of experience, and therefore do not need empirical testing
- **Rationality (Robbins)**: Consistency of choice — if A > B and B > C, then A > C (not necessarily maximisation)

### Hutchison and Logical Empiricism in Economics 🔴
- Economics is a science → must **appeal to facts** → otherwise it is **pseudo-science**
- What separates science from other endeavours: **testability** of propositions
- Propositions in economics are either:
  - **Falsifiable by empirical observation** — genuine knowledge
  - **Tautologies** — analytical, without empirical content; logically true but empirically empty

**Problem with Pure Economic Theory (Hutchison)**
- Pure theory consists of deductive inferences from basic postulates
- Deductions are **analytical** — logically true by definition; no empirical content
- **Ceteris paribus clause** in microeconomics robs empirical content → microeconomic theory effectively **unfalsifiable**

**Hutchison's Demand**
- Not only the *theory*, but also the **assumptions** must be testable/falsifiable
- This stance provoked a debate with **Fritz Machlup**:
  - Hutchison: Assumptions must be testable
  - Machlup: Only lower-level/deduced hypotheses need verification; direct testing of behavioural postulates is unnecessary — *indirect* testing of rationality is sufficient

**Legacy of Hutchison**
- His proposal to **increase empirical investigations** and **abandon the psychological/introspective method** received universal acceptance
- His insistence on testing assumptions remained controversial
- **Logical Empiricism**: All meaningful statements are empirically testable or logically analytic (tautologies); a positivist tradition applied to economics
- **Tautology**: A statement that is logically true by definition and has no empirical content (e.g., "All prices are prices")
- **Ceteris Paribus**: Holding all other things equal — can render a theory unfalsifiable if overused

### Milton Friedman and Instrumentalism in Economics 🔴
1. Must be **logically consistent** with empirical counterparts
2. Must contain **substantive (testable) hypotheses**
3. **"The only relevant test of the validity of a hypothesis is comparison of its predictions with experience"**
1. Theory involves **simplification of reality** — more realism = more complexity (inverse relationship between realism and abstraction)
2. **Discrepancies between assumptions and facts are uninformative** — knowing about the gap doesn't help predict better
3. **"As-if" assumption works**: Even if an assumption is false, if the prediction holds, treat the assumption as if it were true
- **Galileo's Law**: Bodies fall in vacuum → prediction accurate even without vacuum in real life → assumption treated as-if true
- **Billiard Ball Player**: We can **assume** the player knows all laws of physics, because their shots behave **as if** they did → extended to profit maximisation: firms behave **as if** they maximise profit
1. Realism of assumptions is **"largely" irrelevant** — judge theory by predictive success
2. Standard economic theory has an **excellent predictive record**
3. Dynamics of competition over time accounts for this
| Critic | Criticism |
|---|---|
| **Samuelson** | Called it "F-Twist" — a flamboyant exaggeration. Saying assumptions can be *unrealistic* is one thing; saying *more unrealistic = more significant* is absurd |
| **Machlup, Nagel** | Even defenders of Friedman would insist on realism of at least **second-order assumptions** |
| **Caldwell** | Insistence on prediction not explanation → **measurement without theory**; correlation without causation |
| **Blaug** | Accurate prediction is not the only test of validity; impossible to distinguish between genuine and **spurious correlations** |
| **General** | Ignores **truth value** of theories; empirical predictions can match without the underlying mechanism being correct |
- **Instrumentalism**: The view that theories are merely instruments for generating accurate predictions; the "as-if" methodology; realism of assumptions is irrelevant
- **F-Twist**: Samuelson's label for Friedman's extreme claim that more significant theories have more unrealistic assumptions

### Paul Samuelson and Operationalism 🔴
- **A** = Assumptions
- **B** = Theory
- **C** = Consequences/Predictions
1. If all consequences (C) are valid, theory (B) and assumptions (A) must also be valid — contradiction to claim otherwise
2. If only some consequences (C) are valid, the corresponding parts of B and A that yield invalid consequences should be **eliminated**
1. A theory is just a **description of observable experience** — a convenient mnemonic of empirical reality
2. **Knowledge = observational reports**; theory expressible in observational language is superior
3. **Apriorism must be avoided** — theories should be in observational language
- Does not practice what he preaches — his **factor price equalisation** theorem was based on unrealistic assumptions, violating his own norm
- Stanley Wong criticised methodological foundations of Samuelson's **revealed preference theory**
- **Operationalism**: Seeking operationally meaningful theorems — observable, refutable empirical regularities derived from logically equivalent theory-assumption sets
- **Descriptivism**: Explanation in science is just a better kind of description; there is no explanation beyond description
- **Logical Equivalence (Samuelson)**: Theory, assumptions, and consequences are logically equivalent; truth of one implies truth of others
| Period | Stance | Key Advocates |
|---|---|---|
| **1880–1920** | Moderate: assumptions broadly correspond with facts; sacrifice of some accuracy justified for simplicity; need for inductive studies | Classical economists |
| **1920s–1930s** | Apriorism gains: economics is "its own reward"; no need for testing | Robbins, Austrian School |
| **1948–1953** | Instrumentalism: realism of assumptions not a genuine concern | Friedman |
| **1955** | Fritz Machlup joins Friedman; claims support of philosophy of science tradition | Machlup |
| **Today** | Economics continues with methodological inadequacy | Post-Popper mainstream |

### Amartya Sen on Heterogeneity of Explanation in Economics 🔴
| Exercise | Description | Suitable Method |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Predicting** future and **causally explaining** past events | Causal/explanatory economics | Scientific/D-N model, testing |
| 2. **Choosing appropriate descriptions** of states and events | Descriptive economics | Descriptive methods |
| 3. **Normative evaluation** of states, institutions, policies | Welfare economics/policy | Evaluative reasoning |
- Testing is appropriate for **causal theories** — widely accepted
- But NOT suitable for **evaluative exercises** — normative evaluation is not open to testing
- Some **descriptive propositions lack predictive content** — "testing" would be the wrong operation
- Conceptual and analytical phases of research are not meant for testing — one should move to testing only in the next stage
- Following Robbins, value judgements were excluded from economics
- But in welfare economics, being "value-free" **undermines the entire enterprise**
- Welfare economics inherently involves value judgements — making it indispensable in evaluative exercises
- Mathematics helps in **formalising and systematising** economic propositions
- But: **Not all economic propositions can be expressed in mathematics**
- **Over-formalisation** has occurred: excessive concentration on mathematics to the exclusion of other modes of reasoning
- Sen advocates correcting this imbalance
- **Heterogeneity of Economics**: The diverse nature of economic enquiry — predicting, describing, evaluating — requiring different methodological approaches
- **Normative Evaluation**: Assessment of states, institutions, or policies based on values and welfare criteria

### Interpretive Paradigm — Core Philosophy 🔴

**Ontological Assumption: Subjectivism**
- Reality is **socially constructed and interpreted** — not objective and external
- "Social reality is based on people's definition of it" (Neuman, 1997)
- Interpretivists do not recognise the existence of an objective world — they see the world as **bounded by particular time and context**

**Epistemological Assumption**
- Knowledge of reality is obtained from the **accounts that social actors provide**
- "Findings are literally created as the investigation proceeds" (Guba & Lincoln)
- Understanding social reality requires understanding how **practices and meanings are formed by shared language and tacit norms**

**Focus of Interpretive Research**
- Documenting and interpreting:
  - World views, values, meanings, beliefs, thoughts
  - Characteristics of life events, situations, phenomena
- Goal: **Document and interpret as fully as possible** the totality of what is studied, **from the people's viewpoint**

**Key Supporting Philosophies**
| Philosophy | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Verstehen** | Understanding the *particulars* of a situation |
| **Hermeneutics** | Importance of *language and context* in understanding |
| **Phenomenology** | People's *perception* of the world; conscious experience |

**Methodologies Used in Interpretive Research**
1. **Naturalistic Inquiry**: First-hand observation of real-life situations as they unfold; non-manipulative; no predetermined outcomes
2. **Phenomenological Methodologies**: Rely on descriptions of conscious experiences to understand meaning of human action
3. **Constructivism**: Uses perception/self-experience in making and structuring knowledge
4. **Ethnographic Inquiry**: Self-experiencing the culture of participants in the field
5. **Symbolic Interactionism**: Understands human action through meanings derived by humans for objects/people

**Key Axioms of Naturalistic Inquiry (Lincoln and Guba, 1985)**
1. There are **multiple constructed realities** that can only be studied holistically — reality is complex, contextual, constructed, and subjective
2. The **inquirer and the "object" of inquiry interact** to influence one another — knower and known are *inseparable*
3. **No a priori theory** can encompass the multiple realities — theory must **emerge from the data** (grounded theory)

**Research Strategy for Interpretivism**
| Dimension | Post-positivism | Interpretivism |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of reality | External to human mind | Socially constructed |
| Purpose of research | Find universals | Reflect understanding (contextual) |
| Acceptable methods | Scientific method | Subjective and objective methods acceptable |
| Meaning of data | Falsification | Understanding is contextual; universals deemphasised |
| Research-practice relationship | Separate; research guides practice | Integrated activities; both guide each other |
- Risk of **losing theoretical sensitivity** (Strauss & Corbin) — researchers must maintain awareness of subtle meanings in data
- Must evaluate relevance of preliminary theoretical framework against actual findings
- **Interpretivism / Interpretive Paradigm**: "Our knowledge of reality is gained only through social constructions such as language, consciousness, shared meanings, documents, tools, and other artifacts" (Klein & Myers)
- **Subjectivism** (Ontology of Interpretivism): Reality is socially constructed and subjectively interpreted
- **Anti-foundationalism**: Interpretivists believe that the standards guiding research are products of particular groups/cultures — there are no self-evident universal starting points
- **Verstehen**: German for "understanding"; stresses understanding of the particulars of a situation
- **Hermeneutics**: The study of interpretation, especially the role of language and context in understanding
- **Phenomenology**: Philosophical method relying on descriptions of conscious experience to develop understanding of meaning of human action
- **Naturalistic Inquiry**: Research using first-hand observation to understand human action in real-life, non-manipulative settings

### Critical Theory Paradigm 🔴
- Rooted in **Marxism** — class conflict; reality can only be reformed through radical transformation
- The **Frankfurt School** (Institute for Social Research):
  - Herbert Marcuse, Theodor Adorno, Max Horkheimer, Walter Benjamin, Erich Fromm
- Other labels: Neo-Marxism, Materialism, Frankfurt School, Freireism
- Critical response to works of Marx, Kant, Hegel, and Weber
- There is a **reality** that is apprehendable — shaped by social, political, cultural, economic, ethnic, and gender-based forces **crystallised over time into social structures**
- These structures are taken to be "natural" — but critical theorists believe this assumption is **inappropriate** and must be challenged
- We cannot separate ourselves from what we know — knowledge is **inextricably tied to the interaction** between a particular investigator and a particular object/group
- Research is a **first step toward addressing injustice** — it has a **normative and transformative** purpose
- Not interested in "knowledge for knowledge's sake"
- Critical theory is **multi-disciplinary**: applied in anthropology, economics, education, history, psychology, political science, sociology, theology
- Objective is to **analyse power structures** in a social set-up
- Research addresses **social problems arising from power asymmetries**  
- Example: Wage inequities between male, female, and child workers
1. **Ideology Critique**: Examines and challenges hidden ideological assumptions embedded in knowledge, institutions, and practices
2. **Action Research**: Combines understanding with action; researchers not only study but also work to change the situation
1. Understand the **ideologically distorted subjective situation** of a group
2. Explore the **forces that caused** that situation
3. Show that these forces **can be overcome** through awareness by the oppressed
- Critical theory does NOT claim "objective analysis" in the logical positivist sense
- Acknowledges **subjectivism** — but rejects *pure relativism*
- Asserts that rational analysis is fundamental to human emancipation → **Critical Realism** (Morrow, 1994)
1. Provides **insight into systems of oppression and domination** that limit human freedom
2. Is **useful in countering such systems**
| Dimension | Post-positivism | Critical Theory |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of reality | External to human mind | Material and external, but interpreted through ideology |
| Purpose of research | Find universals | Uncover power relationships; empower the oppressed |
| Acceptable methods | Scientific method | Subjective inquiry based on ideology and values; both quantitative and qualitative |
| Meaning of data | Falsification | Interpreted through ideology; used to enlighten and emancipate |
| Research-practice relationship | Separate; research guides practice | Integrated activities; research guides practice |
1. **Elitism**: Critical theorists claim they are *better equipped* to analyse a situation and prescribe solutions → can be paternalistic and out of touch
2. **Out of touch**: Focus on persons in power; not listening to those most adversely affected by policies
3. **Prescriptions may be naïve and unworkable** in contemporary settings
- **Critical Theory**: A theoretical tradition developed at the Frankfurt School; focuses on uncovering oppressive power structures and emancipating the oppressed; based on ideology of class conflict
- **Substantialism**: The ontological position of Critical Theory — matter constitutes reality
- **Historical Ontology**: Reality is apprehendable but shaped and crystallised by historical social, political, cultural, and economic forces
- **Ideology Critique**: A methodology that examines and challenges hidden ideological assumptions embedded in knowledge and social practices
- **Action Research**: Research methodology that combines understanding with practical action to change the social situation
- **Retroduction**: Research strategy starting with a hypothetical model of a mechanism that explains a phenomenon (associated with Critical Realism)
- **Critical Realism**: The view that rational analysis is fundamental to human emancipation; acknowledges subjectivism but rejects pure relativism
