## Section: Explanation in Non-Physical Sciences 🟡
<!-- Exam importance: 🟡 MODERATE -->

### Core Idea
Applying the Positivist/D-N model of explanation to social sciences faces fundamental limitations because social phenomena involve uniqueness, purposive behaviour, and individual history.

### In Simple Terms
The D-N model works well for physics (law: all balls fall the same way). In social sciences, human behaviour is too unique, historical, and motivated to fit neatly into universal laws.

### Three Key Limitations (Hempel and Oppenheim)
1. **Uniqueness and Irrepeatability**: Human events (individual or group) have a peculiar uniqueness that makes them inaccessible to causal explanation. Causal explanation presupposes uniformities and repeatability — which may not hold in social sciences.

2. **History-dependence**: Individual reactions in a situation depend not only on that situation but on the **previous history of the individual** — making general scientific generalisations difficult or impossible.

3. **Purposive/Teleological Behaviour**: Explanation of human action requires reference to **goals and motivations** — this is teleological reasoning (future states explaining present actions), not causal analysis. Many explanations in social science involve motivations with less cognitive significance.

### Implications
- Applying scientific models to non-physical sciences **without closer understanding of context** leads to inappropriate explanations
- Some domains of economics may be amenable to positivist models; many others may not be

### Quick Recall
> **3 reasons D-N fails in social sciences**: Uniqueness | History-dependence | Purposive/Teleological behaviour

## Section: Key Words — Unit 3 Glossary 🟢
<!-- Exam importance: 🟢 CONTEXT -->

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Deduction** | Process of deriving particular statements from general statements |
| **Empiricism** | All knowledge comes from the senses / sense experience |
| **Falsification** | Popper's criterion: a theory is scientific if it can potentially be proven false by empirical observation; theories are never proven true, only corroborated |
| **Induction** | Logical process of moving from particular statements to general statements; used in social sciences to produce theory from data |
| **Logical Positivism** | All meaningful statements are either empirical (testable by observation) or logical (tautologies); no fundamental difference between natural and social sciences |
| **Modelling** | Creating a simplified representation of a system or phenomenon with hypotheses to describe/explain it |
| **Theory** | A set of general laws related in networks generated by inductive or deductive logic; a set of propositions of different levels of generality |
| **Verification** | Use of empirical data, observation, test or experiment to confirm truth or rational justification of a hypothesis |

## Section: Classical Political Economy and Ricardo's Method 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
The roots of the debate about explanation in economics trace back to Classical Political Economy. Ricardo's method of abstract-deductive reasoning set the template for a long line of defence of Economics as a scientific discipline.

### Background
- **Adam Smith**: His methodology was enigmatic — sometimes deductive science, sometimes inductive historical; overall moral/ethical → not clearly positivist
- Primary focus shifts to **David Ricardo**

### 4.2.1 Ricardo's Method
- Ricardo's *Principles of Political Economy* showed abstract-deductive thinking
- Came to be called the **hypothetico-deductive model of explanation** in economics
- "It vigorously denies that facts can speak for themselves and contends that it is theory that can make sense"
- Ricardo claimed political economy was as accurate as the **laws of gravity**

### 4.2.2 N.W. Senior's Defence
Senior's *Outline of the Science of Political Economy* (1836):
- Distinguished **pure/strictly positive science** from **art of economics** (normative)
- Further reduced political economy to **four general propositions**:
  1. Every person desires to maximise wealth with as little sacrifice as possible
  2. Population tends to increase faster than means of subsistence
  3. Labour working with machines produces a positive net product
  4. Agriculture is subject to diminishing returns
- Conclusions hold only in absence of "particular disturbing causes"
- **First introduced the concept of 'Economic Man'**

### 4.2.3 J.S. Mill
- Elaborated Senior's 'economic man': "political economy proceeds under the supposition that man…prefers a greater proportion of wealth to a smaller in all cases"
- 'Economic man' is a **fictional man** to facilitate propositions — he acknowledged this
- Treated political economy as an **"inexact science"** and **"separate science"** using *a priori* method
- Defended deductive method; used **ceteris paribus** clauses because of "tendency laws" and "disturbing causes"

### 4.2.4 J.E. Cairnes
- Political economy is **hypothetical and deductive**
- Its conclusions correspond with facts "only in the absence of disturbing causes" — they represent "not positive but hypothetical truths"

### 4.2.5 J.N. Keynes — Methodological Pluralism
- Argued that deductive reasoning was **preceded by concrete observations**
- Deductive reasoning comes at a later stage drawing on individual experiences  
- Famous observation (read as a manifesto of methodological pluralism):
  > "It is...impossible to establish the right of any one method to hold the field to the exclusion of others. Different methods are appropriate, according to the material available, the stage of investigation reached, and the object in view."

### Definitions
- **Abstract-Deductive Method (Ricardo)**: A method of economic enquiry that starts with a few fundamental postulates and derives conclusions by deductive reasoning, with empirical testing secondary
- **Economic Man**: A fictional human being who always rationally prefers more wealth to less — a theoretical construct to simplify economic analysis
- **Ceteris Paribus**: "All other things being equal" — a clause used to isolate the effect of one variable by assuming others remain constant
- **Methodological Pluralism**: The view that different methodological approaches are appropriate depending on the subject matter, stage of investigation, and research objective (J.N. Keynes)

### Quick Recall
| Economist | Key Contribution |
|---|---|
| Ricardo | Abstract-deductive; hypothetico-deductive method; political economy as science |
| Senior | Pure positive science vs normative art; 4 propositions; introduced Economic Man |
| J.S. Mill | Defended deductive method; ceteris paribus; economic man is fictional |
| Cairnes | Hypothetical-deductive; hypothetical truths not positive truths |
| J.N. Keynes | Methodological pluralism; deduction preceded by observation; comprehensive reconciliation |

## Section: Robbins, Positivism and Apriorism in Economics 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Lionel Robbins defended Economics as a pure positive science based on **self-evident a priori truths** that need not be empirically tested. His rejection of empirical testing drew fire from Hutchison.

### Key Points of Robbins' Argument
- **Context**: Counter criticism from historical/institutional schools that economic propositions were not quantifiable/testable
- **Solution**: Economics is based on deductions from a series of **postulates** universal to human experience — these are *a priori* truths requiring no testing

#### Rationality in Robbins
- **Rationality** does not mean maximising behaviour alone — means **consistency of choice**
- If A > B and B > C, then A > C (transitivity)
- Rationality and perfect foresight are **expository devices** — not realistic assumptions

#### Role of Empirical Studies (Robbins — Three Uses)
Robbins does not reject empirical studies entirely. Proper uses:
1. Check **applicability** of theoretical constructions to particular concrete situations
2. Suggest **auxiliary postulates** to use with fundamental generalisations
3. Bring to light areas where **pure theory can be reformulated or extended**

#### Why Robbins is close to **Apriorism** (Austrian School)
- Economic laws are derived from *a priori* truths of experience
- No need for testing such laws
- Claims Economics is purely positive (value-free) because economic laws are value-free

### Hutchison's Criticism of Robbins
1. **Emptiness**: Propositions of pure theory are analytically empty — no empirical content → borderline "pseudo-science"
2. **Unrealistic**: Assumption of perfect expectations is baseless; rationality postulate is unrealistic
3. **Need for empirical techniques**: Economics must be testable & use more empirical investigation
4. **Psychological method**: Basing postulates on introspection is methodologically problematic

### Definitions
- **Apriorism**: The view that economic laws are derived from self-evident postulates based on a priori truths of experience, and therefore do not need empirical testing
- **Rationality (Robbins)**: Consistency of choice — if A > B and B > C, then A > C (not necessarily maximisation)

### Quick Recall
> **Robbins** = Pure theory + A priori truths + Value-free = Positive science  
> **Hutchison calls it**: Empty, unrealistic, insufficiently testable → Pseudo-science risk

## Section: Hutchison and Logical Empiricism in Economics 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
T.W. Hutchison's *The Significance and Basic Postulates of Economic Theory* (1938) marked a clear logical positivist turn in economic methodology — demanding that economics be tested and falsifiable to qualify as science.

### Key Points
- Economics is a science → must **appeal to facts** → otherwise it is **pseudo-science**
- What separates science from other endeavours: **testability** of propositions
- Propositions in economics are either:
  - **Falsifiable by empirical observation** — genuine knowledge
  - **Tautologies** — analytical, without empirical content; logically true but empirically empty

#### Problem with Pure Economic Theory (Hutchison)
- Pure theory consists of deductive inferences from basic postulates
- Deductions are **analytical** — logically true by definition; no empirical content
- **Ceteris paribus clause** in microeconomics robs empirical content → microeconomic theory effectively **unfalsifiable**

#### Hutchison's Demand
- Not only the *theory*, but also the **assumptions** must be testable/falsifiable
- This stance provoked a debate with **Fritz Machlup**:
  - Hutchison: Assumptions must be testable
  - Machlup: Only lower-level/deduced hypotheses need verification; direct testing of behavioural postulates is unnecessary — *indirect* testing of rationality is sufficient

#### Legacy of Hutchison
- His proposal to **increase empirical investigations** and **abandon the psychological/introspective method** received universal acceptance
- His insistence on testing assumptions remained controversial

### Definitions
- **Logical Empiricism**: All meaningful statements are empirically testable or logically analytic (tautologies); a positivist tradition applied to economics
- **Tautology**: A statement that is logically true by definition and has no empirical content (e.g., "All prices are prices")
- **Ceteris Paribus**: Holding all other things equal — can render a theory unfalsifiable if overused

### Quick Recall
> **Hutchison's demand**: Both theory AND assumptions must be **testable**  
> **Ceteris paribus overuse** → theory becomes **unfalsifiable** = not scientific  
> **Pseudo-science**: Economics that cannot be tested empirically
