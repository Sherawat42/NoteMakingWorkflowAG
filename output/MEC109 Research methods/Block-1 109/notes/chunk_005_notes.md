## Section: Unified View of Rules of Positivism (Kolakowski) 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Kolakowski provides a concise, unified account of Positivism through four rules. Despite decades of critique, Positivism remains the dominant model of scientific explanation — especially in mainstream economics.

### In Simple Terms
Even though Positivism has been attacked, it remains attractive because its prescriptions are **clear, rigid, and prescriptive**, while alternatives are open-ended and speculative.

### Key Concepts

#### Why Positivism Still Dominates (Despite Critique)
1. Its propositions are **rigorous and prescriptive** — simple and attractive
2. Alternative approaches are **open-ended and speculative**
3. Many economists still treat Economics as a "Positive Science"

#### Kolakowski's Four Rules of Positivism
| Rule | Description |
|---|---|
| **K1 — Phenomenalism** | Knowledge is based only on phenomena (sensory experience). Facts and objective facts alone form the basis of knowledge. It is existence, not essence; facts not noumena (things-in-themselves). |
| **K2 — Nominalism (Nouminalism)** | Any insight formulated in general terms has no real reference other than individual facts. Abstract science only abridges experience; no independent knowledge beyond what is observed. |
| **K3 — Value-free Statements** | Knowledge is value-free. Value judgements and normative statements cannot be called knowledge. Science has no room for ethical judgements ("good/bad"). |
| **K4 — Unity of Science** | There is only one method of scientific knowledge. All laws are ultimately derived from a single fundamental form. |

### Definitions
- **Phenomenalism (K1)**: Knowledge is based on what appears in experience — phenomena, not noumena
- **Nominalism/Nouminalism (K2)**: General terms have no real reference beyond individual observable facts
- **Noumena**: Kant's term for "things as they are in themselves" — beyond sensory experience; rejected by Positivists

### Quick Recall
> **K1** = Facts only (Phenomenalism)  
> **K2** = No real meaning in abstractions (Nominalism)  
> **K3** = No value judgements (Value-free)  
> **K4** = One method for all (Unity of Science)

## Section: Search for the Criterion of Cognitive Significance 🟡
<!-- Exam importance: 🟡 MODERATE -->

### Core Idea
The central criterion of Positivism for distinguishing scientific from non-scientific knowledge evolved from Verification → Falsification → Confirmation.

### Evolution of the Criterion of Cognitive Significance

| Stage | Criterion | Proponent | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Positivism | **Verification** — complete verification by observational evidence required | Logical Positivists | Too strict; problem of induction |
| Popperian | **Falsification** — a statement is scientific if falsifiable | Karl Popper | Too strict; few theories meet the standard perfectly |
| Later Positivism | **Confirmation** — repeated positive tests increase degree of confirmation; theories ranked by relative degree of confirmation | Rudolf Carnap | More flexible but still fact/testing-based |

### Quick Recall
> **Testability** = the basis of Positivist cognitive significance  
> **Evolution**: Verification → Falsification → Confirmation

## Section: Rules of Logic / Correct Reasoning 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Formal logic is a foundational block of scientific explanation under Positivism. Two types of syllogism — categorical and hypothetical — form the basis of deductive reasoning.

### In Simple Terms
Deductive reasoning means if the premises are true and the logic is valid, the conclusion must follow. But logical truth does not guarantee material (factual) truth.

### Key Concepts

#### 3.4.1 Categorical Syllogism
Two categorical statements as premises → necessary conclusion.
- **Example** (valid): All As are B; C is an A → Therefore, C is B
- **Example** (real): All Politicians are corrupt; Blogg is a politician → Therefore, Blogg is corrupt *(logically valid, may not be factually true)*
- **Fallacy of Enthymeme**: A conclusion without stated premises ("Blogg is a politician, therefore corrupt" — missing major premise)

#### 3.4.2 Hypothetical Syllogism (Modus Ponens)
- Takes the form: "If A is true, then B is true; A is true → Therefore, B is true"
- Also called **affirming the antecedent**

#### 3.4.3 Logical Truth vs Material Truth
- A statement can be **logically valid** but **factually false**
- Example: "All politicians have two tongues; Blogg is a politician → Blogg has two tongues" — logically true, materially false

#### 3.4.4 Deductive Fallacies
Three types:

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical (Formal) Fallacy** — Affirming the Consequent | Incorrect: If A then B; B is true → Therefore A is true (wrong!) | Should be Modus Ponens, not affirming consequent |
| **Verbal Fallacy** — Fallacy of Composition | What is true of a part is made true of the whole | "If one person stands on tiptoe, they see better → If all stand on tiptoe, all see better" |
| **Material Fallacy** | Various others: | |
| — *Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc* | Because B follows A, B is caused by A | |
| — *Argument by Analogy* | A is similar to B; what's true of A must be true of B | "India = Singapore → open economy will work the same way" |
| — *Appeal to Authority* | Truth asserted by citing an authority | "India is shining because Milton Friedman said so" |

### Definitions
- **Categorical Syllogism**: Two categorical premises → necessary conclusion
- **Hypothetical Syllogism (Modus Ponens / 'Affirming the Antecedent')**: If-then form; affirms antecedent to conclude consequent
- **Logical Truth**: A statement that is true by the rules of logic/formal validity
- **Material Truth**: A statement that is factually true about the world
- **Enthymeme**: A fallacy where a conclusion is drawn without stating the premises

### Quick Recall
> **Modus Ponens** (correct): If A then B → A is true → **B is true**  
> **Fallacy** (wrong): If A then B → B is true → **A is true** (WRONG — affirms consequent)

## Section: Positivist Models of Scientific Explanation 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Two major families of Positivist models of explanation: the Hypothetico-Deductive (H-D) model, and the Covering-Law models (D-N and I-P). All aim to provide a formal logical structure for scientific explanation.

### 3.5.1 Hypothetico-Deductive (H-D) Model
Developed by Carnap and Hempel.

**Key features**:
- Propositions in a deductive system arranged in **levels**:
  - **Higher-level hypotheses**: Refer to theoretical entities; need not all be directly tested
  - **Lower-level hypotheses**: Describe observable phenomena; tested against reality
- Theories as a whole are tested by comparing their **deduced consequences (predictions)** with data
- Not every theoretical term needs a direct empirical counterpart — indirect confirmation through the theory suffices
- Defuses the **Realist vs Instrumentalist controversy**:
  - Realists say: All theoretical terms must refer to real entities
  - Instrumentalists say: Theories are only instruments for prediction
  - H-D accommodates both without resolving the debate philosophically

**Four positive functions of theories (H-D)**:
1. Allow **generality** in specification of scientific laws
2. Possess **formal simplicity** allowing elegant mathematical machinery
3. Allow discovery of **interdependencies among observables**
4. Serve as **heuristic devices** with explanatory function

### 3.5.2 Covering-Law Models (Hempel and Oppenheim)
#### 3.5.2.1 Deductive-Nomological (D-N) Model

Structure of a D-N explanation:

```
Explanans:
  Antecedent conditions: C1, C2, ... Ck
  General Laws:          L1, L2, ... Lr
         ↓  (Logical deduction)
Explanandum: E (description of phenomenon to be explained)
```

**Four conditions of adequacy** for a D-N explanation:
| Condition | Description |
|---|---|
| **R1** (Logical) | Explanandum must be a logical consequence of the Explanans |
| **R2** (Logical) | Explanans must contain at least one general law actually required for deriving explanandum |
| **R3** (Logical) | Explanans must have empirical content — capable of test by experiment/observation |
| **R4** (Empirical) | The sentences in the Explanans must be **true** |

**Symmetry Thesis**: The same formal schema applies to both **explanation** and **prediction** (only the temporal sequence differs).

#### 3.5.2.2 Inductive-Probabilistic (I-P) Model
- Extension of D-N by Hempel (1960s)
- Explanans includes **statistical laws** (not just universal laws)
- Confers **high inductive probability** on the explanandum
- Also a covering-law model; the symmetry between explanation and prediction holds

### Definitions
- **Explanandum**: The sentence describing the phenomenon to be explained (not the phenomenon itself)
- **Explanans**: The set of sentences adduced to account for the phenomenon; includes antecedent conditions and general laws
- **Deductive-Nomological (D-N) Model**: A model where the explanandum is logically deduced from the explanans (antecedent conditions + general laws)
- **Inductive-Probabilistic (I-P) Model**: A model where the explanans includes statistical laws and confers high inductive probability on the explanandum
- **Symmetry Thesis**: Explanation and prediction have the same logical structure; only temporal difference

### Common Mistakes
- Explanandum = the **description of the phenomenon**, not the phenomenon itself
- Symmetry thesis holds D-N just as well for prediction as for explanation

### Quick Recall
> **D-N Model**: (Antecedent Conditions + General Laws) → **Deductive** → Phenomenon  
> **I-P Model**: (Antecedent Conditions + Statistical Laws) → **Inductive Probability** → Phenomenon  
> **R1–R4**: See above conditions of adequacy.

## Section: Critical Appraisal of Covering-Law (D-N, I-P) Models 🟡
<!-- Exam importance: 🟡 MODERATE -->

### Core Idea
The D-N model is a useful starting point but has significant limitations. Critics identified four main problems.

### Four Criticisms of the D-N Model

1. **Laws are not always necessary**  
   - In biology, astronomy, social sciences — explanations need not take the form of laws  
   - Example: Why do Hopi Indians perform rain ceremonies? → "To reinforce group identity" — no law involved  
   - Example: Why do vertebrate hearts beat? → "To circulate blood" — no universal law

2. **Causation vs Correlation**  
   - D-N model is often satisfied by citing causally relevant factors, but this may yield correlation without causation  
   - Hume's "Billiard Ball" problem: two contiguous events are not necessarily cause-effect  
   - Effects do not explain their causes; effects of common causes do not explain each other

3. **Symmetry Thesis is Questionable**  
   - Explanation does not always predict (Darwin's Evolution explains origins but does not predict future species)  
   - Prediction does not always explain (weather prediction does not explain why weather happens)

4. **Ignores Divergence Between Norms and Practice**  
   - Functional/teleological explanations are excluded because they don't involve laws  
   - Motivated/purposive behaviour (future affects present as cause) cannot fit antecedent conditions neatly

### Quick Recall
> **4 Criticisms**: Laws not always needed | Causation ≠ correlation | Symmetry questionable | Excludes functional/motivated explanations
