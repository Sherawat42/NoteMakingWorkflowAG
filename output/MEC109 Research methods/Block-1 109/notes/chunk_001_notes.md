## Section: Research Methodology and its Constituents 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Research Methodology is a broad framework encompassing the entire logic and philosophy behind research. It is distinct from "Research Methods" which focuses only on tools and techniques.

### In Simple Terms
Think of Research Methodology as the **blueprint** of a building (why, what philosophy), while Research Methods are the **tools** (hammer, drill) used to build it.

### Key Concepts

#### Definition of Research
A **scientific and systematic enquiry** to discover or verify facts, their interrelationships, and causal explanations, aiming to discover truth through scientific methods.

#### Three Constituents of Research Methodology
1. **Theoretical perspectives / orientation** — the philosophical lens guiding research
2. **Tools and techniques of data collection** — how data is gathered
3. **Methods of data analysis** — how data is interpreted

#### Research Methods vs Methodology vs Tools
| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| **Research Methodology** | Broader term; includes philosophy + methods + tools |
| **Research Methods** | Techniques and tools for data collection and analysis |
| **Research Techniques** | Practical aspects of data collection and organisation |
| **Research Tools** | Instruments: questionnaires, schedules, diaries, maps, photos |

### Definitions
- **Research**: A scientific and systematic enquiry to discover new facts or verify old facts, their sequences, interrelationships and causal explanations
- **Research Methodology**: Consists of theoretical perspectives, tools/techniques of data collection, and methods of data analysis

### Mechanisms / Processes
- Quantitative data: collected via census, surveys, structured questionnaires
- Qualitative data: via participant observation, semi-structured interviews, life histories, experiments, pilot studies

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing "methodology" with "methods" — methodology is the broader philosophy; methods are the specific tools
- Assuming research = just data collection. Data collection is only one part of the research process.

### Quick Recall
> **Research Methodology = Perspective + Methods + Analysis**

## Section: Theoretical Perspectives — Paradigms 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
A **paradigm** is the overarching worldview that shapes what a researcher considers as reality (ontology), how they gain knowledge (epistemology), and what methods they use.

### In Simple Terms
A paradigm is like a **pair of glasses** — it determines what you see, what you consider real, and what counts as valid knowledge.

### Key Concepts

#### Definition of Paradigm
> "A comprehensive belief system, world view or framework that guides research and practice."

A paradigm has three layers:
1. **Philosophy of Science** (ontology + epistemology)
2. **World view / Conceptual Framework**
3. **Methodological Prescriptions** (tools for data collection & analysis)

#### Four Major Research Paradigms in Social Sciences
| Paradigm | Core Belief |
|---|---|
| **Positivism / Post-positivism** | Reality is external; scientific method; deductive |
| **Critical Theory** | Reality shaped by power; critique and emancipation |
| **Interpretivism** | Reality is socially constructed; subjective understanding |
| **Pragmatism** | Focus on practical consequences of actions |

#### Alternative 3-Category Classification
- **Realism**: Real external world exists; goal is to understand it
- **Constructivism**: Reality is unique to each person's experience and perception
- **Pragmatism**: Questions about reality less important than consequences of action

#### How Paradigm Guides Research
Knowledge of paradigm helps a researcher decide:
1. The research problem
2. Research questions
3. Research strategies
4. Approaches to social inquiry
5. Concepts and theories directing investigation
6. Sources, forms and types of data
7. Methods for collecting and analyzing data

### Definitions
- **Ontology**: Branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of reality — what exists?
- **Epistemology**: Theory of knowledge — how can we know things?
- **Paradigm**: A comprehensive belief system, world view or framework guiding research and practice

### Common Mistakes
- Mixing up Ontology and Epistemology: Ontology = nature of reality; Epistemology = how we know it

### Quick Recall
> **Ontology** = What is real? | **Epistemology** = How do we know it? | **Paradigm** = Complete worldview

## Section: Approaches to Social Enquiry 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Two broad approaches are used in social science research: **Quantitative** (linked to positivism) and **Qualitative** (linked to interpretivism and critical theory). Mixed methods combine both.

### In Simple Terms
Quantitative approach = measuring with numbers and equations; Qualitative approach = understanding through words, context, meanings.

### Key Concepts

#### Quantitative vs Qualitative Approach
| Characteristic | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| Typical data collection | Participant obs., semi-structured interviews, focus groups | Laboratory, questionnaire, structured interviews |
| Questions | Open-ended, two-way exchange | Closed, prepared answer categories |
| Respondent selection | Information maximization; each may be unique | Representativeness; random sampling |
| Timing of analysis | Parallel with data collection | After data collection |
| Analysis methods | Descriptive; mixed methods | Standard statistical methods |
| Role of theory | Used as departure; new theory emerges | A priori theories tested deductively |
| Associated paradigm | Critical theory, Interpretivism | Positivism, Post-positivism |

#### Sub-types of Quantitative Approach
1. **Inferential**: Database via survey; inferences about variable relationships
2. **Experimental**: Controlled environment; variables manipulated to observe effects
3. **Simulation**: Numerical model of a dynamic process; artificial environment with relevant data generation

#### Mixed Methods
- Combines at least one quantitative and one qualitative method
- Used to strengthen validity of research findings

### Definitions
- **Quantitative Research**: Purposes and procedures that are deductive, objective and generalized; follows well-controlled procedures with large number of cases
- **Qualitative Research**: Purposes and procedures integrating inductive, subjective and contextual approaches; evolves meaning through close researcher-participant contact
- **Mixed Methods**: Combining quantitative and qualitative methods in measurement, collection or analysis of data

### Common Mistakes
- Assuming qualitative = less rigorous. Qualitative research has its own rigorous standards
- Equating Quantitative with Positivism exclusively — post-positivism also uses quantitative methods

### Quick Recall
> **Quantitative** → Positivism/Realism → Deductive → Numbers  
> **Qualitative** → Interpretivism/Critical Theory → Inductive → Meanings

## Section: Research Strategies 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Research strategy is the overall logic of enquiry. There are four strategies, each linked to a paradigm and with a distinct starting point.

### In Simple Terms
A research strategy tells you **where to begin** — data first, or theory first?

### Key Concepts

#### Four Research Strategies
| Paradigm | Strategy | Starting Point |
|---|---|---|
| Positivism | **Induction** | Data → Generalisation → Explanation |
| Post-positivism / Realism | **Deduction** | Theory → Test via data collection |
| Critical Realism | **Retroduction** | Hypothetical mechanism → Explain phenomenon |
| Interpretivism | **Abduction** | Meanings in social accounts → Research problem |

#### Detailed Descriptions
1. **Inductive strategy**: Begins with data collection; generalisation is made as an elementary explanation
2. **Deductive strategy**: Starts with a theory providing a possible answer; tests theory via relevant data
3. **Retroductive strategy**: Starts with a hypothetical model of a mechanism that could explain a phenomenon under investigation
4. **Abductive strategy**: Starts with the concepts and meanings in social actors' accounts of activities related to the research problem

### Definitions
- **Induction**: Moving from particular observations to general laws
- **Deduction**: Deriving conclusions from general principles to specific instances
- **Retroduction**: Hypothetical modelling of underlying mechanisms to explain observations
- **Abduction**: Interpreting the meanings social actors assign to their activities

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing Retroduction with Abduction: Retroduction = hypothetical mechanism; Abduction = social meanings and interpretations

### Quick Recall
| Strategy | Starts with |
|---|---|
| Induction | Observations/Data |
| Deduction | Theory |
| Retroduction | Hypothetical mechanism |
| Abduction | Social meanings/accounts |
