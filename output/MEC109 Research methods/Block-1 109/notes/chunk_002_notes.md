## Section: Research Process 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
The Research Process is a series of interconnected, overlapping steps that take a researcher from a research problem to a final report. The steps are not strictly sequential.

### In Simple Terms
Research is like building a house — you can't put up walls before laying foundations, but some tasks overlap (plumbing while plastering). The 7 steps guide you, but they loop back.

### Key Concepts

#### The 7 Steps of the Research Process
1. **Defining the Research Problem**
2. **Review of Literature**
3. **Formulation of Hypothesis** (Objectives + Research Questions)
4. **Research Design**
5. **Collection of Data**
6. **Analysis of Data**
7. **Report Writing**

#### Step 1: Defining the Research Problem
- This is the **foremost step** — the problem must be defined categorically
- Start broad, narrow down operationally
- Key elements to state clearly:
  - Rationale behind the problem
  - Aims and objectives (determines data, hypothesis, techniques)
  - Research questions grounded in theory
  - Ideally revisit and rephrase after literature review
- Technical terms must be explicitly defined; basic assumptions stated

#### Step 2: Review of Literature
- Two types: **(i) Conceptual/Theoretical literature** and **(ii) Empirical literature** (quantitative findings)
- Sources: journals, conference proceedings, government reports, books, internet

#### Step 3: Formulation of Hypothesis
- A **tentative statement** that needs testing for logical and empirical confirmation
- Stated as a proposition about cause-effect or relationship between variables
- Must be **testable** (capable of being accepted or rejected)
- Helps focus research and identify the method of analysis
- Exploratory or descriptive research can proceed **without a hypothesis**

#### Step 4: Research Design
- Logical conceptual structure within which research is conducted
- Blueprint for collection, measurement and analysis of data

#### Step 5: Collection of Data
- Primary Data: collected by researcher (survey, observation, interview, experiment)
- Secondary Data: already published data from agencies (CSO, NSSO, RBI, IMF)

#### Step 6: Analysis of Data
Steps in analysis: **Editing → Coding → Classification → Tabulation → Statistical Analysis**
Types of analysis:
- **Uni-dimensional**: Measures of central tendency, dispersion, skewness, ANOVA, time series
- **Bivariate**: Simple regression, correlation, association of attributes
- **Multivariate**: Multiple regression, factor analysis, cluster analysis, canonical analysis

#### Step 7: Report Writing
- Two components: **Preliminary pages** + **Main text**
- Preliminary pages: Title, researcher name, institution, month/year, preface with key findings
- Main text: Introduction (objectives, rationale, literature summary, hypotheses, methodology, limitations) → Subsequent chapters → Conclusions
- An **Executive Summary** is placed before introductory chapter

### Definitions
- **Research Process**: Different steps involved in a desired sequence in carrying out research

### Common Mistakes
- Treating the process as strictly linear — steps overlap and are not mutually exclusive
- Neglecting the literature review — this step can refine the problem statement itself

### Quick Recall
> **Define → Review → Hypothesize → Design → Collect → Analyse → Report**

## Section: Hypothesis — Types and Sources 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
A hypothesis is a testable, tentative explanation of a phenomenon, describing cause-and-effect or a relationship between variables. There are two main methods of generating hypotheses.

### In Simple Terms
A hypothesis is an **educated guess** that must be proved or disproved by data.

### Key Concepts

#### Definition of Hypothesis
> "Potential explanations that can account for our observations of the external world. They usually describe cause-and-effect relationships between a proposed mechanism (cause) and observations (effect)."
> "A tentative statement made which needs to be tested for its logical and empirical confirmation."

#### Two Methods of Hypothesis Generation
| Method | Flow | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | Theory → Hypothesis → Observation → Confirmation | Starts from theory |
| **Inductive** | Observation → Pattern → Tentative Hypothesis → Theory | Starts from observation |

#### Research Hypothesis (vs. General Hypothesis)
- A **Research Hypothesis** is a **predictive statement** relating an independent variable to a dependent variable
- The relationship must be testable/objectively verifiable — otherwise it does not qualify as a research hypothesis

#### Null and Alternative Hypothesis (implicit)
- Hypotheses tested in quantitative research establish **statistical significance** of findings
- Qualitative research: hypothesis may be about social settings having certain features, confirmed or falsified via observation

### Definitions
- **Hypothesis**: A tentative statement about a phenomenon or cause-effect relationship, formulated to be tested
- **Research Hypothesis**: A predictive statement relating an independent variable to a dependent variable, meant to be tested objectively

### Common Mistakes
- Stating a hypothesis that is not testable — it must be verifiable/falsifiable
- Assuming all research needs a hypothesis — exploratory and descriptive research can proceed without one

### Quick Recall
> **Deductive**: Theory first → test  
> **Inductive**: Observations first → build theory

## Section: Nature, Sources and Types of Data 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Data forms the empirical backbone of any research. It can be primary or secondary, and comes in three structural forms: time series, cross-section, and pooled.

### In Simple Terms
Data = raw material for research. Primary data = you collect it yourself; Secondary data = someone else collected it.

### Key Concepts

#### Types of Data by Structure
| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Time Series** | Values of a variable at different time points | Daily prices, monthly CPI, quarterly GDP, annual budget |
| **Cross-Section** | Data on one or more variables at a single point in time | Census of population at one date |
| **Pooled Data** | Elements of both time series and cross-section combined | State-level savings, investment, GDP from 2000–2013 |
| **Panel/Longitudinal (Micro Panel)** | Special pooled data: same cross-section unit surveyed over time | Same household surveyed every year |

#### Sources of Data
| Source | Description |
|---|---|
| **Primary Data** | Collected by researcher via field work — questionnaire, schedule, interview, participant observation, focus groups |
| **Secondary Data** | Already collected by an agency; CSO, NSSO, RBI, Labour Bureau, IMF, private organisations |

### Definitions
- **Primary Data**: Data collected directly by the investigator/researcher through fieldwork
- **Secondary Data**: Data already collected by a government/international/private agency and available in published form
- **Time Series Data**: A set of observations on values a variable takes at different times
- **Cross-Section Data**: Data on one or more variables collected at the same point in time
- **Pooled Data**: Combination of time series and cross-section data

### Common Mistakes
- Treating Panel Data and Pooled Data as identical — Panel Data is a specific type of Pooled Data where the **same cross-sectional unit** is surveyed over time

### Quick Recall
> **Time Series** = same variable, different times  
> **Cross-Section** = multiple variables, same time  
> **Pooled** = both combined  
> **Panel** = same unit, tracked over time

## Section: Measurement Scales of Variables 🔴
<!-- Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH YIELD -->

### Core Idea
Variables can be measured at four levels (scales). The measurement scale determines which statistical technique is appropriate. Higher scales contain more information.

### In Simple Terms
Think of measurement scales as levels of precision — nominal is the least precise, ratio is the most precise.

### Key Concepts

#### Four Measurement Scales
| Scale | Description | Statistical Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | Categorical, no order or distance (e.g., yes/no, gender) | Mode, chi-square, cross-tabulation, logistic regression, factor analysis |
| **Ordinal** | Ranked/ordered, but no measurable distance between ranks (e.g., preference ranking: 1st, 2nd, 3rd) | Median, mode, rank order correlation, non-parametric methods |
| **Interval** | Ordered with measurable distances, but **no true zero** (e.g., temperature in °C, satisfaction scales 1–7) | Mean, SD, correlation, regression, ANOVA, factor analysis |
| **Ratio** | All properties of interval scale + **true zero** exists; ratios are meaningful (e.g., income, height, weight) | All interval scale techniques; additionally ratios (X1/X2) meaningful |

#### Properties Summary
| Property | Nominal | Ordinal | Interval | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Named categories | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Ordered | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Equal intervals | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |
| True zero | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |

### Definitions
- **Measurement**: Process of observing and recording collected observations
- **Nominal Scale**: Data recorded into unordered categories (yes/no, religion, gender)
- **Ordinal Scale**: Ranked data where order matters but distance between ranks is unknown
- **Interval Scale**: Ordered data with equal, measurable intervals but no absolute zero
- **Ratio Scale**: Highest level of measurement with true zero; both distance and ratio comparisons are meaningful

### Common Mistakes
- Confusing Interval and Ratio: Temperature in °C = Interval (0°C is not "no temperature"); Income = Ratio (₹0 means no income)
- Applying parametric tests (ANOVA, regression) to nominal or ordinal data without transformation

### Quick Recall
> **NOIR** → Nominal < Ordinal < Interval < Ratio (increasing precision)
> Only Ratio has a **true zero**; Only Interval and Ratio can use **mean & SD**

## Section: Key Words — Unit 1 Glossary 🟢
<!-- Exam importance: 🟢 CONTEXT -->

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Epistemology** | Theory of knowledge — how humans come to know what they know. Two types: Rationalism and Empiricism |
| **Rationalism** | Reliable knowledge derived from "pure" reason |
| **Empiricism** | Knowledge obtained only through direct sense-experience |
| **Ontology** | Branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of reality |
| **Induction** | Moving from particular statements to general statements — produces theory from data |
| **Deduction** | Moving from general statements to particular statements — tests hypothesis with data |
| **Abduction** | Two-stage: describe social activities/meanings → derive categories/concepts as basis for understanding |
| **Retroduction** | Going back from observed patterns to discover underlying structures and mechanisms that produce them |
| **Research Hypothesis** | Predictive statement relating an independent to a dependent variable; must be objectively verifiable |
| **Tabulation** | Arranging data in tabular form |
| **Coding** | System of symbols/letters used to categorise and transmit data |
