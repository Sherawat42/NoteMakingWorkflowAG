## Section: Content Analysis — Introduction & Historical Background 🟡

### Core Idea
Content analysis is a qualitative and quantitative research technique used by social scientists to analyse written, verbal, and nonverbal communication material. It evolved from basic word-counting in the 1920s–30s to sophisticated concept and semantic analysis by mid-20th century, and is now facilitated by modern computers.

> **In Simple Terms:** Content analysis is about turning text — newspapers, speeches, interviews, books — into measurable, analyzable data, so we can draw scientific inferences about what topics, themes, and attitudes are present.

### Key Concepts

#### Historical Timeline
- **1920s–30s**: Quantitative content analysis laid by Lazarsfield and Lasswell (USA) — primarily word counting from punch cards
- **1940s**: Adopted as formal research method
- **1952**: First textbook by **Berelson** — defined content analysis
- **Mid-1950s**: Shifted from word-counts → concept and semantic relationship analysis
- **1960s**: Spread to linguistics, psychology, sociology, history, arts
- **1952**: Kracauer criticized superficial quantification ignoring latent content → led to qualitative approaches
- **Today**: Modern computers have made content analysis faster and less error-prone

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Lazarsfield & Lasswell = founders of quantitative content analysis
> - Berelson (1952) = first textbook on content analysis
> - Shift: word frequency → concept mapping → semantic relationships

## Section: Content Analysis — Concept, Meaning & Characteristics 🔴

### Core Idea
Content analysis is a systematic, objective, and quantitative research technique for collecting and organizing information in a standardized format, allowing analysts to make inferences about the characteristics and meaning of written and other recorded materials.

> **In Simple Terms:** Reading text carefully is human — content analysis is scientific. It takes subjective reading and turns it into an objective, rule-based, replicable process of measurement across large amounts of text.

### Definitions
- **Content Analysis (Berelson)**: "A research technique for the objective, systematic, and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Analysis (Paisley)**: "A phase of information processing in which communication content is transformed through objective and systematic application of categorization rules into data that can be summarized and compared." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Analysis (Neuendorf)**: "The systematic, objective, quantitative analysis of message characteristics."
- **Content Analysis (Stone, 1964)**: "Any procedure for answering the relative extent to which specified references, attitudes or themes permeate a given message or document."

### Three Core Characteristics of Content Analysis ⭐

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Objectivity** | Explicitly formulated rules allow two or more investigators to obtain the same results from the same documents — gives scientific standing |
| **Systematic** | Inclusion/exclusion of content follows consistently applied criteria of selection — eliminates arbitrary decisions |
| **Generality** | Findings must have theoretical relevance — purely descriptive information unrelated to other attributes is of little scientific value |

### Key Concepts

#### What Content Analysis Covers
- **Content**: Specific topics or themes in the material
- **Structure**: The form or formatting — e.g., whether an article is on the front page or buried inside (a structural question)

Content analysis is a **method of observation** — instead of observing people directly or interviewing them, the investigator analyses the communications they have produced.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 characteristics: Objectivity + Systematic + Generality
> - Covers both content (themes) and structure (form)
> - Answers "What?" questions, not "Why?" questions

## Section: Terms Used in Content Analysis 🔴

### Core Idea
Several specific technical terms frame the mechanics of content analysis. Understanding these terms — manifest vs. latent, coding, content units, coding schemes — is essential to designing and executing a content analysis study.

> **In Simple Terms:** These are the specialist vocabulary of content analysis — the "grammar" that makes it a replicable scientific method rather than subjective interpretation.

### Definitions

- **Manifest Content Analysis**: Simply counting words, phrases, or "surface" features of the text. Provides reliable quantitative data that can be analyzed using inferential statistics. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Latent Content Analysis**: Interpreting the **underlying meaning** of the text. Requires clearly stated hypothesis about what is being measured; depends on researcher's ability to expose previously unmarked themes, messages, and cultural values. Widely used in qualitative content analysis. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Units**: Two types:
  - **Unit of Analysis**: The general idea or phenomenon being studied
  - **Unit of Observation**: The specific item measured at an individual level

| Type | Focus |
|---|---|
| Manifest | Surface features, word counts, explicit content |
| Latent | Underlying meaning, themes, cultural values |

- **Coding**: The process whereby raw data are systematically transformed and aggregated into units which permit precise description of relevant content characteristics. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Deductive Measurement**: Coding categories developed *before* analysis begins — useful when hypothesis or categories are established.
- **Inductive Measurement (Emergent Coding)**: Coding categories *emerge* during the analysis — useful in exploratory content analysis.
- **Coding Scheme**: The organized system of all coding categories (also called coding book) within which all content instances are analyzed.
- **Master Code Book**: Provides coders with explicit instructions; defines each word/phrase/aspect to be analyzed.
- **Code Sheet**: Form on which coders note every instance of every word/phrase/aspect being analyzed.
- **Coding Dictionary**: Used with computer-based content analysis; a set of words/phrases used as basis for text search.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating manifest and latent analysis interchangeably. → ✅ Correct: Manifest = surface/explicit; Latent = underlying/implicit meaning requiring interpretation.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Manifest → counting surface features; Latent → interpreting hidden meaning
> - Deductive coding = pre-set categories; Inductive = emergent categories
> - Coding Scheme contains: Master Code Book + Code Sheet + Coding Dictionary

## Section: Approaches of Content Analysis 🔴

### Core Idea
Content analysis is not a single technique but a collection of approaches. The two major approaches are **Conceptual (Thematic)** and **Relational (Semantic)** content analysis, with relational analysis further subdivided into affect extraction, proximity analysis, and cognitive mapping.

> **In Simple Terms:** Conceptual analysis asks "How often does a theme appear?" while Relational analysis asks "How do themes connect and relate to each other?"

### Definitions
- **Conceptual Content Analysis**: Choosing a concept for examination and quantifying/tallying its presence in the text. Also known as **thematic analysis**. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Relational Content Analysis**: Begins with identifying concepts, then explores the **relationships between** those concepts. Also termed **semantic analysis**. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Key Concepts: Types of Relational Content Analysis

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Affect Extraction** | Emotional evaluation of concepts explicit in a text — explores emotional/psychological state of speaker/writer |
| **Proximity Analysis** | Co-occurrence of explicit concepts in text — uses a scanning "window" of words — creates a concept co-occurrence matrix |
| **Cognitive Mapping** | Represents relationships visually as a graphic map — allows comparison of semantic connections across texts and time ("map analysis") |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking conceptual analysis examines relationships between concepts. → ✅ Correct: Conceptual analysis only tallies presence/frequency; it is relational analysis that explores connections between concepts.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Conceptual = thematic = presence/frequency
> - Relational = semantic = relationships between concepts
> - Relational subtypes: Affect Extraction → Proximity Analysis → Cognitive Mapping

## Section: Procedure of Content Analysis — Steps 1 to 4 🔴

### Core Idea
Content analysis follows a six-step procedure that converts raw text into scientific data. The first four steps — formulating research questions, determining materials, developing categories, and selecting units of analysis — build the architectural framework of the study.

> **In Simple Terms:** Before you analyze anything, you need to know exactly what you're asking, what texts to analyze, how to sort them, and what the smallest measurable piece of information is.

### Mechanisms / Processes: Six Steps in Content Analysis
1. Formulate research questions / objectives
2. Determine materials (sources) to be included
3. Develop content categories
4. Select units of analysis
5. Code the materials
6. Analyze and interpret the results

### Step 1: Formulation of Research Questions
- Content analysis answers **"What?"** but **NOT "Why?"** questions ⭐
- The research question guides which aspects of content are relevant
- Must be based on clear understanding of research needs and available data

### Step 2: Determining Materials
- Any recorded material can be studied: TV programmes, movies, newspapers, books, journal articles, letters, speeches, photos, regulations, case studies, survey responses
- Sampling may be required if population is too large — simple random, interval, cluster, or multistage sampling methods

### Step 3: Developing Content Categories
- Categories reflect the formulated thinking, hypotheses, and purpose of the study
- "Content analysis stands or falls by its categories" — **Berelson (1952)** ⭐

Three characteristics of content categories (Chadwick et al., 1984):
1. **Exhaustive**: All relevant items in material can be placed in a category
2. **Mutually Exclusive**: No item can be coded in more than one category
3. **Independent**: Recording one unit's category doesn't affect another's

Category formats: Groupings, Scales (rank ordering), Matrices (presence/absence + more)

### Step 4: Selecting Units of Analysis
The smallest unit of content coded under a content category.

| Unit Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Word** | Smallest unit; Lasswell calls it "symbol" — includes word compounds/phrases |
| **Theme** | A single assertion about a subject — most useful unit since it captures issues, values, beliefs, attitudes |
| **Character** | Fictional or historical character as recording unit |
| **Item** | Entire natural unit — a full speech, radio programme, news story |
| **Space/Time Measure** | Physical division — column inch, line, paragraph, minutes |

Three methods of enumeration for units:
- **Frequency**: How often something occurs
- **Direction**: The direction of messages (positive/negative/supporting/opposed)
- **Intensity**: Strength or power of a message in a given direction

### Connections
- Builds on: Grounded Theory — inductive vs. deductive coding (Chunk 002)
- Prerequisite for: Step 5: Coding the Materials (Chunk 004)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Content analysis answers "What?" NOT "Why?"
> - Categories must be: Exhaustive + Mutually Exclusive + Independent
> - Units: Word, Theme, Character, Item, Space/Time
> - Enumeration: Frequency, Direction, Intensity
