## Section: Case Study Method (continued) 🔴
<!-- See chunk 001 for start of this section -->

### Core Idea
Case study research is an empirical inquiry investigating a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context, particularly when boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident. It is holistic, inductive, and idiographic — taking complete account of an organization from all perspectives, with theorizing moving from general to specific.

> **In Simple Terms:** A case study is a 360° deep-dive into one organization or phenomenon, using multiple evidence sources (interviews, records, observation) to understand it from every stakeholder's angle.

### Definitions
- **Case Study**: "An empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context; when the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident and in which multiple sources of evidence are used." (Yin, 1984) ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
Six steps in conducting a case study:
1. Determine and define the research question
2. Select the cases and determine data gathering and analysis techniques
3. Prepare to collect the data
4. Collect data in the field
5. Evaluate and analyse the data
6. Prepare the report

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Generalizations drawn for one organization are **not necessarily applicable** to others — each organization is a distinct unit.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Case study = holistic, inductive, idiographic
> - Multiple evidence sources used simultaneously
> - 6-step process — must memorize sequence

## Section: Narratives 🟡

### Core Idea
Social science research is increasingly moving from a positivist (scientific) position to a narrative approach. Narrative research acknowledges that different respondents interpret the same questions differently, so standardized tools lose both meaning and plurality of responses.

> **In Simple Terms:** People don't answer identically to the same question — their answers are shaped by personal context. Narrative research embraces this variation rather than forcing answers into boxes.

### Key Concepts

#### Narrative vs. Structured Research
In structured interviews, standardized questions assume uniform meanings — but people interpret language differently. Narrative research recognizes that while there may be standardized questions, there are **no 'standardized meanings'**. It values plurality of responses and allows subjects' individual interpretations to surface.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Narrative research: standardized questions, no standardized meanings
> - From positivist (50s-80s) to narrative approach — a paradigm shift in social sciences

## Section: Focus Group Discussion 🔴

### Core Idea
Focus Group Discussion (FGD) is a qualitative method in which a small group of targeted audience has a frank, moderated discussion on a defined topic. It is frequently used in market surveys, evaluation studies, and media and health research.

> **In Simple Terms:** Gather 6–12 relevant people in a room, let them discuss a topic freely while a moderator gently guides, and the natural group conversation reveals insights no individual interview could.

### Definitions
- **Focus Group Discussion**: "A carefully planned discussion designed to obtain perceptions on a defined area of interest in a permissive, non-threatening environment." (Kruger, 1988) ⭐ (exam-important)

### Key Concepts

#### When to Use FGD
Bloor et al. (2001) caution that FGDs are the method of choice only when the purpose is *"to study group norms, group meanings and group processes"* (cf. Barbour, 2008). They should not be used as a default replacement for individual interviews.

### Mechanisms / Processes (Steps for FGD)
1. Topic must be of **general interest** to all participants
2. Number of participants can vary — usually **6–8** (market researchers prefer 10–12)
3. Use **stimulus material** (video clips, leaflets, cartoons) to initiate free-flowing discussion
4. Moderator manages discussion without being intrusive
5. **Anticipate the analysis** through attentive moderating (Barbour, 2008)
6. Locate **themes** through repetitive patterning of responses
7. Consider **second stage sampling** to further explore emerging theories or trends

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Using FGDs for all qualitative research situations. → ✅ Correct: FGDs are best suited specifically for studying group norms, meanings, and processes.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Kruger's definition is exam-critical
> - FGD group size: 6–8 (research); 10–12 (market research)
> - 7-step process for conducting FGDs

## Section: Grounded Theory 🔴

### Core Idea
Proposed by Glaser and Strauss (1967), grounded theory is a method of inductive theorizing where theories are generated from data as research proceeds, rather than being tested against pre-set hypotheses. Researchers go to the field first, explore, and let the theory emerge from empirical evidence.

> **In Simple Terms:** Don't go to the field with an answer you want to prove — go with open eyes, collect evidence, and let the theory build itself from what you find.

### Definitions
- **Grounded Theory**: A method of inductive theorizing (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) in which "good theory is systematically discovered from and verified with the data of social research." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Substantive Theories**: Grounded theories located in a specific context and related to a specific process.
- **Formal Theories**: Grounded theories at a higher level of generalization, applicable to multiple substantive areas.

### Key Concepts

#### Inductive vs. Deductive Approach
In quantitative research, hypotheses are pre-set and tested. In grounded theory, "research… is not a pre-planned linear process of testing hypotheses, but rather an evolving process in which what has been 'discovered' at any point will determine what happens next." (Blaikie, 2010)

| Feature | Quantitative Research | Grounded Theory |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Pre-set hypothesis | Field exploration |
| Theory | Tested | Generated |
| Process | Linear | Evolving/iterative |
| Analysis | After data collection | Continuous with data |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Grounded theory = testing a pre-formed hypothesis. → ✅ Correct: Grounded theory generates theory inductively from empirical evidence, not validates prior assumptions.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Glaser & Strauss (1967) — grounded theory originators
> - Theory emerges from data, not imposed upon it
> - Two types: Substantive (specific) and Formal (generalizable)

## Section: Data Sources and Sampling 🔴

### Core Idea
Participatory researchers use three types of data sources — primary, secondary, and tertiary — and rely predominantly on non-probability sampling methods suited for qualitative inquiry where smaller, resource-intensive samples are the norm.

> **In Simple Terms:** Unlike massive surveys, qualitative research uses small, purposefully selected samples — quality over quantity.

### Definitions
- **Primary Data**: Data collected and analysed by the researcher(s) working on the project.
- **Secondary Data**: Data already collected by other researchers, agencies, or institutions, available in public domain (e.g. Census, NSS, NCAER, Economic Survey). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Tertiary Data**: Analysed data by researchers/agencies whose results are available for comparison or reinterpretation.

### Key Concepts: Non-Probability Sampling Methods (used by participatory researchers)

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Accidental/Convenience Sampling** | Interviewing any individual without keeping research population in focus; used only when no other method is feasible |
| **Quota Sampling** | Selecting respondents based on certain criteria to represent population characteristics |
| **Judgemental/Purposive Sampling** | Studying a pre-identified sub-set of population (e.g. organizations with successful management) |
| **Snowball Sampling** | Chain referral — a key respondent leads to others with similar characteristics |

### Definitions (Sampling)
- **Snowball Sampling**: A technique for gathering subjects through an initial subject who provides names of other actors. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Quota Sampling**: Selecting respondents based on demographic/criterion-based quotas to represent the population.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Expecting participatory sampling to be representative in the probabilistic sense. → ✅ Correct: Qualitative research is resource-intensive and intentionally uses smaller, non-representative samples.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 3 data types: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary
> - 4 non-probability methods: Convenience, Quota, Purposive, Snowball
> - Snowball = chain referral / network-based

## Section: Subjectivity & Transcription 🟡

### Core Idea
Qualitative researchers openly acknowledge subjectivity in their work, recognizing that researcher involvement shapes data interpretation. Transcription — rewriting field data — is a critical and often difficult step in making qualitative data ready for analysis.

> **In Simple Terms:** Being honest about your own biases actually makes the research more trustworthy, not less. And turning rough field notes into readable text is harder than it sounds.

### Definitions
- **Subjectivity**: The acknowledged influence of a researcher's own perspective on how they collect and interpret interview data. (Hollway and Jefferson, 2000)
- **Transcription**: Rewriting data procured from the field (from memory, paper, audio, or video recordings) for analysis purposes.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Qualitative researchers don't deny subjectivity — they acknowledge and work with it
> - Transcription is one of the most difficult steps in qualitative research

## Section: Analysis of Qualitative Data 🔴

### Core Idea
Qualitative data analysis is iterative, not linear. Unlike quantitative methods where analysis follows data collection, qualitative analysis proceeds simultaneously with data generation. It uses a **constant comparative method** — continuously comparing and contrasting notes to derive meaning.

> **In Simple Terms:** You don't wait until all the data is in to start analysis — you're thinking and comparing as you go, like solving a puzzle while pieces are still arriving.

### Definitions
- **Constant Comparative Method**: A qualitative analysis approach involving constant comparing and contrasting of notes, focusing on "who is saying what and in what context." ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes (Steps for Qualitative Data Analysis)
1. Compilation of field notes and observations from field diary
2. Verbatim transcription OR indexed recordings and notes (researcher's choice)
3. Interrogation of data — diligence is key to reliable generalizations
4. Develop **codes and themes** to capture meaning
5. Apply **grounded theory** for in-field concepts developed by respondents
6. Computer or manual analysis
7. Software: **NUD*IST**, **ATLAS/TI** (computer-aided qualitative analysis tools)

### Key Concepts
- Qualitative data doesn't produce "results" — it produces **"findings"**
- Process is **iterative** (recurring) rather than being linear like quantitative research

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Qualitative analysis = iterative, not linear
> - Constant comparative method = core technique
> - Computer tools: NUD*IST, ATLAS/TI

## Section: Report Writing in Qualitative Research 🟡

### Core Idea
Richardson (2000) describes writing as "a method of inquiry" itself — not merely documentation. Qualitative research reports differ in style from quantitative ones: they often use the subtitle "findings" instead of "results" and allow more flexible writing formats.

> **In Simple Terms:** Your report isn't just a container for data — the act of writing it actually helps you discover and make sense of what you found.

### Mechanisms / Processes
Standard format: Introduction/Background → Methods → Sample → Results → Discussion → Conclusion/Recommendations

- In qualitative research: **"Findings"** often combines Discussion and Conclusion as the data is iterative
- Writing style varies based on audience: project agency, journal article, or dissertation/thesis
- For indexed journals: must conform to journal's specific writing and referencing style

## Section: Criticisms of Participatory Methods 🔴

### Core Idea
Participatory research faces significant criticism: personal bias affects objectivity, findings are restricted to small areas and may not generalize, methods are slow, studies appear impressionistic, and results are sometimes labelled "soft" and unreliable.

> **In Simple Terms:** Critics say participatory research is too personal, too local, too slow, and too "fuzzy" — it lacks the objectivity demanded by scientific (positivist) standards.

### Key Concepts: Main Criticisms
1. **Personal bias** impacts objectivity
2. **Small area** restriction — findings may not be generalized elsewhere
3. Methods are **slow** compared to quantitative surveys
4. Studies are considered **impressionistic and unreliable**
5. Called a **"soft" method** by positivist critics

### Edge Cases & Caveats
- Many researchers address limitations by adopting a **mixed methods approach** (blending quantitative and qualitative) — details covered in Unit 6.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Key criticisms: bias, limited generalizability, slow, impressionistic, "soft"
> - Solution proposed: mixed methods approach

## Section: Advantages of Participatory Research 🔴

### Core Idea
Cornwell & Jewkes (1995) summarize the three key advantages of participatory research as: **Reflexive, Flexible, and Iterative**. These attributes make it uniquely suited for capturing genuine field realities that standardized quantitative methods miss.

> **In Simple Terms:** Participatory research is like a skilled detective who observes, adapts questions as clues emerge, and revisits leads — as opposed to a rigid checklist-follower who only sees what the form allows.

### Definitions
- **Reflexivity**: Researcher's ability to take stock of their own biases, prejudices, and reservations — and work through them to record objective responses. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Iterative**: The ability to repeatedly seek answers to the same query in different ways to validate responses; repetitive patterning of responses builds generalizations.
- **'Bottom-up' approach**: Participatory research explores people's felt needs locally, and suggests policies for meeting those real needs — contrasted with "trickle down" planning.

### Key Concepts

#### Reflexivity
Qualitative researchers are the primary instrument of data collection. Hence, reflexivity — acknowledging and processing one's subjective involvement — is essential (Diane Watt, 2007). Quantitative research dismisses subjectivity as "sampling error"; qualitative research consciously admits and manages it.

#### Being vs. Trickle-Down
Prior to participatory approaches, planning used the 'trickle down effect' — experts plan for the rich, expecting benefits to filter down. Participatory approach reverses this: **start with local people's felt needs**, then plan interventions. Example: Housing projects for tribal communities failed because they weren't designed around tribal lifestyles.

#### Ethical Obligations
Good participatory research requires:
- Respecting confidentiality of respondents
- Refraining from encroaching on privacy
- Respecting distinct traditions and cultural values
- Conducting interviews without argument or offence
- Never disclosing respondents' name/location in the report
- Seeking informed consent before interviewing

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking reflexivity weakens research by admitting bias. → ✅ Correct: Reflexivity is a strength — it distinguishes qualitative methods by turning subjective awareness into methodological rigour.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Three advantages: Reflexive + Flexible + Iterative (Cornwell & Jewkes)
> - Reflexivity = being "subjectively objective"
> - Bottom-up vs. trickle-down planning approach
> - Key ethical rules: confidentiality, consent, no-disclosure
