### Participatory Research 🔴

**Data Generation vs. Data Collection**

**Ethnography and EMIC View**
- **Participatory Research**: A qualitative framework emphasizing direct interaction in generating data from an insider's perspective, preserving the voices and nuances of the respondents.
- **EMIC view**: An insider's perspective where the people being studied tell their stories and the researcher interprets them as "thick descriptions". ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Data generation**: The process of interactive data procurement where a researcher uses flexibility to reconstruct queries and generate plural responses. ⭐ (exam-important)

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking data collection and data generation are the same. → ✅ Correct: Data collection uses structured, rigid schedules, whereas data generation relies on interactive, flexible generation of meaning from subjects.

**Quick Recall:**
- Participatory research implies direct respondent-researcher interaction.
- Relies on Emic view (ethnography).
- Generates data flexibly instead of mere collection.

### Methods of Participatory Research: Observation Method 🔴

**Participant Observation**

**Non-Participant Observation**

**Quasi-Participant Observation**
- **Participant Observation**: A procedure where the investigator disguises themselves as a member of a group to record information without disturbing normal activities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Non-Participant Observation**: Observing individuals without actively participating, sometimes using video to capture people's gestures.
- **Quasi-Participant Observation**: Participating in some activities and observing others from outside to balance access and context. ⭐ (exam-important)

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing participant observation guarantees objectivity. → ✅ Correct: It's subjective and the researcher may become emotionally involved, leading to loss of objectivity.

**Quick Recall:**

- Quasi-participant is the versatile middle ground often used today.
- **Life History**: Reconstructing an individual's history using memories and diaries to understand the historical context and social processes. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Case Study Method 🔴

### Case Study Method (continued) 🔴
- **Case Study**: "An empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context; when the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident and in which multiple sources of evidence are used." (Yin, 1984) ⭐ (exam-important)
1. Determine and define the research question
2. Select the cases and determine data gathering and analysis techniques
3. Prepare to collect the data
4. Collect data in the field
5. Evaluate and analyse the data
6. Prepare the report
- Generalizations drawn for one organization are **not necessarily applicable** to others — each organization is a distinct unit.

**Quick Recall:**
- Case study = holistic, inductive, idiographic
- Multiple evidence sources used simultaneously
- 6-step process — must memorize sequence

**Quick Recall:**
- Narrative research: standardized questions, no standardized meanings

### Focus Group Discussion 🔴
- **Focus Group Discussion**: "A carefully planned discussion designed to obtain perceptions on a defined area of interest in a permissive, non-threatening environment." (Kruger, 1988) ⭐ (exam-important)

**When to Use FGD**
1. Topic must be of **general interest** to all participants
2. Number of participants can vary — usually **6–8** (market researchers prefer 10–12)
3. Use **stimulus material** (video clips, leaflets, cartoons) to initiate free-flowing discussion
4. Moderator manages discussion without being intrusive
5. **Anticipate the analysis** through attentive moderating (Barbour, 2008)
6. Locate **themes** through repetitive patterning of responses
7. Consider **second stage sampling** to further explore emerging theories or trends

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Using FGDs for all qualitative research situations. → ✅ Correct: FGDs are best suited specifically for studying group norms, meanings, and processes.

**Quick Recall:**
- Kruger's definition is exam-critical
- FGD group size: 6–8 (research); 10–12 (market research)
- 7-step process for conducting FGDs

### Grounded Theory 🔴
- **Grounded Theory**: A method of inductive theorizing (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) in which "good theory is systematically discovered from and verified with the data of social research." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Substantive Theories**: Grounded theories located in a specific context and related to a specific process.
- **Formal Theories**: Grounded theories at a higher level of generalization, applicable to multiple substantive areas.

**Inductive vs. Deductive Approach**
| Feature | Quantitative Research | Grounded Theory |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Pre-set hypothesis | Field exploration |
| Theory | Tested | Generated |
| Process | Linear | Evolving/iterative |
| Analysis | After data collection | Continuous with data |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Grounded theory = testing a pre-formed hypothesis. → ✅ Correct: Grounded theory generates theory inductively from empirical evidence, not validates prior assumptions.

**Quick Recall:**
- Glaser & Strauss (1967) — grounded theory originators
- Theory emerges from data, not imposed upon it
- Two types: Substantive (specific) and Formal (generalizable)

### Data Sources and Sampling 🔴
- **Primary Data**: Data collected and analysed by the researcher(s) working on the project.
- **Secondary Data**: Data already collected by other researchers, agencies, or institutions, available in public domain (e.g. Census, NSS, NCAER, Economic Survey). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Tertiary Data**: Analysed data by researchers/agencies whose results are available for comparison or reinterpretation.
| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Accidental/Convenience Sampling** | Interviewing any individual without keeping research population in focus; used only when no other method is feasible |
| **Quota Sampling** | Selecting respondents based on certain criteria to represent population characteristics |
| **Judgemental/Purposive Sampling** | Studying a pre-identified sub-set of population (e.g. organizations with successful management) |
| **Snowball Sampling** | Chain referral — a key respondent leads to others with similar characteristics |
- **Snowball Sampling**: A technique for gathering subjects through an initial subject who provides names of other actors. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Quota Sampling**: Selecting respondents based on demographic/criterion-based quotas to represent the population.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Expecting participatory sampling to be representative in the probabilistic sense. → ✅ Correct: Qualitative research is resource-intensive and intentionally uses smaller, non-representative samples.

**Quick Recall:**
- 3 data types: Primary, Secondary, Tertiary
- 4 non-probability methods: Convenience, Quota, Purposive, Snowball
- Snowball = chain referral / network-based

### Analysis of Qualitative Data 🔴
- **Constant Comparative Method**: A qualitative analysis approach involving constant comparing and contrasting of notes, focusing on "who is saying what and in what context." ⭐ (exam-important)
1. Compilation of field notes and observations from field diary
2. Verbatim transcription OR indexed recordings and notes (researcher's choice)
3. Interrogation of data — diligence is key to reliable generalizations
4. Develop **codes and themes** to capture meaning
5. Apply **grounded theory** for in-field concepts developed by respondents
6. Computer or manual analysis
7. Software: **NUD*IST**, **ATLAS/TI** (computer-aided qualitative analysis tools)
- Qualitative data doesn't produce "results" — it produces **"findings"**
- Process is **iterative** (recurring) rather than being linear like quantitative research

**Quick Recall:**
- Qualitative analysis = iterative, not linear
- Constant comparative method = core technique
- Computer tools: NUD*IST, ATLAS/TI

4. Studies are considered **impressionistic and unreliable**
5. Called a **"soft" method** by positivist critics

### Content Analysis — Concept, Meaning & Characteristics 🔴
- **Content Analysis (Berelson)**: "A research technique for the objective, systematic, and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Analysis (Paisley)**: "A phase of information processing in which communication content is transformed through objective and systematic application of categorization rules into data that can be summarized and compared." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Analysis (Neuendorf)**: "The systematic, objective, quantitative analysis of message characteristics."
- **Content Analysis (Stone, 1964)**: "Any procedure for answering the relative extent to which specified references, attitudes or themes permeate a given message or document."
| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Objectivity** | Explicitly formulated rules allow two or more investigators to obtain the same results from the same documents — gives scientific standing |
| **Systematic** | Inclusion/exclusion of content follows consistently applied criteria of selection — eliminates arbitrary decisions |
| **Generality** | Findings must have theoretical relevance — purely descriptive information unrelated to other attributes is of little scientific value |

**What Content Analysis Covers**
- **Content**: Specific topics or themes in the material
- **Structure**: The form or formatting — e.g., whether an article is on the front page or buried inside (a structural question)

**Quick Recall:**
- 3 characteristics: Objectivity + Systematic + Generality
- Covers both content (themes) and structure (form)
- Answers "What?" questions, not "Why?" questions

### Terms Used in Content Analysis 🔴
- **Manifest Content Analysis**: Simply counting words, phrases, or "surface" features of the text. Provides reliable quantitative data that can be analyzed using inferential statistics. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Latent Content Analysis**: Interpreting the **underlying meaning** of the text. Requires clearly stated hypothesis about what is being measured; depends on researcher's ability to expose previously unmarked themes, messages, and cultural values. Widely used in qualitative content analysis. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Content Units**: Two types:
  - **Unit of Analysis**: The general idea or phenomenon being studied
  - **Unit of Observation**: The specific item measured at an individual level
| Type | Focus |
|---|---|
| Manifest | Surface features, word counts, explicit content |
| Latent | Underlying meaning, themes, cultural values |
- **Coding**: The process whereby raw data are systematically transformed and aggregated into units which permit precise description of relevant content characteristics. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Deductive Measurement**: Coding categories developed *before* analysis begins — useful when hypothesis or categories are established.
- **Inductive Measurement (Emergent Coding)**: Coding categories *emerge* during the analysis — useful in exploratory content analysis.
- **Coding Scheme**: The organized system of all coding categories (also called coding book) within which all content instances are analyzed.
- **Master Code Book**: Provides coders with explicit instructions; defines each word/phrase/aspect to be analyzed.
- **Code Sheet**: Form on which coders note every instance of every word/phrase/aspect being analyzed.
- **Coding Dictionary**: Used with computer-based content analysis; a set of words/phrases used as basis for text search.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Treating manifest and latent analysis interchangeably. → ✅ Correct: Manifest = surface/explicit; Latent = underlying/implicit meaning requiring interpretation.

**Quick Recall:**
- Manifest → counting surface features; Latent → interpreting hidden meaning
- Deductive coding = pre-set categories; Inductive = emergent categories
- Coding Scheme contains: Master Code Book + Code Sheet + Coding Dictionary
---

### Approaches of Content Analysis 🔴
- **Conceptual Content Analysis**: Choosing a concept for examination and quantifying/tallying its presence in the text. Also known as **thematic analysis**. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Relational Content Analysis**: Begins with identifying concepts, then explores the **relationships between** those concepts. Also termed **semantic analysis**. ⭐ (exam-important)
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Affect Extraction** | Emotional evaluation of concepts explicit in a text — explores emotional/psychological state of speaker/writer |
| **Proximity Analysis** | Co-occurrence of explicit concepts in text — uses a scanning "window" of words — creates a concept co-occurrence matrix |
| **Cognitive Mapping** | Represents relationships visually as a graphic map — allows comparison of semantic connections across texts and time ("map analysis") |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking conceptual analysis examines relationships between concepts. → ✅ Correct: Conceptual analysis only tallies presence/frequency; it is relational analysis that explores connections between concepts.

**Quick Recall:**
- Conceptual = thematic = presence/frequency
- Relational = semantic = relationships between concepts
- Relational subtypes: Affect Extraction → Proximity Analysis → Cognitive Mapping

### Procedure of Content Analysis — Steps 1 to 4 🔴
1. Formulate research questions / objectives
2. Determine materials (sources) to be included
3. Develop content categories
4. Select units of analysis
5. Code the materials
6. Analyze and interpret the results
- Content analysis answers **"What?"** but **NOT "Why?"** questions ⭐
- The research question guides which aspects of content are relevant
- Must be based on clear understanding of research needs and available data
- Any recorded material can be studied: TV programmes, movies, newspapers, books, journal articles, letters, speeches, photos, regulations, case studies, survey responses
- Sampling may be required if population is too large — simple random, interval, cluster, or multistage sampling methods
- Categories reflect the formulated thinking, hypotheses, and purpose of the study
- "Content analysis stands or falls by its categories" — **Berelson (1952)** ⭐
1. **Exhaustive**: All relevant items in material can be placed in a category
2. **Mutually Exclusive**: No item can be coded in more than one category
3. **Independent**: Recording one unit's category doesn't affect another's
| Unit Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Word** | Smallest unit; Lasswell calls it "symbol" — includes word compounds/phrases |
| **Theme** | A single assertion about a subject — most useful unit since it captures issues, values, beliefs, attitudes |
| **Character** | Fictional or historical character as recording unit |
| **Item** | Entire natural unit — a full speech, radio programme, news story |
| **Space/Time Measure** | Physical division — column inch, line, paragraph, minutes |
- **Frequency**: How often something occurs
- **Direction**: The direction of messages (positive/negative/supporting/opposed)
- **Intensity**: Strength or power of a message in a given direction
- Builds on: Grounded Theory — inductive vs. deductive coding (Chunk 002)
- Prerequisite for: Step 5: Coding the Materials (Chunk 004)

**Quick Recall:**
- Content analysis answers "What?" NOT "Why?"
- Categories must be: Exhaustive + Mutually Exclusive + Independent
- Units: Word, Theme, Character, Item, Space/Time
- Enumeration: Frequency, Direction, Intensity

### Content Analysis — Steps 5 & 6 (Coding & Interpretation) 🔴
- **Coding**: Placing units of analysis into content categories
- Material can be coded manually or by computer
- Analyst writes **coding instructions** before beginning (Kripendorff, 1980), which include:
  - Definition of recording units
  - Procedures for identifying themes
  - Descriptions of variables and categories
  - Outline of cognitive procedures for placing data in categories
  - Instructions for data sheets
1. Categories are clearly specified and meet requirements
2. Coding instructions are adequate
3. Coders are suitable for coding
- Reliability among coders and consistency in individual decisions determines whether full coding begins
- **Absolute frequency**: Number of times statements/issues appear in the sample
- **Relative frequency**: Percentage of sample size
- **Cross-tabulation**: Examines co-occurrence of variables
- **Contingency analysis, Cluster analysis, Factor analysis**: Discovery of patterns in data
  - The intentions of those who created the text
  - The effects of messages on those who received them

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking content analysis can explain why writers said what they did. → ✅ Correct: Content analysis answers **"What?"** only — not "Why?" or "What effect?"

**Quick Recall:**
- Pre-testing before full coding is mandatory
- Frequency, cross-tabulation, contingency/cluster/factor analysis for interpretation
- Cannot reveal authors' intentions or audience effects

**Quick Recall:**
- Used in: media, economics, consumer research, political science, cultural studies
- Can study: TV, films, newspapers, books, journals, speeches, open-ended surveys, photographs

### Advantages and Disadvantages of Content Analysis 🔴
| # | Advantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Economy** — No large research staff or special equipment needed; cost-effective in time and money |
| 2 | **Correctable** — Easier to repeat portions of study than other methods; errors can be fixed |
| 3 | **Longitudinal** — Permits study of processes occurring over a long time |
| 4 | **Unobtrusive** — Has no effect on the subject being studied |
| 5 | **Objective account** — Presents events/themes not immediately apparent to casual readers |
| 6 | **Large volume** — Can handle large volumes of data; computers ease processing |
| # | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Limited to recorded communication** — oral, written, or graphic must be in accessible format |

- Key disadvantages: Recorded-only, Subjective risk, Validity issues, Descriptive only, Misses quality

### Action Research — Introduction & Historical Background 🔴

**Origins and Historical Background**
- **Kurt Lewin** is the pioneer of action research — work in 1940s focused on changing lives of disadvantaged groups (housing, employment, working conditions)
- Lewin coined the term **"action research"** in **1946** in his paper *"Action Research and Minority Problems"* ⭐
- He defined it as: *"A comparative research on the conditions and effect of various forms of social action and research leading to social action, using a process of a spiral steps, each of which is composed of a circle of planning, action and fact-finding about the result of the action."*
- **Eric Trist** — social psychiatrist, another major contributor; collaborated with Lewin on systematic change in organizations
- **John Collier** — suggested as alternate originator (Deshler & Ewart, 1995); used AR to improve race relations during WWII
- Immediately after Lewin, AR was seen as four different processes: diagnostic, participant, empirical, and experimental
- Builds on: Participatory Method (Unit 18, Chunk 001–002)
- Contrasts with: Positivist/post-positivist quantitative research
- Rooted in: **Critical theory paradigm** (alongside emancipatory research)

**Quick Recall:**
- AR = knowing through doing → social action → change
- Kurt Lewin coined "action research" in 1946
- AR = research + practice integrated, collaborative between researcher and practitioner

### Definitions of Action Research 🔴
| Scholar | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Frost (2002)** | "Process of systematic reflection, enquiry and action carried out by individuals about their own professional practice." |
| **Hopkins (1985)** | Combination of action and research rendering action as disciplined inquiry to understand, improve and reform practice. |
| **Ebbutt (1985)** | Systematic study combining action and reflection with intention of improving practice. |
| **Corey (1953)** | Process in which practitioners study problems scientifically to evaluate, improve and steer decision-making and practice. |
| **Kemmis & McTaggart (1992)** | To plan, act, observe and reflect more carefully, systematically, and rigorously than in everyday life. |
| **Dick (2002)** | "A flexible spiral process which allows action (change, improvement) and research (understanding, knowledge) to be achieved at the same time." |
| **Cohen & Manion (1980)** | "Essentially an on the spot procedure designed to deal with a concrete problem located in an immediate situation." |
| **Kemmis & McTaggart (1988)** ⭐ | "A form of collective self-reflective enquiry undertaken by participants in social situations in order to improve the rationality and justice of the own social or educational practices." |
- **Dual commitment**: Study a system AND simultaneously collaborate to change it
- **Co-learning**: Active collaboration between researchers and clients as primary aspect
- **Catalytic role**: Research produces new dynamic and concrete changes
- **Dissolves researcher–researchee separation**: Affected persons are not passive; they analyze their own situations

### Principles of Action Research 🔴
| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Reflective Critical Analysis** | Becoming aware of our own perceptual biases; ensuring practical descriptions give rise to theoretical considerations by making interpretations, biases, and assumptions explicit |
| **2. Dialectical Critical Analysis** | Understanding relationships between elements that make up phenomena in context; social reality is validated through language; focus on unstable/opposing elements most likely to create change |
| **3. Collaborative Resource** | Each person's ideas are equally significant as potential resources; participants are co-researchers; everyone's view contributes to understanding the situation |
| **4. Risk** | Understanding taken-for-granted processes and willingness to submit them to critique; initiator assures participants that everyone undergoes the same process |
| **5. Plural Structure** | Developing multiple accounts and critiques rather than a single authoritative interpretation; multiple views, commentaries, and analyses lead to multiple possible actions |
| **6. Theory, Practice & Transformation** | Theory informs practice and practice refines theory in a continuous cycle; single change process has two intertwined aspects — theory and practice continuously alternating |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking AR arrives at a single, authoritative conclusion. → ✅ Correct: The plural structure principle deliberately maintains multiple interpretations — a report is support for ongoing discussion, not a final conclusion.

**Quick Recall:**
- 6 principles: Reflective + Dialectical + Collaborative + Risk + Plural Structure + Theory-Practice-Transformation
- Collaborative = all participants are co-researchers
- Plural Structure = many interpretations, ongoing discussion, not a final fact

### Characteristics of Action Research 🔴
1. **Collaborative**: Everyone's view is a contribution; practitioners from similar contexts can collaborate
2. **Systems planning/restructuring**: Helps identify systemic problems and redesign processes (e.g. changing classroom seating to fix concentration issues)
3. **Small-scale intervention**: Narrowly focused; may not be applicable to others
- 4. **Contextual**: Specific to a particular class, school, or situation; results cannot be generalized ⭐
5. **Enhances competencies**: Gives practitioners clearer vision of problematic situations
6. **Improves human relationships**: Seeks better quality of human interaction
7. **Understands complex social situations**: Schools, communities, organizations
8. **Identifies remedial measures**: Specific to the situation being studied
9. **Systematic and scientific but not very rigorous**

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Expecting action research results to be generalizable across contexts. → ✅ Correct: AR is inherently contextual — results are specific to the setting and cannot be generalized.

**Quick Recall:**
- Cannot generalize — contextual and specific
- Participatory + collaborative at its core
- Mixes quantitative and qualitative methods
- Documentation and reflection are ongoing

### Models of Action Research 🔴
| Model | Key Feature |
|---|---|
| **Kemmis & McTaggart's Spiral Model** | Self-reflective cycles: Plan → Act → Observe → Reflect → Re-plan. Fluid, open, responsive — stages overlap in practice |
| **Elliot's Action Research Model** | Based on Lewin's work; includes: General idea → Reconnaissance (fact finding) → Planning → Action → Evaluation → Amend plan → Next action step |
| **O'Leary's Cycles of Action Research** | Cyclic process; cycles converge toward better understanding; experiential learning approach; continually refines methods, data, and interpretation |
| **Stringer's Interacting Spiral Model** | "Simple yet powerful" — Look, Think, Act routine; each stage leads to the next |
| **Lewin's Action Research Spiral** | Fact finding → Planning → Taking action → Evaluating → Amending the plan → Second action step |
| **Calhoun's Action Research Cycle** | Cyclical but not spiral-shaped; solid lines = primary direction; dotted lines = back-and-forth movement for refinement |
| **Bachman's Action Research Spiral** | Downward spiral — gather info, plan, observe/evaluate, reflect, plan new cycle |
| **Riel's Progressive Problem Solving** | Four steps per cycle: Planning → Taking action → Collecting evidence → Reflecting |
| **Hendricks's Action Research Model** | School-based context; focuses on Acting → Evaluating → Reflecting |
| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **i) Planning** | Identify issue; seek information from similar projects; develop research questions and plan |
| **ii) Acting** | Trial the change; collect and compile evidence; question the process and make changes |
| **iii) Observing** | Analyse evidence; discuss with co-researchers; write report; share findings with stakeholders |
| **iv) Reflecting** | Evaluate the cycle; implement findings or new strategy; revisit the process |
- AR is cyclical — progress in small chunks
- Heavily based on **critical reflection**
- Wide range of data collection methods — limit to manageable number
- Participants must have meaningful roles in data collection and presentation
- Not every cycle will be complete — may need to stop mid-cycle and start fresh
- Flexibility means cycles may be truncated as new ways emerge
- Builds on: Grounded Theory — iterative, evolving processes (Chunk 002)
- Builds on: Action Research definition by Kemmis & McTaggart (Chunk 004)

**Quick Recall:**
- 9 models but 4 universal stages: Planning → Acting → Observing → Reflecting
- Most associated with Kemmis & McTaggart's Spiral Model
- Lewin coined AR (1946); his spiral remains foundational
- Key match: Kemmis & McTaggart = Spiral Model; Stringer = Interacting Spiral; O'Leary = Cycles; Riel = Progressive Problem Solving

### Steps Involved in Action Research 🔴
- Need arises from perceived dissatisfaction with an existing situation
- Focus questions: *What is the cause of the problem? Why is it happening? What can I do? Which steps can I take?*
- Choose a problem that is meaningful and solvable within stipulated time
- Prerequisites: Identifying the problem clearly before beginning
- Identify and state probable causes in concise, unambiguous terms
- Find causes underlying the problem and related issues
- State research questions clearly
- Develop a tentative theory (propositions) about the problem's genesis
- Establish conceptual and functional relationships to understand the situation
- **Step 4: Data Collection** ⭐
- Most important step — determines what action is needed
- Sources of data:
  - Videotapes and audio tapes
  - Report cards
  - Attendance records, student work samples, projects, performances
  - Interviews with parents, students, teachers
  - Cumulative and anecdotal records
  - School diaries
  - Photos
  - Questionnaires
  - Focus group discussions
  - Checklists
  - Observation schedules
- Data is arranged by gender, classroom, grade level, school, etc.
- Purposive samples used for larger groups
- Review qualitative data for common themes → summarize in table formats
- Analyze quantitative data with simple statistics: percentages, frequency tables, descriptive statistics
- Data is converted into **information** helpful for decision-making
- This stage requires maximum time
- Identifies important patterns and emerging trends
- Review current literature relevant to the findings
- Three steps in literature review:
  1. Identify topics related to study area
  2. Gather research reports, books, and videotapes relating to the problem
  3. Organise materials to draw inferences in light of AR results
- Suggest one action at a time; observe its outcome before suggesting next
- Well-informed decision-making is the goal

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Suggesting multiple actions simultaneously in AR. → ✅ Correct: Suggest one action at a time to isolate and clearly observe its effects on the situation.

**Quick Recall:**
- 6 steps: Identify → Formulate → Research Questions → Data Collection → Analysis → Discussion/Evaluation
- Data collection = most important step
- Step 5 = maximum time needed
- Suggest one action at a time!

### Advantages and Disadvantages of Action Research 🔴
| # | Advantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Workplace/community applicable** — Practitioners (agents of change) can use it as part of their normal activities |
| 2 | **Maximises conscious learning** — The AR cycle is also a learning cycle; systematic reflection is effective practitioner development |
| 3 | **Participative and ethical** — Partnership with clients; more ethically satisfying; occupationally relevant |
| 4 | **Helps determine policy** — Useful for developing policy related to identified problems |
| # | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | **More difficult to execute** — Researcher responsible for change AND for research; more work to set up; no credit for setup work |
| 2 | **Counter-cultural** — Doesn't conform to expectations of some examiners; deliberately ignores some conventional research requirements |
| 3 | **Difficult to learn** — Researcher must learn AR methodology almost from scratch; limited exposure in mainstream research training |
| 4 | **More demanding library work** — Relevant literature is defined by the data collected, not known in advance; first collect data → then find literature |
| 5 | **Difficult to report** — Especially for thesis purposes; must justify overall AR approach, not just findings |

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Thinking the literature review in AR is done before data collection. → ✅ Correct: In most forms of AR, you collect data *first* and then go to the literature to challenge your findings (Step 6).

**Quick Recall:**
- Key advantage: Practitioners learn consciously from their own practice cycles
- Key disadvantage: Counter-cultural, harder to report, literature work is demanding
- AR literature review = after data collection (unlike conventional research)
