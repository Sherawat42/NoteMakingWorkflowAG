### 13.1 Introduction: Role and Relevance of Agriculture in Indian Economy 🔴
- **Contribution to National Income**: Share in GDP declined from 59% (1950-51) to ~15% (2017-18). However, ~43% of the population still depends on it. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Largest Employment Provider**: Employed 73.9% of the workforce in 1972-73, dropping to 43% by 2017-18. Despite the drop, absolute numbers increased, leading to **disguised unemployment** (marginal productivity of labour ≤ 0). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Provision of Food Surplus**: Meets the food demands of an expanding population. Food consumption constitutes ~60% of average household income. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Providing Raw Materials to Industries**: Essential for cotton textiles, jute, sugar, rubber, and food processing industries. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Market for Industrial Products**: Increased rural purchasing power stimulates industrial development (as per Nurkse and Lewis). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Commercial Importance**: Agricultural exports accounted for 70-75% of export earnings post-independence, which declined to 12.3% by 2016-17 due to economic diversification. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 13.2 Agriculture Production and Productivity After Independence 🔴
- **Wheat & Rice**: Consistent and significant increase, especially post-mid-1960s (New Agricultural Strategy) due to 'miracle seeds'. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Jowar & Bajra**: Erratic trends. Area shifted towards HYV maize. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Pulses**: Lagged behind for a long time requiring imports. However, production shot up to 23 million tons in 2016-17, achieving near self-sufficiency. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Oilseeds**: Large gap between demand and supply; ~60% of edible oil demand is still met through imports. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cotton**: Production surged from 9 million bales (2000-01) to 33 million bales (2010-11) due to the widespread adoption of **Bt Cotton** in 2002. ⭐ (exam-important)
- Yield per hectare of total food grains increased ~4 times from 552 kg/ha (1950-51) to 2299 kg/ha (2018-19).
- **Wheat**: Huge jump from 655 kg/ha to 3507 kg/ha. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Rice**: Increased from 1123 kg/ha (1970-71) to 2659 kg/ha. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Pulses**: Highly disappointing; stagnant for decades, currently at 806 kg/ha. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Economic Reforms Impact**: Agriculture growth stagnated post-1991 reforms (Agri-GDP growth at 1.9% during 2014-19 compared to 7.2% overall GDP), implying reforms largely bypassed agriculture. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 13.3 Causes for Stagnation in Agriculture Growth in India 🔴
- Share of agriculture in gross capital formation declined since the 1980s. Total investment in agriculture as a proportion of GDP fell from 9.9% (1990-91) to 3.5% (1999-2000).
- Public investment declined mainly due to the diversion of resources from capital investment towards current expenditures in the form of **subsidies**.
- Only ~49% of Gross Cropped Area (GCA) was irrigated as of 2016-17.
- **Skewed Distribution**: Irrigation is skewed across states (Punjab 98.5% vs Maharashtra 19.5%) and crops (Wheat 93.6% vs Pulses 20%). ⭐ (exam-important)
- Low irrigation cover leads to severe rainfall dependency, causing high correlation between production and rainfall.

### 13.3 Causes for Stagnation in Agriculture Growth in India (Continued) 🔴
- Failure to make major breakthroughs in frontier areas like biotechnology.
- Inadequate investment in Research & Education (R&E): Total R&E expenditure was only about 0.54% of Agriculture GDP in 2014-15.
- Unsatisfactory spread of High Yielding Varieties (HYVs) and farm management techniques.
- The average landholding size shrank to 1.1 hectare (ha) in 2015-16.
- Nearly 1/3rd of farmers have < 1 ha, and 37% have < 0.4 ha.
- Small farm size holds back agricultural productivity, making it difficult to access institutional credit, adopt new technologies, and undertake capital-intensive land improvements.
- Reduction in rural bank branches and declining credit-deposit ratios in the 1990s increased reliance on private moneylenders at exploitative rates.
- Despite rural credit increases post-2001, the main beneficiaries have been large agribusiness companies due to the inclusion of indirect finance under priority sector lending.
- Optimum NPK (Nitrogen, Phosphate, Potash) ratio for India is 4:2:1.
- In 2012-13, the ratio was highly skewed at 8.2:3.2:1, aggravating the loss of soil fertility.
- **Low Awareness**: Only 10% of farmers were aware of MSPs before the sowing season. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Implementation Issues**: Long distances to procurement centres, high transport costs, inadequate storage, and delays in payments limit its effectiveness. ⭐ (exam-important)
- Quality seeds account for a 20-25% increase in productivity.
- High cost of HYVs acts as a disincentive for small and marginal farmers.
- Overall mechanisation level in India is < 50% (compared to 90% in developed nations).
- Small landholdings and lack of access to customized, low-cost machinery hinder mechanisation.
- Inadequate storage causes huge post-harvest losses: Fruits & Vegetables (up to 40%), Pulses (6.4-8.4%), and Oilseeds (5.3-9.9%).

### 13.4 Transformation of Indian Agriculture: Strategies for Development (1951-2002) 🔴
- Technologically stagnant phase relying on increased traditional inputs (land, labour).
- Initiated Community Development Programs and Grow More Food Campaigns.
- Culminated in a severe food crisis in 1965-66 due to droughts and wars, necessitating food imports.
- **New Agriculture Strategy (Green Revolution)** introduced to combat the food crisis. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Package Programme**: Combined improved seeds, chemical fertilizers, irrigation, and plant protection. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Miracle Seeds**: HYVs were highly responsive to fertilizers and utilized nutrients efficiently without lodging. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Confined to Wheat**: ~90% of wheat lands benefited; limited strictly to irrigated areas. ⭐ (exam-important)
- Wheat production growth shot up from 4% to 5.7% per annum; productivity doubled.
- Yield growth became the dominant source of output growth (85.2%), replacing area expansion.
- Generated employment in diverse non-farm activities (milling, warehousing, marketing).
- **Inequality**: Being capital-intensive, it favored large farmers, leading to interpersonal inequalities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Regional Disparities**: Confined to irrigated regions (Punjab, Haryana, Western UP). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Restrictive Crop Coverage**: Ignored commercial crops and pulses, leading to skewed cropping patterns. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 13.5 Transformation of Indian Agriculture: Strategies for Development (2002-2014) 🔴
- Launched in 2007 to incentivize states to increase investment in agriculture.
- Provided autonomy to states in planning and executing agricultural plans, successfully raising Agri-GDP growth to 3.7% in the 11th Plan.
- The first Green Revolution caused ecological harm: soil fertility loss, salinity, water-logging, and groundwater depletion.
- **Second Green Revolution** aims for sustainable livelihood without ecological harm by promoting: ⭐ (exam-important)
  - Agro-forestry on degraded lands.
  - Organic and Eco-agriculture.
  - One-straw Revolution (natural farming without chemical inputs).

### 13.6 Transformation of Indian Agriculture through Doubling of Farmers’ Income (DFI) from 2015 to 2022 🔴
1. Emphasis on irrigation ('More crop per drop').
2. Quality seeds and nutrients.
3. Investments in warehouses and cold chains to stop post-harvest losses.
4. Value addition through food processing.
5. Implementation of National Agricultural Markets (e-NAM).
6. Mitigation of risk via crop insurance.
7. Promotion of allied activities (dairy, poultry, fisheries).
- **PMKSY (Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana)**: Launched in 2015 to extend irrigation coverage ('Har Khet Ko Pani') and improve water use efficiency. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana)**: Launched in 2016 for comprehensive crop insurance to mitigate risk from extreme weather events, replacing older flawed schemes. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Operation Green**: Aims to shift cropping patterns from cereals to high-value crops (fruits/vegetables). ⭐ (exam-important)
  - Organise farmers into **Farmers Producers Organisations (FPOs)** to bypass middlemen.
  - Replace restrictive APMC Act with the New APLM Act, 2017.
  - Link processing industries directly with FPOs using the Contract Farming Act, 2018.
- **e-NAM (E-National Agriculture Market)**: A unified pan-India electronic trading platform to facilitate transparent price discovery. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **MSP (Minimum Support Price)** is recommended by the CACP (Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices) based on the A-2 concept (cash costs + imputed family labour). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Procurement Mechanisms**: Handled by Food Corporation of India (FCI), Price Support Scheme (PSS), and Market Intervention Scheme (MIS). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **PM-AASHA**: Umbrella scheme launched to strictly enforce remunerative prices via PSS, Price Deficiency Payment Scheme (PDPS), and Pilot Private Procurement. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Soil Health Card (SHC)**: Recommends exact fertilizer inputs (4R principle) based on 12 soil parameters to stop the indiscriminate use of nitrogenous fertilizers. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Pest Management & Mechanisation**: Implement Integrated Pest Management (IPM) and Sub Mission on Agricultural Mechanisation (SMAM). ⭐ (exam-important)

### 13.7 Relevance of Non-Agricultural Activities in Doubling Income 🔴
- Due to shrinking farm sizes, relying solely on agriculture leads to poverty. 
- **NABARD Survey**: Only 13% of agricultural households have a single income source. Incomes comprise 35% from cultivation, 34% wage labour, 16% salaries, and 15% livestock/non-farm. Diversification is critical. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 14.2 Industrialisation and Economic Development 🔴
- **Case for Industrialisation**: It offers much higher labour productivity, massive employment generation, and mobilises surplus savings effectively to break the cycle of underdevelopment. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 14.3 Growth Strategy in India 🔴
- The Second Five-Year Plan adopted the **Mahalanobis Plan Frame**, shifting focus explicitly towards heavy and capital goods industries to build a self-sufficient economy. 
- Require huge capital investments.
- Long gestation periods before yielding output.
- Natural monopolies subject to increasing returns to scale. 
- *Consequence*: Private enterprise was deterred, forcing the State to exclusively set up and operate them.
1. Development of heavy/machine-building industries.
2. Expansion of the public sector.
3. Establishment of a cooperative sector.
4. Prevention of private monopolies.
- **IDRA, 1951 (Industries Development & Regulation Act)**: Mandated compulsory registration and licensing from the Centre to establish a new unit, expand capacity, or manufacture a new article. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **MRTP Act, 1969**: Aimed to prevent the concentration of economic power and prohibit restrictive trade practices. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **FERA, 1973**: Highly restrictive foreign exchange regulation. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 14.4 Review of Industrial Licensing in India 🔴
- **Foreign Investment**: Automatic approval for FDI up to 51%-100% in specific sectors. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Technology Agreements**: Automatic permission for foreign technology agreements. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Public Sector**: Disinvestment of shares; sectors unreserved to allow private entry. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **MRTP Act**: Threshold asset limits removed; pre-entry restrictions deleted. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Internal Deregulation**: Sweeping removal of physical controls to inject competition. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 14.4.1 Phase of Liberalisation (Continued) 🔴
- **Corporate Restructuring**: Triggered a wave of restructuring, mergers, and acquisitions. Entrepreneurs shifted focus towards "world-size plants" to achieve scale economies. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **World-Class Quality**: Adoption of global manufacturing management techniques significantly improved quality. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mass Market Innovation**: Fostered an environment for low-cost innovation, catering to the growing middle-class mass market. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Global Expansion**: Indian businesses emerged leaner and more efficient, allowing them to confidently expand overseas. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Skewed Investment**: Massive investments flowed into 'sunrise' sectors, causing a slow pace of investment in basic/strategic industries (engineering, power, machine tools). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Weak Interlinkages**: There is a need to strengthen linkages between new sectors and old sectors to prevent the loss of momentum through leakages abroad. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **New Intangible Variables**: Government environmental policies and regulations are now acting as new business "game-changers." ⭐ (exam-important)
- **GST**: Aimed at creating a common market, eliminating cascading taxes, reducing logistics costs, and bringing unorganised industries into the fold. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Make in India (2014)**: Position India as a global manufacturing and innovation hub. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Start-up India**: Nurture innovation to generate large-scale employment. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ease of Doing Business**: Simplification of rules and massive introduction of IT in governance. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **IPR Policy (2016)**: To improve the intellectual property ecosystem ("Creative India; Innovative India"). ⭐ (exam-important)

### 14.5 Critical Issues before Industrial Sector 🔴
- **Definition**: A unit is deemed "sick" if its loan account turns into a Non-Performing Asset (NPA). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **External Causes**: High input costs, transport bottlenecks, power shortages, recessionary trends, and fiscal imposts. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Internal Causes**: Improper demand estimation, defective capital structure, wrong location, and **managerial ineffectiveness** (the most significant cause). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Government Response**: The Sick Industrial Companies Act (SICA) of 1985 led to the creation of the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction (BIFR) to monitor and rehabilitate sick units. ⭐ (exam-important)
- Slow pace of modernisation stems primarily from **paucity of finance**.
- **Historical Factors**: The restrictive licensing policy previously rejected modernisation as it resulted in "capacity expansion." Price controls depressed returns, and depreciation based on historical costs was inadequate during inflationary periods. ⭐ (exam-important)
- Relying purely on capital accumulation hits diminishing returns; **Total Factor Productivity (TFP)** growth is essential.
- **J-Curve Pattern**: TFP growth in Indian manufacturing slowed drastically in the 1990s (due to the shock of import liberalisation and exchange rate reforms rendering old capital obsolete), but expanded sharply to 1.41% annually from 2002-2008 as firms adapted. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 15.2 What are Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs)? 🔴
- **World Bank Definition**: Primarily relies on the number of employees (Micro < 10, Small < 50, Medium < 300), alongside asset size and annual sales. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Indian Definition (MSMED Act, 2006)**: Classifies units based strictly on **investment in plant & machinery/equipment** (excluding land and buildings). ⭐ (exam-important)
  - **Manufacturing Sector**: Micro (≤ ₹25 Lakh), Small (> ₹25 Lakh to ₹5 Crore), Medium (> ₹5 Crore to ₹10 Crore).
  - **Service Sector**: Micro (≤ ₹10 Lakh), Small (> ₹10 Lakh to ₹2 Crore), Medium (> ₹2 Crore to ₹5 Crore).
- **Proposed Turnover-Based Definition**: The investment-based criteria faced criticism for ignoring inflation and being hard to verify for informal sectors. With the advent of GST, the government proposed a transparent, turnover-based classification: ⭐ (exam-important)
  - Micro (≤ ₹5 Crore), Small (> ₹5 Crore to ₹75 Crore), Medium (> ₹75 Crore to ₹250 Crore).

### 15.3 Significance of MSMEs in the Indian Economy 🔴
- Accounts for ~95% of all industrial units.
- Contributes ~45% to total manufacturing output.
- Constitutes ~40% of the country’s total exports.
- Currently contributes ~29% to the GDP (with a government target to raise it to 50% in the next five years).
- **Geographical Distribution**: Uttar Pradesh (14.2%) and West Bengal (14%) house the highest number of MSMEs in India. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Largest Employment Generator**: Second only to agriculture, the MSME sector creates ~111 million jobs. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Micro Dominance**: The Micro sector alone provides 97% of total MSME employment. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Socio-Economic Impact**: Because MSMEs are less capital intensive, they check rural-urban migration by creating local employment and help reduce regional imbalances. Broad activity categories (Manufacturing, Trade, Other Services) share employment almost equally (32%, 35%, 33% respectively). ⭐ (exam-important)
- The sector's share in total exports increased from 43% in 2012-13 to 49% in 2017-18. The growth trajectory of MSME exports closely mirrors the country's overall total exports.

### 15.4 Comparison of the MSME Sector with the Overall Industrial Sector 🔴
- MSMEs have historically registered a higher growth rate than the overall industrial sector. 
- During 2003–2009, following the MSMED Act of 2006, MSME growth peaked at over 10%. However, growth sharply fell to the 4-7% range following the 2008 global financial crisis.

### 15.5 Issues and Challenges Faced by the MSME Sector 🔴
1. **Lack of Adequate Capital and Credit**: The RBI Working Group identified this as a crucial reason for industrial sickness. Issues include complex collateral requirements, cumbersome sanction procedures, delayed disbursements, and high interest rates. Venture capital/equity financing is underutilised.
2. **Delayed Payments**: MSMEs frequently face delayed payments from buyers, destroying their working capital cycles.
3. **Inadequate Infrastructural Facilities**: Poor access to reliable power, water, and road/rail connectivity inflates operational costs and damages competitiveness.
4. **Inadequate Access to Marketing Linkages**: Weak marketing infrastructure and inadequate government patronage.
5. **Lack of Skilled Human Resources**: Non-availability of affordable skilled workers and managerial expertise.
6. **Lack of Access to New Technology**: A low technology base leads to low productivity.
7. **Dilatory Regulatory Practices**: Cumbersome procedures for resolving insolvency, taxation, and construction permits.
- **Demonetisation**: Caused immediate liquidity crunches, restricting payments to contractual labour (especially in apparel and gems/jewellery). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **GST**: Brought many MSMEs into the formal tax net for the first time, leading to an immediate increase in compliance and operating costs. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Long-term Outlook**: However, both reforms are expected to be beneficial long-term by enhancing digitisation, generating transaction histories, and improving access to formal finance. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Liquidity Crunch & Supply Chain Disruption**: The nationwide lockdown devastated MSMEs, reducing earnings by 20-50%. It halted the supply of finished goods, raw material procurement, and caused massive labor shortages. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Trade Collapse**: The sudden halt in trade deeply affected labour-intensive exports (gems, garments, sea food) supplied mainly by MSMEs. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Survival & Innovation**: MSMEs in essential commodities survived better. Many innovated by shifting to produce hand sanitizers, PPE kits, and masks. The pandemic also drastically accelerated the adoption of e-commerce and digitisation. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 15.6 Policy Initiatives by the Government 🔴
- **MSMED Act, 2006**: The landmark act provided a legal framework recognizing "enterprises" (both manufacturing and services). It defined medium enterprises and addressed issues like public procurement and delayed payments. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ministry of MSME**: Formed in 2007 by merging the Ministry of Small Scale Industries and the Ministry of Agro and Rural Industries. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Institutions**: KVIC (Khadi & Village Industries Commission), Coir Board, NSIC, and SIDBI (established in 1990) provide financial and developmental support. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Key Schemes**: ⭐ (exam-important)
  - **CGTMSE (2000)**: Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises provides collateral-free credit.
  - **PMEGP (2008)**: Prime Minister’s Employment Generation Programme generates self-employment ventures.
- **Need for Formalisation**: Without registration, MSMEs cannot access formal credit, and policymakers lack robust data for interventions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Incentives for Registration**: Collateral-free loans, 15% Credit Linked Capital Subsidy Scheme (CLCSS), tax exemptions, reduced interest rates, and protection against delayed payments. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Udyog Aadhaar (2015)**: The registration process was radically simplified into a user-friendly, Aadhaar-based electronic platform. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 16.2 What Constitutes the Services Sector? 🔴
- **Definition**: Services are intangible economic products where production and consumption generally occur simultaneously. They cannot be stored. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Classification**: ⭐ (exam-important)
  - The WTO's General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) classifies services into 12 core sectors (e.g., Business, Communication, Financial, Health, Tourism, Transport).
  - India's National Accounts use the National Industrial Classification (NIC) to classify economic activities into Agriculture, Industry, and Services.

### 16.3 Service Sector Measurement Issues 🔴
- **Intangibility**: Unlike physical goods, it is extremely difficult to separate price changes from quality improvements in services (e.g., healthcare, education). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Informal Sector**: A massive portion of services in India (e.g., street vendors, domestic workers) operates informally, making data capture in national accounts difficult and reliant on estimations. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 16.4 Pattern of Growth in Services in India 🔴
- **Standard Theory (Fisher, Clark, Kuznets)**: As an economy develops, its centre of gravity shifts from Agriculture to Industry, and finally to Services. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **The Indian Anomaly**: India's structural transformation largely **bypassed the manufacturing sector**. The economy leapfrogged directly from an agriculture-dominated economy to a services-led one. The share of services in India's Gross Value Added (GVA) surged from ~30% in 1950-51 to over 54% by 2018-19. ⭐ (exam-important)
1. **Trade, Hotels, Transport, Communication, and Broadcasting**: ~18.3% of GVA.
2. **Financial, Real Estate, and Professional Services**: ~21.3% of GVA (The fastest-growing segment).
3. **Public Administration, Defence, and Other Services**: ~14.6% of GVA.
- India's service exports grew exponentially post-liberalisation, currently accounting for ~38% of total exports. 
- **Software Services** form the bedrock, constituting over 40% of all service exports. ⭐ (exam-important)
- The services sector attracts the highest Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) equity inflows into India, reflecting global confidence in India's service delivery capabilities.

### 16.5 Factors behind Service Sector Growth 🔴
- **High Income Elasticity of Demand**: As per capita income rises, consumers spend a disproportionately higher share of income on services (healthcare, education, tourism, entertainment) compared to basic goods (Engel's Law). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Splintering (Outsourcing)**: Modern manufacturing has become highly specialised. Firms increasingly outsource ancillary services (accounting, legal, logistics, cleaning) rather than maintaining in-house departments, which statistically shifts value from 'Industry' to 'Services'. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Economic Reforms of 1991**: The deregulation of heavily state-controlled sectors like Telecommunications, Banking, Insurance, and Aviation injected massive private capital and competition. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Information & Communication Technology (ICT) Revolution**: Technological advancements overcame the traditional non-tradability of services, enabling the cross-border delivery of IT and Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) services. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 16.6 Organised Service Sectors – Cross Cutting Policy Initiatives and Issues 🔴
- **Short-Term Disruption**: Severely impacted cash-intensive, informal service sectors such as retail trade, unorganised transport, and local hotels/restaurants due to sudden liquidity constraints. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Long-Term Formalisation**: Accelerated the transition towards digital payments and formalised a significant portion of the shadow economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Integration**: subsumed multiple cascading taxes into a single structure (tax slabs for services generally fall under 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Challenges**: Initially caused confusion regarding classification. The compliance burden increased, and complexities surrounding Input Tax Credit (ITC) created working capital issues for certain service providers (e.g., restaurants). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mode 1 (Cross-border supply)**: e.g., Telemedicine, BPO. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mode 2 (Consumption abroad)**: e.g., Tourism, students studying abroad. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mode 3 (Commercial presence)**: e.g., Setting up a foreign bank branch. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Mode 4 (Presence of natural persons)**: e.g., IT professionals traveling abroad for projects. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Non-Tariff Barriers (NTBs)**: Strict domestic regulations (complex licensing, foreign equity caps, and lack of standardization) restrict the free flow of services. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Immigration Laws**: Highly restrictive visa regimes (like the H-1B visa restrictions in the US) severely hamper India's Mode 4 exports (movement of IT professionals). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Jobless Growth**: While the services sector contributes over 54% to GVA, its share in total employment is disproportionately low (~30%). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Skill Bias**: The fastest-growing sectors (IT, Telecom, Finance) are highly skill-intensive and capital-intensive. They generate high income but absorb a very small fraction of India's large, largely unskilled labour force. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 17.1 Introduction 🔴

### 17.2 What is the Informal Service Sector? 🔴
- **Definition by NCEUS (National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector)**: The informal (or unorganized) sector consists of "all unincorporated private enterprises owned by individuals or households engaged in the sale and production of goods and services operated on a proprietary or partnership basis and with less than ten total workers." ⭐ (exam-important)
- The distinction between formal and informal is primarily based on the size of employment and the legal status of the enterprise.

### 17.3 Characteristics of the Informal Sector 🔴
- **Small Scale & Low Organisation**: Typically operates on a very small scale (often single-person or family-run) with minimal formal organisation or accounting records. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Low Capital & High Labour Intensity**: Relies heavily on manual labour with very little investment in capital, technology, or machinery. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Ease of Entry and Exit**: Requires minimal capital or specialized skills to enter, making it the default fallback option for the unemployed. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Lack of Legal Protection**: Operates outside the purview of labor laws. Workers have no formal contracts, no job security, and no social security benefits (like pensions, provident funds, health insurance, or paid leave). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Heterogeneity**: It is highly diverse, encompassing everything from street vendors, domestic workers, and rickshaw pullers to small independent mechanics and unregulated transport operators. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 17.4 Factors Behind the Growth of the Informal Sector 🔴
- **Rural-Urban Migration (Push Factor)**: Agrarian distress and surplus labour in rural areas push millions to migrate to urban centers. Lacking the skills for formal jobs, they get absorbed into the urban informal service sector. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Jobless Growth in the Formal Sector**: The formal manufacturing and high-end service sectors (like IT) are highly capital/skill-intensive. They have completely failed to generate mass employment at a rate matching the demographic dividend. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Sub-contracting (Flexibilisation of Labour)**: To bypass rigid Indian labour laws and reduce operational costs, formal sector enterprises increasingly sub-contract work to informal units or hire temporary/contract workers who lack formal protections. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 17.5 Contribution of the Informal Sector 🔴
- **Employment**: It absorbs over **80-90% of the total workforce** in India, acting as the ultimate "shock absorber" for the unemployed. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **GDP Contribution**: The unorganized/informal sector contributes approximately **50% to the nation's GDP**. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Linkages**: It provides essential, low-cost services that subsidize the cost of living for urban formal workers and supports the formal economy through supply chain linkages. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 17.6 Issues and Challenges 🔴
- **Income Insecurity and Poverty**: Earnings are erratic, highly seasonal, and often below the minimum wage.  ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Poor Working Conditions**: Workers endure long hours, lack of occupational safety, and hazardous environments with zero employer liability for accidents or health issues. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Extreme Vulnerability to Economic Shocks**: Because they have zero savings and no safety nets, shocks like the COVID-19 lockdowns disproportionately devastated informal workers, leading to massive reverse migration and hunger crises. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Harassment and Rent-Seeking**: Vulnerable groups like street vendors and rickshaw pullers face constant harassment, eviction, and extortion from local municipal authorities and police due to their ambiguous legal status. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Exclusion from Formal Finance**: Lack of collateral forces them into the clutches of local moneylenders charging usurious interest rates. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 17.7 Policy Interventions 🔴
- **Street Vendors Act, 2014**: The *Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act* aims to protect the rights of urban street vendors and regulate vending activities to prevent arbitrary evictions. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **PM SVANidhi**: Launched during the COVID-19 pandemic, it provides a special micro-credit facility (working capital loans up to ₹10,000) to street vendors to restart their livelihoods. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act, 2008**: An umbrella legislation mandating the government to formulate welfare schemes for life/disability cover, health/maternity benefits, and old age protection. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan-dhan (PM-SYM)**: A voluntary and contributory pension scheme designed specifically to ensure old age protection for unorganised workers. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **e-Shram Portal**: Launched recently to create the first comprehensive national database of unorganised workers (migrant workers, construction workers, gig/platform workers) to facilitate the targeted delivery of social security schemes. ⭐ (exam-important)

### 17.8 Let Us Sum Up 🔴

### 17.9 Key Words 🔴
- **Social Security**: Public measures to provide protection against economic and social distress caused by stoppage or substantial reduction of earnings. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **PM SVANidhi**: Micro-credit scheme exclusively for street vendors. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Vulnerability**: The degree to which a population is susceptible to, and unable to cope with, adverse economic or social shocks. ⭐ (exam-important)
