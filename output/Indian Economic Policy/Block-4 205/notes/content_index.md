# Unit 13: Agriculture: Issues, Concerns, Policy and Programmatic Initiatives

## 13.1 Introduction: Role and Relevance of Agriculture in Indian Economy
Agriculture is a crucial sector of the Indian economy with several key contributions:
- **Contribution to National Income**: Share in GDP declined from 59% (1950-51) to ~15% (2017-18). However, ~43% of the population still depends on it.
- **Largest Employment Provider**: Employed 73.9% of the workforce in 1972-73, dropping to 43% by 2017-18. Despite the drop, absolute numbers increased, leading to **disguised unemployment** (marginal productivity of labour ≤ 0).
- **Provision of Food Surplus**: Meets the food demands of an expanding population. Food consumption constitutes ~60% of average household income.
- **Providing Raw Materials to Industries**: Essential for cotton textiles, jute, sugar, rubber, and food processing industries.
- **Market for Industrial Products**: Increased rural purchasing power stimulates industrial development (as per Nurkse and Lewis).
- **Commercial Importance**: Agricultural exports accounted for 70-75% of export earnings post-independence, which declined to 12.3% by 2016-17 due to economic diversification.

## 13.2 Agriculture Production and Productivity After Independence
Crops are classified into **Food grains** (wheat, rice, cereals, pulses) and **Non-food grains** (oilseeds, fibres, sugarcane, plantation crops). Food crops make up ~2/3rd of total agricultural production.

### 13.2.1 Trends in Production
- **Wheat & Rice**: Consistent and significant increase, especially post-mid-1960s (New Agricultural Strategy) due to 'miracle seeds'.
- **Jowar & Bajra**: Erratic trends. Area shifted towards HYV maize.
- **Pulses**: Lagged behind for a long time requiring imports. However, production shot up to 23 million tons in 2016-17, achieving near self-sufficiency.
- **Oilseeds**: Large gap between demand and supply; ~60% of edible oil demand is still met through imports.
- **Cotton**: Production surged from 9 million bales (2000-01) to 33 million bales (2010-11) due to the widespread adoption of **Bt Cotton** in 2002.

### 13.2.2 Trends in Productivity
- Yield per hectare of total food grains increased ~4 times from 552 kg/ha (1950-51) to 2299 kg/ha (2018-19).
- **Wheat**: Huge jump from 655 kg/ha to 3507 kg/ha.
- **Rice**: Increased from 1123 kg/ha (1970-71) to 2659 kg/ha.
- **Pulses**: Highly disappointing; stagnant for decades, currently at 806 kg/ha.
- **Economic Reforms Impact**: Agriculture growth stagnated post-1991 reforms (Agri-GDP growth at 1.9% during 2014-19 compared to 7.2% overall GDP), implying reforms largely bypassed agriculture.

## 13.3 Causes for Stagnation in Agriculture Growth in India
### 13.3.1 Deceleration in Agriculture Investment
- Share of agriculture in gross capital formation declined since the 1980s. Total investment in agriculture as a proportion of GDP fell from 9.9% (1990-91) to 3.5% (1999-2000).
- Public investment declined mainly due to the diversion of resources from capital investment towards current expenditures in the form of **subsidies**.

### 13.3.2 Inadequate Irrigation Coverage and Falling Productivity of Irrigation
- Only ~49% of Gross Cropped Area (GCA) was irrigated as of 2016-17.
- **Skewed Distribution**: Irrigation is skewed across states (Punjab 98.5% vs Maharashtra 19.5%) and crops (Wheat 93.6% vs Pulses 20%).
- Low irrigation cover leads to severe rainfall dependency, causing high correlation between production and rainfall.

## 13.3 Causes for Stagnation in Agriculture Growth in India (Continued)
### 13.3.3 Failure to Evolve and Adopt New Technologies
- Failure to make major breakthroughs in frontier areas like biotechnology.
- Inadequate investment in Research & Education (R&E): Total R&E expenditure was only about 0.54% of Agriculture GDP in 2014-15.
- Unsatisfactory spread of High Yielding Varieties (HYVs) and farm management techniques.

### 13.3.4 Shrinking Farm Size
- The average landholding size shrank to 1.1 hectare (ha) in 2015-16.
- Nearly 1/3rd of farmers have < 1 ha, and 37% have < 0.4 ha.
- Small farm size holds back agricultural productivity, making it difficult to access institutional credit, adopt new technologies, and undertake capital-intensive land improvements.

### 13.3.5 Non-availability or Inadequate Credit from Formal Sources
- Reduction in rural bank branches and declining credit-deposit ratios in the 1990s increased reliance on private moneylenders at exploitative rates.
- Despite rural credit increases post-2001, the main beneficiaries have been large agribusiness companies due to the inclusion of indirect finance under priority sector lending.

### 13.3.6 Imbalance in the Use of Fertilizer
- Optimum NPK (Nitrogen, Phosphate, Potash) ratio for India is 4:2:1.
- In 2012-13, the ratio was highly skewed at 8.2:3.2:1, aggravating the loss of soil fertility.

### 13.3.7 Effectiveness of Minimum Support Prices (MSPs)
- **Low Awareness**: Only 10% of farmers were aware of MSPs before the sowing season.
- **Implementation Issues**: Long distances to procurement centres, high transport costs, inadequate storage, and delays in payments limit its effectiveness.

### 13.3.8 Inadequate Availability and Access to Quality Seeds
- Quality seeds account for a 20-25% increase in productivity.
- High cost of HYVs acts as a disincentive for small and marginal farmers.

### 13.3.9 Farm Mechanisation: Major Challenges
- Overall mechanisation level in India is < 50% (compared to 90% in developed nations).
- Small landholdings and lack of access to customized, low-cost machinery hinder mechanisation.

### 13.3.10 Post-harvest Activities-Inadequate Storage Facilities
- Inadequate storage causes huge post-harvest losses: Fruits & Vegetables (up to 40%), Pulses (6.4-8.4%), and Oilseeds (5.3-9.9%).

## 13.4 Transformation of Indian Agriculture: Strategies for Development (1951-2002)
Three distinct phases characterize Indian agricultural growth:
### 13.4.1 Phase I: Traditional Agriculture (1951-1966)
- Technologically stagnant phase relying on increased traditional inputs (land, labour).
- Initiated Community Development Programs and Grow More Food Campaigns.
- Culminated in a severe food crisis in 1965-66 due to droughts and wars, necessitating food imports.

### 13.4.2 Phase II: Technologically Dynamic Agriculture with New Agriculture Strategy (NAS): 1966 - 2002
- **New Agriculture Strategy (Green Revolution)** introduced to combat the food crisis.
- **Package Programme**: Combined improved seeds, chemical fertilizers, irrigation, and plant protection.
- **Miracle Seeds**: HYVs were highly responsive to fertilizers and utilized nutrients efficiently without lodging.
- **Confined to Wheat**: ~90% of wheat lands benefited; limited strictly to irrigated areas.

### 13.4.3 Impact of Green Revolution
**Positive Impacts:**
- Wheat production growth shot up from 4% to 5.7% per annum; productivity doubled.
- Yield growth became the dominant source of output growth (85.2%), replacing area expansion.
- Generated employment in diverse non-farm activities (milling, warehousing, marketing).

**Negative Impacts:**
- **Inequality**: Being capital-intensive, it favored large farmers, leading to interpersonal inequalities.
- **Regional Disparities**: Confined to irrigated regions (Punjab, Haryana, Western UP).
- **Restrictive Crop Coverage**: Ignored commercial crops and pulses, leading to skewed cropping patterns.

## 13.5 Transformation of Indian Agriculture: Strategies for Development (2002-2014)
### 13.5.1 Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (RKVY)
- Launched in 2007 to incentivize states to increase investment in agriculture.
- Provided autonomy to states in planning and executing agricultural plans, successfully raising Agri-GDP growth to 3.7% in the 11th Plan.

### 13.5.2 Second Green Revolution (Evergreen Revolution)
- The first Green Revolution caused ecological harm: soil fertility loss, salinity, water-logging, and groundwater depletion.
- **Second Green Revolution** aims for sustainable livelihood without ecological harm by promoting:
  - Agro-forestry on degraded lands.
  - Organic and Eco-agriculture.
  - One-straw Revolution (natural farming without chemical inputs).

## 13.6 Transformation of Indian Agriculture through Doubling of Farmers’ Income (DFI) from 2015 to 2022
Past strategies successfully multiplied food production 3.7 times (1965-2015) and achieved self-sufficiency, but completely neglected farmers' welfare. Farm income per cultivator dropped to just 25% of a non-agricultural worker's income by 1993-94, causing agrarian distress.

### 13.6.1 Prime Minister’s Multi-dimensional Seven Points Strategy
1. Emphasis on irrigation ('More crop per drop').
2. Quality seeds and nutrients.
3. Investments in warehouses and cold chains to stop post-harvest losses.
4. Value addition through food processing.
5. Implementation of National Agricultural Markets (e-NAM).
6. Mitigation of risk via crop insurance.
7. Promotion of allied activities (dairy, poultry, fisheries).

### 13.6.2 Enhancing Output per Hectare: Improving Cropping Intensity
- **PMKSY (Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana)**: Launched in 2015 to extend irrigation coverage ('Har Khet Ko Pani') and improve water use efficiency.
- **PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana)**: Launched in 2016 for comprehensive crop insurance to mitigate risk from extreme weather events, replacing older flawed schemes.

### 13.6.3 Policy Initiatives for Crop Diversification and Value Realisation
- **Operation Green**: Aims to shift cropping patterns from cereals to high-value crops (fruits/vegetables).
  - Organise farmers into **Farmers Producers Organisations (FPOs)** to bypass middlemen.
  - Replace restrictive APMC Act with the New APLM Act, 2017.
  - Link processing industries directly with FPOs using the Contract Farming Act, 2018.
- **e-NAM (E-National Agriculture Market)**: A unified pan-India electronic trading platform to facilitate transparent price discovery.

### 13.6.4 Ensuring Remunerative Price: MSP Policy and Procurement
- **MSP (Minimum Support Price)** is recommended by the CACP (Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices) based on the A-2 concept (cash costs + imputed family labour).
- **Procurement Mechanisms**: Handled by Food Corporation of India (FCI), Price Support Scheme (PSS), and Market Intervention Scheme (MIS).
- **PM-AASHA**: Umbrella scheme launched to strictly enforce remunerative prices via PSS, Price Deficiency Payment Scheme (PDPS), and Pilot Private Procurement.

### 13.6.5 Resource Efficiency / Cost Reduction
- **Soil Health Card (SHC)**: Recommends exact fertilizer inputs (4R principle) based on 12 soil parameters to stop the indiscriminate use of nitrogenous fertilizers.
- **Pest Management & Mechanisation**: Implement Integrated Pest Management (IPM) and Sub Mission on Agricultural Mechanisation (SMAM).

## 13.7 Relevance of Non-Agricultural Activities in Doubling Income
- Due to shrinking farm sizes, relying solely on agriculture leads to poverty. 
- **NABARD Survey**: Only 13% of agricultural households have a single income source. Incomes comprise 35% from cultivation, 34% wage labour, 16% salaries, and 15% livestock/non-farm. Diversification is critical.

# Unit 14: Large Scale Industries in India: Issues and Policy

## 14.2 Industrialisation and Economic Development
**Industrialisation** refers to the shifting of the economic gravity from agriculture to industry. 
- **Case for Industrialisation**: It offers much higher labour productivity, massive employment generation, and mobilises surplus savings effectively to break the cycle of underdevelopment.

## 14.3 Growth Strategy in India
### 14.3.1 Evolution of Strategy in India’s Plans
- The Second Five-Year Plan adopted the **Mahalanobis Plan Frame**, shifting focus explicitly towards heavy and capital goods industries to build a self-sufficient economy. 

### 14.3.2 Essential Features of Heavy Industries
- Require huge capital investments.
- Long gestation periods before yielding output.
- Natural monopolies subject to increasing returns to scale. 
- *Consequence*: Private enterprise was deterred, forcing the State to exclusively set up and operate them.

### 14.3.4 Industrial Policy Resolution (IPR), 1956
Known as the **"Economic Constitution of India"**, the IPR 1956 emphasized:
1. Development of heavy/machine-building industries.
2. Expansion of the public sector.
3. Establishment of a cooperative sector.
4. Prevention of private monopolies.

### 14.3.5 Legislative Support to Strategy
- **IDRA, 1951 (Industries Development & Regulation Act)**: Mandated compulsory registration and licensing from the Centre to establish a new unit, expand capacity, or manufacture a new article.
- **MRTP Act, 1969**: Aimed to prevent the concentration of economic power and prohibit restrictive trade practices.
- **FERA, 1973**: Highly restrictive foreign exchange regulation.

## 14.4 Review of Industrial Licensing in India
While licensing initially protected domestic industry, it resulted in a high-cost, low-quality industrial structure devoid of international competitiveness. 

### 14.4.1 Phase of Liberalisation (New Industrial Policy, 1991)
The 1991 reforms ("reform by storm") dismantled the restrictive regime:
- **Foreign Investment**: Automatic approval for FDI up to 51%-100% in specific sectors.
- **Technology Agreements**: Automatic permission for foreign technology agreements.
- **Public Sector**: Disinvestment of shares; sectors unreserved to allow private entry.
- **MRTP Act**: Threshold asset limits removed; pre-entry restrictions deleted.
- **Internal Deregulation**: Sweeping removal of physical controls to inject competition.

## 14.4.1 Phase of Liberalisation (Continued)
**Positive Effects of Liberalisation:**
- **Corporate Restructuring**: Triggered a wave of restructuring, mergers, and acquisitions. Entrepreneurs shifted focus towards "world-size plants" to achieve scale economies.
- **World-Class Quality**: Adoption of global manufacturing management techniques significantly improved quality.
- **Mass Market Innovation**: Fostered an environment for low-cost innovation, catering to the growing middle-class mass market.
- **Global Expansion**: Indian businesses emerged leaner and more efficient, allowing them to confidently expand overseas.

**Negative Effects & Challenges:**
- **Skewed Investment**: Massive investments flowed into 'sunrise' sectors, causing a slow pace of investment in basic/strategic industries (engineering, power, machine tools).
- **Weak Interlinkages**: There is a need to strengthen linkages between new sectors and old sectors to prevent the loss of momentum through leakages abroad.
- **New Intangible Variables**: Government environmental policies and regulations are now acting as new business "game-changers."

### 14.4.2 Recent Policy Initiatives Impacting Industrial Growth
- **GST**: Aimed at creating a common market, eliminating cascading taxes, reducing logistics costs, and bringing unorganised industries into the fold.
- **Make in India (2014)**: Position India as a global manufacturing and innovation hub.
- **Start-up India**: Nurture innovation to generate large-scale employment.
- **Ease of Doing Business**: Simplification of rules and massive introduction of IT in governance.
- **IPR Policy (2016)**: To improve the intellectual property ecosystem ("Creative India; Innovative India").

## 14.5 Critical Issues before Industrial Sector
### 14.5.1 Industrial Sickness
- **Definition**: A unit is deemed "sick" if its loan account turns into a Non-Performing Asset (NPA).
- **External Causes**: High input costs, transport bottlenecks, power shortages, recessionary trends, and fiscal imposts.
- **Internal Causes**: Improper demand estimation, defective capital structure, wrong location, and **managerial ineffectiveness** (the most significant cause).
- **Government Response**: The Sick Industrial Companies Act (SICA) of 1985 led to the creation of the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction (BIFR) to monitor and rehabilitate sick units.

### 14.5.2 Technological Obsolescence and Modernisation
- Slow pace of modernisation stems primarily from **paucity of finance**.
- **Historical Factors**: The restrictive licensing policy previously rejected modernisation as it resulted in "capacity expansion." Price controls depressed returns, and depreciation based on historical costs was inadequate during inflationary periods.

### 14.5.3 Productivity in Indian Industry
- Relying purely on capital accumulation hits diminishing returns; **Total Factor Productivity (TFP)** growth is essential.
- **J-Curve Pattern**: TFP growth in Indian manufacturing slowed drastically in the 1990s (due to the shock of import liberalisation and exchange rate reforms rendering old capital obsolete), but expanded sharply to 1.41% annually from 2002-2008 as firms adapted.

## 14.6 Approach to a New Industrial Policy
The government released a discussion paper outlining strategic objectives:
1. **Global Linkages**: Create global brands out of India.
2. **Enhance Competitiveness**: Reduce infrastructure and logistics costs.
3. **Gainful Employment**: Harness the demographic dividend and tackle 'job-less' growth and automation risks.
4. **Sustainability**: Balance industrial growth with the environment.
5. **Tech Ecosystem**: Transition from mere assembly to actual component manufacturing, R&D, and technology transfer.

# Unit 15: Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs): Issues and Policy

## 15.1 Introduction
The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSME) sector is widely regarded as the engine of growth in both developing and developed countries. According to the WTO, MSMEs represent 95% of all companies globally and account for 60% of employment. However, their potential is often constrained by both external factors (finance access, regulations, global competition) and internal factors (managerial competence, technology).

## 15.2 What are Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs)?
Defining an MSME varies globally:
- **World Bank Definition**: Primarily relies on the number of employees (Micro < 10, Small < 50, Medium < 300), alongside asset size and annual sales.
- **Indian Definition (MSMED Act, 2006)**: Classifies units based strictly on **investment in plant & machinery/equipment** (excluding land and buildings).
  - **Manufacturing Sector**: Micro (≤ ₹25 Lakh), Small (> ₹25 Lakh to ₹5 Crore), Medium (> ₹5 Crore to ₹10 Crore).
  - **Service Sector**: Micro (≤ ₹10 Lakh), Small (> ₹10 Lakh to ₹2 Crore), Medium (> ₹2 Crore to ₹5 Crore).
- **Proposed Turnover-Based Definition**: The investment-based criteria faced criticism for ignoring inflation and being hard to verify for informal sectors. With the advent of GST, the government proposed a transparent, turnover-based classification:
  - Micro (≤ ₹5 Crore), Small (> ₹5 Crore to ₹75 Crore), Medium (> ₹75 Crore to ₹250 Crore).

## 15.3 Significance of MSMEs in the Indian Economy
### 15.3.1 Contribution of MSMEs in the Country’s Economy
The MSME sector is a vital cog in the Indian economy:
- Accounts for ~95% of all industrial units.
- Contributes ~45% to total manufacturing output.
- Constitutes ~40% of the country’s total exports.
- Currently contributes ~29% to the GDP (with a government target to raise it to 50% in the next five years).
- **Geographical Distribution**: Uttar Pradesh (14.2%) and West Bengal (14%) house the highest number of MSMEs in India.

### 15.3.2 Employment in the MSME Sector
- **Largest Employment Generator**: Second only to agriculture, the MSME sector creates ~111 million jobs.
- **Micro Dominance**: The Micro sector alone provides 97% of total MSME employment.
- **Socio-Economic Impact**: Because MSMEs are less capital intensive, they check rural-urban migration by creating local employment and help reduce regional imbalances. Broad activity categories (Manufacturing, Trade, Other Services) share employment almost equally (32%, 35%, 33% respectively).

### 15.3.3 Comparison of MSME Export to Total Exports
- The sector's share in total exports increased from 43% in 2012-13 to 49% in 2017-18. The growth trajectory of MSME exports closely mirrors the country's overall total exports.

## 15.4 Comparison of the MSME Sector with the Overall Industrial Sector
- MSMEs have historically registered a higher growth rate than the overall industrial sector. 
- During 2003–2009, following the MSMED Act of 2006, MSME growth peaked at over 10%. However, growth sharply fell to the 4-7% range following the 2008 global financial crisis.

## 15.5 Issues and Challenges Faced by the MSME Sector
Despite its strategic importance, the MSME sector faces severe constraints:
1. **Lack of Adequate Capital and Credit**: The RBI Working Group identified this as a crucial reason for industrial sickness. Issues include complex collateral requirements, cumbersome sanction procedures, delayed disbursements, and high interest rates. Venture capital/equity financing is underutilised.
2. **Delayed Payments**: MSMEs frequently face delayed payments from buyers, destroying their working capital cycles.
3. **Inadequate Infrastructural Facilities**: Poor access to reliable power, water, and road/rail connectivity inflates operational costs and damages competitiveness.
4. **Inadequate Access to Marketing Linkages**: Weak marketing infrastructure and inadequate government patronage.
5. **Lack of Skilled Human Resources**: Non-availability of affordable skilled workers and managerial expertise.
6. **Lack of Access to New Technology**: A low technology base leads to low productivity.
7. **Dilatory Regulatory Practices**: Cumbersome procedures for resolving insolvency, taxation, and construction permits.

### 15.5.1 Impact of Demonetisation and GST on the MSME Sector
Since MSMEs largely operate in the informal sector dealing in cash and daily wage earners, they were highly vulnerable to structural shocks:
- **Demonetisation**: Caused immediate liquidity crunches, restricting payments to contractual labour (especially in apparel and gems/jewellery).
- **GST**: Brought many MSMEs into the formal tax net for the first time, leading to an immediate increase in compliance and operating costs.
- **Long-term Outlook**: However, both reforms are expected to be beneficial long-term by enhancing digitisation, generating transaction histories, and improving access to formal finance.

### 15.5.2 Impact of the Covid-19 Pandemic on the MSME Sector
- **Liquidity Crunch & Supply Chain Disruption**: The nationwide lockdown devastated MSMEs, reducing earnings by 20-50%. It halted the supply of finished goods, raw material procurement, and caused massive labor shortages.
- **Trade Collapse**: The sudden halt in trade deeply affected labour-intensive exports (gems, garments, sea food) supplied mainly by MSMEs.
- **Survival & Innovation**: MSMEs in essential commodities survived better. Many innovated by shifting to produce hand sanitizers, PPE kits, and masks. The pandemic also drastically accelerated the adoption of e-commerce and digitisation.

## 15.6 Policy Initiatives by the Government
### 15.6.1 Legislation and Institutional Support System
- **MSMED Act, 2006**: The landmark act provided a legal framework recognizing "enterprises" (both manufacturing and services). It defined medium enterprises and addressed issues like public procurement and delayed payments.
- **Ministry of MSME**: Formed in 2007 by merging the Ministry of Small Scale Industries and the Ministry of Agro and Rural Industries.
- **Institutions**: KVIC (Khadi & Village Industries Commission), Coir Board, NSIC, and SIDBI (established in 1990) provide financial and developmental support.
- **Key Schemes**:
  - **CGTMSE (2000)**: Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises provides collateral-free credit.
  - **PMEGP (2008)**: Prime Minister’s Employment Generation Programme generates self-employment ventures.

### 15.6.2 Formalisation of MSMEs
According to the 73rd NSS round, there are 63.39 million MSMEs in India, but a vast majority remain informal ("the art of not being governed") to avoid paperwork and rent-seeking.
- **Need for Formalisation**: Without registration, MSMEs cannot access formal credit, and policymakers lack robust data for interventions.
- **Incentives for Registration**: Collateral-free loans, 15% Credit Linked Capital Subsidy Scheme (CLCSS), tax exemptions, reduced interest rates, and protection against delayed payments.
- **Udyog Aadhaar (2015)**: The registration process was radically simplified into a user-friendly, Aadhaar-based electronic platform.

## 15.7 Let Us Sum Up
MSMEs are the backbone of the economy, fostering entrepreneurship and generating employment at a low capital cost. While they register high growth rates, they require a holistic ecosystem addressing credit, technology, and formalisation to remain globally competitive.

## 15.8 Key Words
- **Demonetisation**: Stripping a currency unit of its status as legal tender.
- **Formalisation**: Bringing the unorganised sector into the formal fold.
- **Informal Sector**: Unincorporated private enterprises owned by individuals/households with less than ten total workers.

# Unit 16: Services Sector I: Organised Sector - Issues and Policy

## 16.1 Introduction
The services sector is the dominant force in the Indian economy, driving economic growth, attracting Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), and significantly contributing to exports.

## 16.2 What Constitutes the Services Sector?
- **Definition**: Services are intangible economic products where production and consumption generally occur simultaneously. They cannot be stored.
- **Classification**:
  - The WTO's General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) classifies services into 12 core sectors (e.g., Business, Communication, Financial, Health, Tourism, Transport).
  - India's National Accounts use the National Industrial Classification (NIC) to classify economic activities into Agriculture, Industry, and Services.

## 16.3 Service Sector Measurement Issues
Accurately measuring the output of the services sector is statistically challenging:
- **Intangibility**: Unlike physical goods, it is extremely difficult to separate price changes from quality improvements in services (e.g., healthcare, education).
- **Informal Sector**: A massive portion of services in India (e.g., street vendors, domestic workers) operates informally, making data capture in national accounts difficult and reliant on estimations.

## 16.4 Pattern of Growth in Services in India
### 16.4.1 Structural Change in Indian Economy
- **Standard Theory (Fisher, Clark, Kuznets)**: As an economy develops, its centre of gravity shifts from Agriculture to Industry, and finally to Services.
- **The Indian Anomaly**: India's structural transformation largely **bypassed the manufacturing sector**. The economy leapfrogged directly from an agriculture-dominated economy to a services-led one. The share of services in India's Gross Value Added (GVA) surged from ~30% in 1950-51 to over 54% by 2018-19.

### 16.4.2 Relative Shares of Sub-Sectors of Service GDP
The services sector comprises three broad sub-sectors:
1. **Trade, Hotels, Transport, Communication, and Broadcasting**: ~18.3% of GVA.
2. **Financial, Real Estate, and Professional Services**: ~21.3% of GVA (The fastest-growing segment).
3. **Public Administration, Defence, and Other Services**: ~14.6% of GVA.

### 16.4.3 Share of Services in Trade
- India's service exports grew exponentially post-liberalisation, currently accounting for ~38% of total exports. 
- **Software Services** form the bedrock, constituting over 40% of all service exports.

### 16.4.4 Share of Services in FDI
- The services sector attracts the highest Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) equity inflows into India, reflecting global confidence in India's service delivery capabilities.

## 16.5 Factors behind Service Sector Growth
### 16.5.1 Demand Side Factors
- **High Income Elasticity of Demand**: As per capita income rises, consumers spend a disproportionately higher share of income on services (healthcare, education, tourism, entertainment) compared to basic goods (Engel's Law).
- **Splintering (Outsourcing)**: Modern manufacturing has become highly specialised. Firms increasingly outsource ancillary services (accounting, legal, logistics, cleaning) rather than maintaining in-house departments, which statistically shifts value from 'Industry' to 'Services'.

### 16.5.2 Supply Side Factors: Trade Liberalisation and Reforms
- **Economic Reforms of 1991**: The deregulation of heavily state-controlled sectors like Telecommunications, Banking, Insurance, and Aviation injected massive private capital and competition.
- **Information & Communication Technology (ICT) Revolution**: Technological advancements overcame the traditional non-tradability of services, enabling the cross-border delivery of IT and Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) services.

## 16.6 Organised Service Sectors – Cross Cutting Policy Initiatives and Issues
### 16.6.1 Impact of Demonetisation on Service Sector in India
- **Short-Term Disruption**: Severely impacted cash-intensive, informal service sectors such as retail trade, unorganised transport, and local hotels/restaurants due to sudden liquidity constraints.
- **Long-Term Formalisation**: Accelerated the transition towards digital payments and formalised a significant portion of the shadow economy.

### 16.6.2 Goods and Services Tax (GST): Impact on Services
- **Integration**: subsumed multiple cascading taxes into a single structure (tax slabs for services generally fall under 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%).
- **Challenges**: Initially caused confusion regarding classification. The compliance burden increased, and complexities surrounding Input Tax Credit (ITC) created working capital issues for certain service providers (e.g., restaurants).

### 16.6.3 Domestic Regulations and Barriers to Trade in Services
The WTO's General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) defines four modes of trade:
- **Mode 1 (Cross-border supply)**: e.g., Telemedicine, BPO.
- **Mode 2 (Consumption abroad)**: e.g., Tourism, students studying abroad.
- **Mode 3 (Commercial presence)**: e.g., Setting up a foreign bank branch.
- **Mode 4 (Presence of natural persons)**: e.g., IT professionals traveling abroad for projects.

**Barriers to Trade**:
- **Non-Tariff Barriers (NTBs)**: Strict domestic regulations (complex licensing, foreign equity caps, and lack of standardization) restrict the free flow of services.
- **Immigration Laws**: Highly restrictive visa regimes (like the H-1B visa restrictions in the US) severely hamper India's Mode 4 exports (movement of IT professionals).

### 16.6.4 Low Employment Elasticity in Organised Service Sectors
- **Jobless Growth**: While the services sector contributes over 54% to GVA, its share in total employment is disproportionately low (~30%).
- **Skill Bias**: The fastest-growing sectors (IT, Telecom, Finance) are highly skill-intensive and capital-intensive. They generate high income but absorb a very small fraction of India's large, largely unskilled labour force.

## 16.7 Sector-specific Policy Initiatives and Issues in Selected Organised Sectors
### 16.7.1 IT-BPM / Software Sector
- **Significance**: It is the largest private-sector employer and the premier export earner for India.
- **Challenges**:
  - **Automation & AI**: Emerging technologies threaten traditional, low-end Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) jobs.
  - **Protectionism**: Increasing protectionist policies and stringent visa norms in key markets (USA, UK) restrict market access.
  - **Infrastructure**: Poor urban infrastructure and high attrition rates.

### 16.7.2 Challenges and Prospects of Indian Tourism
- **Significance**: High potential for mass employment generation and foreign exchange earnings.
- **Challenges**: Severe infrastructure deficit (roads, sanitation), pervasive safety and security concerns for tourists, high taxation structures, and a shortage of trained hospitality personnel. 
- **Policy Boost**: Introduction of the E-visa facility has been a major positive step to stimulate foreign tourist arrivals.

### 16.7.3 Shipping and Port Services: Initiatives and Challenges
- **Significance**: Critical for India's international trade; handles ~95% of trade by volume and ~68% by value.
- **Sagarmala Programme**: A flagship initiative aiming for "port-led development" by modernizing existing ports, developing new ones, and improving hinterland connectivity to reduce logistics costs.
- **Challenges**:
  - **High Turnaround Time**: Indian ports suffer from significantly higher turnaround times compared to global benchmarks (e.g., Singapore, Rotterdam).
  - **Inadequate Draft Depth**: Many ports cannot accommodate ultra-large vessels.
  - **Connectivity**: Weak road and rail connectivity to the hinterland delays cargo evacuation.

### 16.7.4 Real Estate Sector in India: Policy Initiatives and Challenges
- **Significance**: A massive contributor to GDP and the second-largest employer (after agriculture), with deep forward and backward linkages to over 250 ancillary industries.
- **RERA (Real Estate Regulation and Development Act, 2016)**: A landmark reform designed to protect homebuyers, ensure timely completion of projects, and bring transparency and accountability to the notoriously opaque sector.

### 16.7.5 Policy Initiatives and Challenges being Faced by Banking and Financial Services
- **PMJDY (Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana)**: The world's largest financial inclusion drive, bringing millions of unbanked citizens into the formal financial system.
- **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC, 2016)**: Established a consolidated and time-bound framework for resolving insolvency and bankruptcies, shifting control from debtors to creditors.
- **Challenges**: 
  - **High NPAs**: The sector, particularly Public Sector Banks (PSBs), is burdened with massive Non-Performing Assets.
  - **Twin Balance Sheet Problem**: The economy suffered simultaneously from overleveraged corporate balance sheets and stressed bank balance sheets, restricting fresh credit disbursement.

## 16.8 Policy Implications
The organized services sector requires continuous regulatory reforms to remove Non-Tariff Barriers, improve the ease of doing business, enhance skill development to tackle 'jobless growth', and upgrade physical infrastructure to maintain global competitiveness.

## 16.9 Let Us Sum Up
The services sector has leapfrogged manufacturing to become the primary engine of India's economic growth. While it dominates GVA, exports, and FDI, its inability to generate mass employment for unskilled labor remains a critical macroeconomic challenge.

# Unit 17: Services Sector II: Informal Sector - Issues and Policy

## 17.1 Introduction
While the organized services sector (IT, Banking) drives headline GDP growth, the **informal services sector** (street vendors, domestic workers, rickshaw pullers, barbers) employs the vast majority of the Indian workforce. This sector is characterized by a lack of regulation, absence of social security, and precarious working conditions.

## 17.2 What is the Informal Service Sector?
- **Definition by NCEUS (National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector)**: The informal (or unorganized) sector consists of "all unincorporated private enterprises owned by individuals or households engaged in the sale and production of goods and services operated on a proprietary or partnership basis and with less than ten total workers."
- The distinction between formal and informal is primarily based on the size of employment and the legal status of the enterprise.

## 17.3 Characteristics of the Informal Sector
The informal service sector exhibits distinct features that separate it from the formal economy:
- **Small Scale & Low Organisation**: Typically operates on a very small scale (often single-person or family-run) with minimal formal organisation or accounting records.
- **Low Capital & High Labour Intensity**: Relies heavily on manual labour with very little investment in capital, technology, or machinery.
- **Ease of Entry and Exit**: Requires minimal capital or specialized skills to enter, making it the default fallback option for the unemployed.
- **Lack of Legal Protection**: Operates outside the purview of labor laws. Workers have no formal contracts, no job security, and no social security benefits (like pensions, provident funds, health insurance, or paid leave).
- **Heterogeneity**: It is highly diverse, encompassing everything from street vendors, domestic workers, and rickshaw pullers to small independent mechanics and unregulated transport operators.

## 17.4 Factors Behind the Growth of the Informal Sector
The persistence and expansion of the informal sector are driven by a combination of macroeconomic factors:
- **Rural-Urban Migration (Push Factor)**: Agrarian distress and surplus labour in rural areas push millions to migrate to urban centers. Lacking the skills for formal jobs, they get absorbed into the urban informal service sector.
- **Jobless Growth in the Formal Sector**: The formal manufacturing and high-end service sectors (like IT) are highly capital/skill-intensive. They have completely failed to generate mass employment at a rate matching the demographic dividend.
- **Sub-contracting (Flexibilisation of Labour)**: To bypass rigid Indian labour laws and reduce operational costs, formal sector enterprises increasingly sub-contract work to informal units or hire temporary/contract workers who lack formal protections.

## 17.5 Contribution of the Informal Sector
Despite its vulnerabilities, the informal sector is a massive pillar of the Indian economy:
- **Employment**: It absorbs over **80-90% of the total workforce** in India, acting as the ultimate "shock absorber" for the unemployed.
- **GDP Contribution**: The unorganized/informal sector contributes approximately **50% to the nation's GDP**.
- **Linkages**: It provides essential, low-cost services that subsidize the cost of living for urban formal workers and supports the formal economy through supply chain linkages.

## 17.6 Issues and Challenges
The informal sector workforce faces severe structural and systemic vulnerabilities:
- **Income Insecurity and Poverty**: Earnings are erratic, highly seasonal, and often below the minimum wage. 
- **Poor Working Conditions**: Workers endure long hours, lack of occupational safety, and hazardous environments with zero employer liability for accidents or health issues.
- **Extreme Vulnerability to Economic Shocks**: Because they have zero savings and no safety nets, shocks like the COVID-19 lockdowns disproportionately devastated informal workers, leading to massive reverse migration and hunger crises.
- **Harassment and Rent-Seeking**: Vulnerable groups like street vendors and rickshaw pullers face constant harassment, eviction, and extortion from local municipal authorities and police due to their ambiguous legal status.
- **Exclusion from Formal Finance**: Lack of collateral forces them into the clutches of local moneylenders charging usurious interest rates.

## 17.7 Policy Interventions
Recognizing the necessity to protect informal workers, the government has launched several key interventions aimed at formalisation and social security:
- **Street Vendors Act, 2014**: The *Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act* aims to protect the rights of urban street vendors and regulate vending activities to prevent arbitrary evictions.
- **PM SVANidhi**: Launched during the COVID-19 pandemic, it provides a special micro-credit facility (working capital loans up to ₹10,000) to street vendors to restart their livelihoods.
- **Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act, 2008**: An umbrella legislation mandating the government to formulate welfare schemes for life/disability cover, health/maternity benefits, and old age protection.
- **Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan-dhan (PM-SYM)**: A voluntary and contributory pension scheme designed specifically to ensure old age protection for unorganised workers.
- **e-Shram Portal**: Launched recently to create the first comprehensive national database of unorganised workers (migrant workers, construction workers, gig/platform workers) to facilitate the targeted delivery of social security schemes.

## 17.8 Let Us Sum Up
The informal service sector remains the defining characteristic of the Indian labour market. While it exhibits low productivity and severe vulnerabilities, it is indispensable as the primary provider of livelihoods. The policy imperative is not merely to eradicate informality, but to systematically formalise the sector by extending legal recognition, credit access, and a robust social safety net to ensure inclusive economic growth.

## 17.9 Key Words
- **Social Security**: Public measures to provide protection against economic and social distress caused by stoppage or substantial reduction of earnings.
- **PM SVANidhi**: Micro-credit scheme exclusively for street vendors.
- **Vulnerability**: The degree to which a population is susceptible to, and unable to cope with, adverse economic or social shocks.
