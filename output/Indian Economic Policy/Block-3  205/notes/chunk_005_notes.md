## Section: 10.6 Currency Derivative Market in India 🔴

### Core Idea
Currency derivatives allow buyers and sellers to hedge against foreign exchange risks by locking in future exchange rates. While India has active over-the-counter (OTC) currency forwards and swaps used by banks and traders, there are no exchange-traded currency derivatives.

> **In Simple Terms:** If you are an exporter and worry the value of the Dollar might drop against the Rupee next year, you can use a currency derivative to lock in today's exchange rate for your future dollars.

### Key Concepts
- **Currency Derivative**: A contract deriving its value from an underlying currency exchange rate.
- **Pairs Traded**: USD, EUR, GBP, JPY.
- **Market Structure**: OTC instruments (forwards and swaps) are popular among importers/exporters. Exchange-traded currency derivatives do not currently exist in India.

## Section: 10.7 Long-Term Government Bond and Corporate Debt Market 🔴

### Core Idea
While India's equity and government bond markets are highly developed, the corporate debt market remains in a nascent, illiquid stage. Most corporate bonds are issued via private placement by public sector institutions. The Patil Committee recommended significant reforms to stimulate this market, including rationalizing issuances, enhancing exchange trading, and establishing robust clearing mechanisms (DVP III).

> **In Simple Terms:** It's easy for companies to sell shares to the public in India, but it's much harder for them to borrow money by selling corporate bonds. The government is trying to fix this so companies have more ways to raise money.

### Key Concepts

#### Current State of Corporate Debt Market
- Restricted, arbitrage-driven, and highly illiquid.
- Primary issuances are mostly private placements to institutional investors.
- Secondary market is small. Turnover ratio is relatively high, but the absolute size of outstanding bonds is small.

#### Patil Committee Recommendations
A High-Level Committee on Corporate Bonds and Securitisation proposed:
1. Rationalizing primary issuance procedures to move away from private placements.
2. Facilitating exchange trading (BSE, NSE, FIMMDA).
3. Rationalizing stamp-duty and abolishing tax deduction at source (like G-Secs).
4. Implementing Delivery Versus Payment (DVP) III for net settlement of funds and securities to reduce counterparty risk.

### Definitions
- **Delivery Versus Payment (DVP) III**: A settlement mechanism where both funds and securities legs are settled for net amounts, enhancing efficiency and reducing risk. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Private Placement**: The sale of securities to a relatively small number of select investors as a way of raising capital, rather than a public offering.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Corporate debt market in India = underdeveloped. Equity market = highly developed.
> - Patil Committee focuses on corporate bond market reforms.

## Section: Unit 11 Introduction to Fiscal Policy and IS-LM Framework 🔴

### Core Idea
Fiscal policy involves government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand. Rooted in Keynesian economics born during the Great Depression, the IS-LM framework theoretically demonstrates how fiscal changes (like public works) increase output and employment directly, and indirectly via secondary employment generated by increased consumer expenditure.

> **In Simple Terms:** Fiscal policy is how the government taxes us and spends that money. Keynes argued that during a recession, the government should spend more to create jobs and boost demand.

### Key Concepts

#### Components of Aggregate Demand (AD)
$Y = C + I + G + NX$
- **C**: Private Final Consumption Expenditure (PFCE) - largest share of India's GDP.
- **I**: Gross Capital Formation (GCF) / Business Investment.
- **G**: Government Final Consumption Expenditure (GFCE).
- **NX**: Net Exports (Exports - Imports).

#### Keynesian Consumption Function
$C = \bar{C} + c(Y - tY)$
- Consumption depends on disposable income (National income minus tax).
- **c** (Marginal Propensity to Consume): The increase in consumer expenditure per unit increase in disposable income.

#### Tools of Fiscal Policy
1. **Government Spending**: Changes in revenue or capital expenditure that directly impact GDP. Acts as a counter-cyclical measure.
2. **Taxes**: Changes in direct and indirect taxes that influence economic agent behavior and thus GDP.

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Policy**: Government policy regarding taxation and public spending to influence aggregate demand and the economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)**: The increase in consumer expenditure resulting from a one-unit increase in disposable income.
