## Section: 12.1 & 12.2 Principles and Pillars of Federal Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
Fiscal federalism deals with how economic responsibilities (revenue raising and spending) are divided between different levels of government (Union, State, Local). This division is guided by economic efficiency: nationwide public goods (defense) go to the Centre, while localized services (street lighting) go to local/state bodies. However, this division inherently creates imbalances—states have high expenditure needs but limited revenue powers (vertical imbalance), and poorer states struggle more than richer ones (horizontal imbalance). These imbalances are corrected via inter-governmental transfers.

> **In Simple Terms:** In a country as big as India, the central government can't manage everything locally, so it gives states and local bodies the power to spend money on their specific needs. But because the central government collects the biggest taxes (like Income Tax and corporate tax), it has to transfer a chunk of that money back to the states so they can actually afford to do their jobs.

### Key Concepts

#### Decentralisation Theorem
- A decentralized system of governance caters better to local tastes and preferences and is therefore more efficient than a centralized system for certain localized public goods.

#### Three Economic Responsibilities of Government (Musgrave)
1. **Resource Allocation**: Suitable for all levels depending on the service.
2. **Distribution of Wealth/Income**: Best suited for the national government (otherwise rich people would just migrate out of high-tax states).
3. **Stabilisation of the Economy**: Best suited for the national government.

#### The Assignments
- **Expenditure Assignment**: Governed by the benefit area (Subsidiarity Principle). Defense -> Union; Public Lighting -> Local.
- **Revenue Assignment**: Broad-based, mobile taxes (Personal Income Tax, Corporate Tax, Customs) are assigned to the Centre to prevent tax-induced market distortions. Less mobile taxes (Property tax, Stamp duty, Motor Vehicle tax) are assigned to the States/Local bodies.

#### Imbalances in Federal Finance
1. **Vertical Imbalance**: Expenditure responsibilities of sub-national governments are higher than their revenue-raising capacity.
2. **Horizontal Imbalance**: Disparity in the ability of constituent units at the same level (e.g., Bihar vs. Haryana) to meet their expenditure needs due to varying levels of economic development.

#### Correcting Imbalances: Inter-governmental Transfers
- **Revenue-sharing**: A specified percentage of central taxes is shared with the states.
- **Grants**: 
  - *Conditional (Specific Purpose)*: Tied to a specific use (e.g., matching grants for Centrally Sponsored Schemes to incentivize state behavior).
  - *Unconditional (General Purpose)*: Best for correcting baseline vertical/horizontal imbalances.

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Federalism**: The financial relations between units of governments in a federal government system. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Vertical Imbalance**: The mismatch between revenue-raising capacity and expenditure responsibilities at different levels of government. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Horizontal Imbalance**: The disparity in the fiscal capacity of government units at the same level. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Centre handles: Stabilization, Wealth Distribution, Broad-based Taxes (Income/Corporate).
> - States handle: Resource Allocation for regional goods, specific taxes (Stamp duty, Motor vehicle).
> - To fix the resulting gap, the Centre uses Revenue Sharing and Grants.

## Section: 12.3 Institutions of Federalism in India 🔴

### Core Idea
To manage the complex financial relationship between the Centre and the States, India relies on key institutions. The most important is the constitutionally mandated Finance Commission, which determines how central taxes are shared. Historically, the Planning Commission also played a massive role, and recently, the GST Council has emerged as a powerful joint decision-making body.

> **In Simple Terms:** Because the Centre and States constantly need to share money, there are specific referees (like the Finance Commission and the GST Council) who sit down, look at the rules, and decide exactly who gets what so it's fair for everyone.

### Key Concepts

#### 1. The Finance Commission (Art. 280)
- Appointed by the President every 5 years (or earlier).
- **Core Mandate**: Recommend tax devolution (sharing of central tax revenue) and grants-in-aid to cover state revenue deficits, fund local bodies, and disaster relief.
- **Process**: Estimates State revenue deficits, recommends a percentage share of the Union's distributable tax pool to the states, and provides revenue-deficit grants to states that still fall short after devolution.
- *Note*: State Finance Commissions (created via 73rd/74th amendments) do the same for State-to-Local body transfers.

#### 2. Planning Commission / NITI Aayog
- *Erstwhile Planning Commission*: Provided Plan grants/loans and a forum for states (National Development Council). It had massive allocative powers.
- *NITI Aayog (2015 onwards)*: Replaced the Planning Commission. Acts purely as an advisory "think tank" with no direct role in allocating fiscal transfers.

#### 3. GST Council (Art. 279A)
- A joint forum of the Centre and States. Takes decisions on GST rates, exemptions, and administrative procedures.
- Represents a pooling of sovereignty, moving towards cooperative federalism.

### Definitions
- **Tax Devolution**: The statutory sharing of central tax revenues with the states, as recommended by the Finance Commission. ⭐ (exam-important)
