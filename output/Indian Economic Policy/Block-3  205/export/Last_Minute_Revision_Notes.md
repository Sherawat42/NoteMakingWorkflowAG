# Last Minute Revision Notes: Indian Economic Policy (Block-3)

## 📚 Key Definitions (⭐ Exam-Important)
- **Money**: Anything that is generally acceptable as a medium of payment. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Fiat Money**: Money that has no intrinsic value and derives its value from a government order or fiat. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Inflation**: The rate of increase in the general price level over a period of time. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Core Inflation**: The long-term component of inflation calculated by removing volatile components like food and fuel. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: The share of NDTL that a bank is required to maintain in safe and liquid assets. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)**: The average daily balance a bank must maintain with the RBI as a percentage of its NDTL. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Repo Rate**: The rate at which the Reserve Bank provides overnight liquidity to banks against the collateral of government and other approved securities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Reverse Repo Rate**: The rate at which the Reserve Bank absorbs liquidity, on an overnight basis, from banks against the collateral of eligible government securities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Nominal Anchor**: A variable that policymakers use to "tie down" the price level or its path (e.g., CPI-Combined headline inflation). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**: A six-member committee instituted to determine the policy rate required to achieve the inflation target. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Capital Market**: A market for financial assets which have a long or indefinite maturity. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Liquidity**: How easily an asset can be converted to currency without loss of value. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Market Capitalisation Ratio (MCR)**: The value of listed shares divided by GDP, used as an indicator of stock market size. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Turnover Ratio (TOR)**: The value of total shares traded divided by market capitalisation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Value Traded Ratio (VTR)**: The total value of traded shares in the stock market divided by GDP. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Derivative Security**: A financial contract whose value is derived from the value of something else, such as a stock price, commodity price, or index. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Delivery Versus Payment (DVP) III**: A settlement mechanism where both funds and securities legs are settled for net amounts, enhancing efficiency and reducing risk. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Fiscal Policy**: Government policy regarding taxation and public spending to influence aggregate demand and the economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Crowding-Out Effect**: The decline in private investment as a result of increasing government spending and borrowing. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Minimum Alternative Tax (MAT)**: A tax introduced to ensure that "zero-tax companies" using exemptions pay a minimum percentage of their book profits as tax. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **FRBM Act (2003)**: Legislation mandating the central and state governments to institute transparent fiscal management systems and progressively reduce their fiscal and revenue deficits. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Goods and Services Tax (GST)**: A comprehensive, multi-stage, destination-based tax levied on every value addition. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cascading Effect**: When a tax is levied on a product at every step of the sale, including on the tax levied at the previous step (tax on tax). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Fiscal Federalism**: The financial relations between units of governments in a federal government system. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Vertical Imbalance**: The mismatch between revenue-raising capacity and expenditure responsibilities at different levels of government. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Horizontal Imbalance**: The disparity in the fiscal capacity of government units at the same level. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Tax Devolution**: The statutory sharing of central tax revenues with the states, as recommended by the Finance Commission. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Tax Devolution Formula**: The mathematical formula used by the Finance Commission (incorporating factors like population, area, income distance, and forest cover) to determine each state's share of the central tax pool. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)**: Schemes funded partially by the central government but implemented by states in areas that fall under the State List, usually requiring matching state funds. ⭐ (exam-important)

## 🧠 In Simple Terms (Concept Simplifiers)
> **In Simple Terms:** This section acts as a roadmap for the course, telling you exactly what overarching economic tools the government uses to steer the economy and what each upcoming unit will discuss.

> **In Simple Terms:** Inflation is just things getting more expensive over time. Because it impacts everyone, the central bank uses monetary policy to control the amount of money flowing around to keep prices stable.

> **In Simple Terms:** Money is anything widely accepted as payment. Modern currency is "fiat" money—it only has value because the government says it does.

> **In Simple Terms:** Inflation reduces the purchasing power of your money. It's measured using indices like CPI (retail) and WPI (wholesale). High inflation hurts the poor the most because they rely on cash and unindexed wages.

> **In Simple Terms:** If the central bank prints more money than the economy actually grows, the extra money just chases the same amount of goods, causing prices to rise.

> **In Simple Terms:** The RBI tweaks interest rates to control how much it costs to borrow money. Cheaper loans mean people spend more, which boosts growth but can cause inflation.

> **In Simple Terms:** Instead of juggling multiple goals at once, the RBI's primary job now is to keep inflation at a specific target (4%). To decide how to do this, a special committee (the MPC) meets and votes on interest rates.

> **In Simple Terms:** The financial system is the engine that takes money from people who are saving it and gives it to businesses that need to invest it, keeping the economy growing.

> **In Simple Terms:** The capital market is where companies go to raise long-term money (by selling shares or bonds) and where investors go to buy and trade those assets.

> **In Simple Terms:** A stock market is crucial because it helps businesses grow and create jobs by raising money from the public, while giving the public a chance to grow their savings.

> **In Simple Terms:** India's stock markets used to be fragmented, but technology like screen-based trading united them. The BSE and NSE are the giants here, and they react sharply to major events like GST or pandemics, showing how connected the stock market is to the real world.

> **In Simple Terms:** A derivative is like a bet or an insurance policy on the future price of a stock. People use them to protect themselves against price drops (hedging) or to try and make a quick profit (speculation).

> **In Simple Terms:** If you are an exporter and worry the value of the Dollar might drop against the Rupee next year, you can use a currency derivative to lock in today's exchange rate for your future dollars.

> **In Simple Terms:** It's easy for companies to sell shares to the public in India, but it's much harder for them to borrow money by selling corporate bonds. The government is trying to fix this so companies have more ways to raise money.

> **In Simple Terms:** Fiscal policy is how the government taxes us and spends that money. Keynes argued that during a recession, the government should spend more to create jobs and boost demand.

> **In Simple Terms:** If the government spends more money, it boosts the economy's total income, but also drives up interest rates because there's more demand for money. If the government raises taxes, people have less to spend, so total income drops and interest rates fall.

> **In Simple Terms:** Fiscal policy is the government's budget strategy. Post-independence, India taxed heavily to fund public projects. After the 1991 crisis, the government realized high taxes were causing evasion and debt, leading to massive reforms that simplified and lowered taxes.

> **In Simple Terms:** The government lowered tax rates to encourage people to actually pay them, and introduced new taxes (like the service tax) to cast a wider net. However, because they still had massive past debts and subsidies to pay off, their bank account was still bleeding money, requiring strict new rules to force them to balance the budget.

> **In Simple Terms:** The FRBM Act is a law that forces the government to stick to a strict financial diet. It legally binds the government to stop spending more than it earns on everyday expenses (Revenue Deficit) and to limit its total borrowing (Fiscal Deficit).

> **In Simple Terms:** When the 2008 global crash happened, the Indian government had to abandon its strict financial diet (FRBM Act) and spend heavily to save the economy. Afterwards, they had to rewrite the rules to get back on track.

> **In Simple Terms:** Before GST, goods were taxed multiple times as they moved across state borders or from factory to store, driving up prices. GST replaced this mess with one unified tax across the whole country, ensuring taxes are only paid on the "value added" at each step.

> **In Simple Terms:** In a country as big as India, the central government can't manage everything locally, so it gives states and local bodies the power to spend money on their specific needs. But because the central government collects the biggest taxes (like Income Tax and corporate tax), it has to transfer a chunk of that money back to the states so they can actually afford to do their jobs.

> **In Simple Terms:** Because the Centre and States constantly need to share money, there are specific referees (like the Finance Commission and the GST Council) who sit down, look at the rules, and decide exactly who gets what so it's fair for everyone.

> **In Simple Terms:** The 14th Finance Commission decided to trust the states more. Instead of giving them money with strict conditions on how to spend it, they gave the states a much bigger, unconditional slice of the tax pie and told them to decide their own priorities.

> **In Simple Terms:** The system is rigged slightly in favor of the Centre. The Centre has more ways to raise money (including adding special 'cesses' that they don't have to share with states). Meanwhile, poorer states are caught in a trap: they are poor, so they collect less tax, which means they can't afford to build infrastructure, which keeps them poor.

> **Quick Recall:**

> - BSE and NSE are the largest exchanges in India (11th and 12th globally by market cap in 2019).
> - Market Liquidity is measured by Turnover Ratio and Value Traded Ratio.
> - Major shocks (2008 Crisis, Demonetisation, GST, COVID-19) severely impact market capitalisation and volatility.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Corporate debt market in India = underdeveloped. Equity market = highly developed.
> - Patil Committee focuses on corporate bond market reforms.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Gov Spending ↑ = IS shifts right = Income ↑, Interest Rate ↑ (Expansionary)
> - Taxes ↑ = IS shifts left/steepens = Income ↓, Interest Rate ↓ (Contractionary)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - FRBM Act Target: Fiscal Deficit to 3% of GDP. Revenue Deficit to 0%.
> - Enacted: 2003 (effective 2004).

> - Dual Model: CGST (Centre), SGST (State), IGST (Inter-state).
> - Not in GST: Alcohol, Petroleum, Electricity.
> - GST Council Voting: Centre has 1/3 weight, States have 2/3 weight. 75% required to pass.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Centre handles: Stabilization, Wealth Distribution, Broad-based Taxes (Income/Corporate).
> - States handle: Resource Allocation for regional goods, specific taxes (Stamp duty, Motor vehicle).
> - To fix the resulting gap, the Centre uses Revenue Sharing and Grants.

## 🧮 Important Formulas & Mechanics
- - **Classical / Monetarist**: Expressed via the equation of exchange ($M \times V = P \times Y$). Inflation = Growth rate of money supply + Growth rate of velocity of money - Growth rate of output. Assuming constant velocity and supply-determined output, money supply directly controls inflation.
- > - Equation of Exchange: $M \times V = P \times Y$
- $Y = C + I + G + NX$
- $C = \bar{C} + c(Y - tY)$
