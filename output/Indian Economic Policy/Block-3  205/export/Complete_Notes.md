## Section: Introduction to Inflation and Monetary Policy 🟢

### Core Idea
This section introduces inflation as a common economic issue affecting citizens and highlights the role of monetary policy. It emphasizes the need to understand money to comprehend inflation and briefly mentions that India's monetary framework has evolved to suit economic needs.

> **In Simple Terms:** Inflation is just things getting more expensive over time. Because it impacts everyone, the central bank uses monetary policy to control the amount of money flowing around to keep prices stable.

## Section: 9.2 Money 🔴

### Core Idea
Money serves as a medium of payment, store of value, and unit of account. While commodity money has intrinsic value, modern fiat money derives its value from a government order. The RBI measures money supply using various aggregates (M0 to M4) ranging from highly liquid currency to broader time and savings deposits.

> **In Simple Terms:** Money is anything widely accepted as payment. Modern currency is "fiat" money—it only has value because the government says it does. 

### Key Concepts

#### Functions of Money
1. **Store of Value**: Allows purchasing power to be taken forward from present to future.
2. **Unit of Account**: Acts as a common denominator to measure the value of goods and services.
3. **Medium of Exchange**: Overcomes the "double coincidence of wants" by acting as a generally acceptable means of payment.

#### Types of Money
| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Commodity Money** | Has intrinsic value beyond its use as money. | Gold, silver, cigarettes in WWII. |
| **Fiat Money** | Has no intrinsic value; derives value from government order. | Currency notes and coins. |

### Definitions
- **Money**: Anything that is generally acceptable as a medium of payment. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Fiat Money**: Money that has no intrinsic value and derives its value from a government order or fiat. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Functions: Store of Value, Unit of Account, Medium of Exchange.
> - Measures of money by RBI: M0 (Reserve Money), M1 (Narrow Money), M2, M3 (Broad Money), M4.

## Section: 9.3 Inflation 🔴

### Core Idea
Inflation is the rate of increase in the general price level, typically measured by calculating a weighted average price of a representative basket of goods. High inflation has economic costs, including "shoe-leather" costs, "menu" costs, relative price distortions, and undesirable income redistribution that widens the wealth gap.

> **In Simple Terms:** Inflation reduces the purchasing power of your money. It's measured using indices like CPI (retail) and WPI (wholesale). High inflation hurts the poor the most because they rely on cash and unindexed wages.

### Key Concepts

#### Inflation Measurement in India (CPI vs WPI)
1. **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: Reflects average retail prices faced by consumers. The RBI uses **CPI-Combined** for monetary policy purposes.
2. **Wholesale Price Index (WPI)**: Reflects prices for the first point of wholesale/bulk transactions and serves as a proxy for producer prices.

| Index | Base Year | Compiled By | Focus |
|-------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| CPI (Combined) | 2012 | National Statistical Office | Retail level, used for monetary policy |
| WPI | 2011-12 | Office of Economic Adviser | Wholesale/Producer level |

#### Costs of Inflation
- **Shoe-leather cost**: The inconvenience of making frequent trips to the bank because people hold less cash.
- **Menu cost**: The cost incurred by firms to frequently change their posted prices.
- **Income Redistribution**: Inflation hurts low-income households reliant on cash and wages, widening the gap between rich and poor.

### Definitions
- **Inflation**: The rate of increase in the general price level over a period of time. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Shoe-leather cost**: The inconvenience of frequently visiting the bank to withdraw money during high inflation.
- **Menu cost**: The cost associated with frequently changing the posted prices of products due to changing input prices.

## Section: 9.4 Money and Prices 🔴

### Core Idea
The Quantity Theory of Money (Classical view) posits that the price level is determined by the quantity of money, with inflation directly linked to money supply growth. However, in the short-term, inflation can also be caused by demand-pull or cost-push factors, which are often not responsive to monetary policy. 

> **In Simple Terms:** If the central bank prints more money than the economy actually grows, the extra money just chases the same amount of goods, causing prices to rise.

### Key Concepts

#### Theoretical Perspectives (Classical vs Keynesian)
- **Classical / Monetarist**: Expressed via the equation of exchange ($M \times V = P \times Y$). Inflation = Growth rate of money supply + Growth rate of velocity of money - Growth rate of output. Assuming constant velocity and supply-determined output, money supply directly controls inflation.
- **Keynesian**: Money affects income via interest rates. Increasing money supply lowers interest rates, stimulating demand. It only causes inflation if the economy is operating at full capacity.

#### Causes of Inflation
1. **Demand-pull Inflation**: High aggregate demand (e.g., due to government spending) puts upward pressure on prices.
2. **Cost-push Inflation**: Increased costs of production (e.g., raw materials, supply shocks like poor rainfall) drive prices up.

### Definitions
- **Core Inflation**: The long-term component of inflation calculated by removing volatile components like food and fuel. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Income Velocity of Money ($V$)**: The number of times a rupee is used in transactions; how often a rupee changes hands.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Equation of Exchange: $M \times V = P \times Y$
> - "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." – Milton Friedman.
> - Headline inflation = Core inflation + Non-core (volatile) inflation.

## Section: 9.5 Monetary Policy in India 🔴

### Core Idea
The RBI uses monetary policy to maintain price stability while supporting growth. Historically, the RBI relied on direct instruments (like CRR and SLR) and deficit monetization. However, following financial reforms, the primary operating instrument shifted to indirect, market-based mechanisms like the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) and the Repo Rate.

> **In Simple Terms:** The RBI tweaks interest rates to control how much it costs to borrow money. Cheaper loans mean people spend more, which boosts growth but can cause inflation. 

### Key Concepts

#### Objectives of Monetary Policy
- Primary objective: **Maintain price stability** while keeping in mind the objective of growth.
- Ensure adequate credit flow to productive sectors.

#### Instruments of Monetary Policy
1. **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: The share of net demand and time liabilities (NDTL) banks must maintain in safe, liquid assets like government securities or gold.
2. **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)**: The average daily balance a bank must maintain with the RBI as a percentage of its NDTL.
3. **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF)**: Emerged as the principal operating instrument in 2004. Consists of overnight and term repo auctions.
   - **Repo Rate**: Rate at which RBI provides overnight liquidity to banks against collateral. This is the single independent policy rate.
   - **Reverse Repo Rate**: Rate at which RBI absorbs liquidity from banks.

#### Monetary Policy Transmission
- The process by which changes in RBI policy rates affect lending/deposit rates, asset prices, aggregate demand, and ultimately inflation.
- **Challenges in India**: Transmission is often slow and incomplete because a large portion of bank deposits have fixed maturities, and high non-performing assets (NPAs) constrain bank profitability.

### Definitions
- **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: The share of NDTL that a bank is required to maintain in safe and liquid assets. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)**: The average daily balance a bank must maintain with the RBI as a percentage of its NDTL. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Repo Rate**: The rate at which the Reserve Bank provides overnight liquidity to banks against the collateral of government and other approved securities. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Reverse Repo Rate**: The rate at which the Reserve Bank absorbs liquidity, on an overnight basis, from banks against the collateral of eligible government securities. ⭐ (exam-important)

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Monetary Policy Transmission (Interest Rate Channel)**
1. RBI lowers the policy repo rate.
2. $\rightarrow$ Banks' cost of funds decreases.
3. $\rightarrow$ Banks lower lending rates for households and businesses.
4. $\rightarrow$ Borrowing increases, stimulating aggregate demand.
5. $\rightarrow$ Higher demand puts upward pressure on prices (inflation).

## Section: 9.5.5 Inflation Targeting Framework 🔴

### Core Idea
Following the Urjit Patel Committee recommendations (2014), India adopted a Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework. This shifted the monetary policy objective to maintaining a specific numerical inflation target, bringing transparency, accountability, and operational independence to the RBI. A Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) was instituted to determine the policy rate necessary to achieve this target.

> **In Simple Terms:** Instead of juggling multiple goals at once, the RBI's primary job now is to keep inflation at a specific target (4%). To decide how to do this, a special committee (the MPC) meets and votes on interest rates.

### Key Concepts

#### Flexible Inflation Targeting Framework
- Adopted following the Urjit Patel Committee recommendations.
- A Monetary Policy Framework Agreement in 2015 set the target: **4% with a band of +/- 2%** (i.e., inflation should stay between 2% and 6%).
- If inflation breaches this band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI is deemed to have failed and must submit a report to the Central Government explaining the failure and proposed remedial actions.
- RBI publishes a Monetary Policy Report every six months detailing sources and forecasts of inflation.

#### Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)
- Instituted via an amendment to the RBI Act in 2016.
- Consists of **six members**:
  1. Governor of RBI (Chairperson)
  2. Deputy Governor in charge of Monetary Policy
  3. One officer of the RBI nominated by the Central Board
  4. Three persons appointed by the Central Government
- Meets at least 4 times a year to determine the policy rate. Decisions are by majority vote, with the Governor having a casting vote in the event of a tie.

### Definitions
- **Nominal Anchor**: A variable that policymakers use to "tie down" the price level or its path (e.g., CPI-Combined headline inflation). ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**: A six-member committee instituted to determine the policy rate required to achieve the inflation target. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Inflation Target: 4% (+/- 2%).
> - MPC Composition: 3 RBI members, 3 Government appointees.
> - MPC decisions are published along with minutes after 14 days.

## Section: Introduction to Capital Market and its Regulations 🟢

### Core Idea
A dynamic financial system consists of markets, intermediation, and instruments that allocate resources from surplus segments to deficit segments. It transforms idle wealth into productive financial instruments (bonds, shares), lowering the cost of finance and boosting economic growth.

> **In Simple Terms:** The financial system is the engine that takes money from people who are saving it and gives it to businesses that need to invest it, keeping the economy growing.

### Key Concepts
- Components: Money Market, Capital Market.
- The system replaces idle assets (gold, real estate) with financial instruments, advancing integration between the real and financial sectors.

## Section: 10.2 Role, Significance and Function of Capital Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The capital market is a segment of the financial market for long-term or indefinite maturity assets, divided into primary (new issues) and secondary (trading existing shares) markets. It facilitates the transfer of capital from savers to investors, providing liquidity and efficient resource allocation.

> **In Simple Terms:** The capital market is where companies go to raise long-term money (by selling shares or bonds) and where investors go to buy and trade those assets. 

### Key Concepts

#### Capital Market Structure
1. **Primary Market**: New stock or bond issues are sold (underwriting).
2. **Secondary Market**: Existing shares are traded on organized exchanges (stock exchanges, over-the-counter).

#### Functions of the Capital Market
- Efficient dissemination of information for quick valuation.
- Providing insurance against market and price risk.
- Lowering transactional costs and settlement timings.
- Channelizing funds optimally to accelerate capital formation and industrial growth.

#### Financial Reforms (1991 onwards)
Initiated after the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis to promote a diversified, competitive financial system. Pre-reform, state-owned banks controlled 90% of assets and interest rates were administratively determined. Reforms aimed to mitigate risks, efficiently allocate resources, make the system globally competitive, and open the external sector.

### Definitions
- **Capital Market**: A market for financial assets which have a long or indefinite maturity. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Liquidity**: How easily an asset can be converted to currency without loss of value. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Primary Market**: The market where new stock or bond issues are sold.
- **Secondary Market**: The market where already issued shares are traded through organized exchanges.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Capital market = Primary (new) + Secondary (existing).
> - Regulator: SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India).

Stock markets provide companies with capital for expansion and allow investors to earn returns while diversifying risks. A developed stock market is a barometer of economic growth because it mobilizes domestic savings, allocates capital efficiently, and enforces corporate managerial discipline.

### Key Concepts
- **Market Capitalisation Ratio (MCR)**: Defined as the value of listed shares divided by GDP. It is used as a measure of stock market size and reflects the ability to mobilize capital and diversify risk.

### Definitions
- **Stock Market**: A public market where investors can buy and sell shares representing fractional ownership of a business.
- **Market Capitalisation Ratio (MCR)**: The value of listed shares divided by GDP, used as an indicator of stock market size. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: 10.4 Structure and Performance of Indian Stock Market 🔴

### Core Idea
The Indian stock market has evolved significantly, dominated by the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) and the National Stock Exchange (NSE). Reforms such as nationwide connectivity, electronic trading, and the introduction of platforms for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) have modernized the exchanges. The market's performance metrics (Market Capitalisation Ratio, Turnover Ratio, Value Traded Ratio) reflect its growth, though it remains sensitive to domestic policies (like demonetisation and GST) and global shocks (like the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic).

> **In Simple Terms:** India's stock markets used to be fragmented, but technology like screen-based trading united them. The BSE and NSE are the giants here, and they react sharply to major events like GST or pandemics, showing how connected the stock market is to the real world.

### Key Concepts

#### Evolution and Structure
- Historically, BSE (established 1875) dominated. Pre-1994, regional pricing errors limited market efficiency.
- NSE established satellite communications, creating a unified national market and eliminating pricing discrepancies.
- Several regional stock exchanges were derecognized by SEBI; currently, there are 9 recognized stock exchanges in India.
- **SME Platforms**: BSE launched the first SME platform in 2012 to help start-ups and SMEs raise capital and provide exit options for venture capitalists. A Startups platform followed in 2018 for high-tech industries.

#### Performance Indicators
1. **Market Capitalisation Ratio (MCR)**: Value of listed shares / GDP. (Average 76.26% from 2003-2019).
2. **Turnover Ratio (TOR)**: Value of total shares traded / Market Capitalisation. Measures liquidity.
3. **Value Traded Ratio (VTR)**: Total value of traded shares / GDP.
4. **Volatility**: Measures asset price movement; signals market development and risk.

#### Impact of Major Events
- **2008 Global Financial Crisis**: Drastically reduced MCR and VTR.
- **Demonetisation (2016)** & **GST (2017)**: Caused short-term declines in MCR and TOR, followed by recovery.
- **COVID-19 (2020)**: Resulted in a precipitous fall in market capitalization, severe negative growth for BSE Sensex (-22.65%), and high volatility across sectors (except Healthcare and FMCG which were less affected).

### Definitions
- **Turnover Ratio (TOR)**: The value of total shares traded divided by market capitalisation. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Value Traded Ratio (VTR)**: The total value of traded shares in the stock market divided by GDP. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - BSE and NSE are the largest exchanges in India (11th and 12th globally by market cap in 2019).
> - Market Liquidity is measured by Turnover Ratio and Value Traded Ratio.
> - Major shocks (2008 Crisis, Demonetisation, GST, COVID-19) severely impact market capitalisation and volatility.

## Section: 10.5 Equity Derivatives in India 🔴

### Core Idea
A derivative is a financial contract whose value depends on an underlying asset. In India, derivatives are used primarily for hedging, though speculation and arbitrage also occur. The National Stock Exchange (NSE) dominates the exchange-traded equity derivatives market, which has seen massive success, particularly with single-stock futures and index options.

> **In Simple Terms:** A derivative is like a bet or an insurance policy on the future price of a stock. People use them to protect themselves against price drops (hedging) or to try and make a quick profit (speculation).

### Key Concepts

#### Types of Derivatives Traders
1. **Hedgers**: Use derivatives to offset potential losses in the spot market (primary legal use in India).
2. **Speculators**: Take positions to profit from anticipated price movements.
3. **Arbitrageurs**: Profit from price discrepancies between spot and derivative markets, keeping markets efficient.

#### OTC vs. Exchange-Traded Derivatives
| Feature | Over-the-Counter (OTC) | Exchange-Traded |
|---------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **Format** | Customised, bilaterally negotiated | Standardised contract size and delivery |
| **Risk** | Substantial credit/counterparty risk | Minimal credit risk (guaranteed by clearinghouse) |
| **Regulation** | Generally prohibited in India (except via RBI/Forward Markets Commission) | Traded on organised exchanges (NSE, BSE) |

#### Evolution of Equity Derivatives
- Started in June 2000 (Index futures), followed by index options (2001) and single stock futures/options.
- **NSE Dominance**: Accounts for virtually all exchange-traded derivatives in India (almost 100% market share in equity derivatives).
- **Single Stock Futures**: Hugely popular in India, with NSE ranking among the highest globally in volume.

### Definitions
- **Derivative Security**: A financial contract whose value is derived from the value of something else, such as a stock price, commodity price, or index. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Hedging**: Taking positions in derivatives markets to offset potential losses in the underlying or spot market.
- **Speculation**: Taking positions in the market to profit from anticipated price movements.
- **Arbitrage**: Profiting from discrepancies in the relationship of spot and derivatives prices.

## Section: 10.6 Currency Derivative Market in India 🔴

### Core Idea
Currency derivatives allow buyers and sellers to hedge against foreign exchange risks by locking in future exchange rates. While India has active over-the-counter (OTC) currency forwards and swaps used by banks and traders, there are no exchange-traded currency derivatives.

> **In Simple Terms:** If you are an exporter and worry the value of the Dollar might drop against the Rupee next year, you can use a currency derivative to lock in today's exchange rate for your future dollars.

### Key Concepts
- **Currency Derivative**: A contract deriving its value from an underlying currency exchange rate.
- **Pairs Traded**: USD, EUR, GBP, JPY.
- **Market Structure**: OTC instruments (forwards and swaps) are popular among importers/exporters. Exchange-traded currency derivatives do not currently exist in India.

## Section: 10.7 Long-Term Government Bond and Corporate Debt Market 🔴

### Core Idea
While India's equity and government bond markets are highly developed, the corporate debt market remains in a nascent, illiquid stage. Most corporate bonds are issued via private placement by public sector institutions. The Patil Committee recommended significant reforms to stimulate this market, including rationalizing issuances, enhancing exchange trading, and establishing robust clearing mechanisms (DVP III).

> **In Simple Terms:** It's easy for companies to sell shares to the public in India, but it's much harder for them to borrow money by selling corporate bonds. The government is trying to fix this so companies have more ways to raise money.

### Key Concepts

#### Current State of Corporate Debt Market
- Restricted, arbitrage-driven, and highly illiquid.
- Primary issuances are mostly private placements to institutional investors.
- Secondary market is small. Turnover ratio is relatively high, but the absolute size of outstanding bonds is small.

#### Patil Committee Recommendations
A High-Level Committee on Corporate Bonds and Securitisation proposed:
1. Rationalizing primary issuance procedures to move away from private placements.
2. Facilitating exchange trading (BSE, NSE, FIMMDA).
3. Rationalizing stamp-duty and abolishing tax deduction at source (like G-Secs).
4. Implementing Delivery Versus Payment (DVP) III for net settlement of funds and securities to reduce counterparty risk.

### Definitions
- **Delivery Versus Payment (DVP) III**: A settlement mechanism where both funds and securities legs are settled for net amounts, enhancing efficiency and reducing risk. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Private Placement**: The sale of securities to a relatively small number of select investors as a way of raising capital, rather than a public offering.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Corporate debt market in India = underdeveloped. Equity market = highly developed.
> - Patil Committee focuses on corporate bond market reforms.

## Section: Unit 11 Introduction to Fiscal Policy and IS-LM Framework 🔴

### Core Idea
Fiscal policy involves government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand. Rooted in Keynesian economics born during the Great Depression, the IS-LM framework theoretically demonstrates how fiscal changes (like public works) increase output and employment directly, and indirectly via secondary employment generated by increased consumer expenditure.

> **In Simple Terms:** Fiscal policy is how the government taxes us and spends that money. Keynes argued that during a recession, the government should spend more to create jobs and boost demand.

### Key Concepts

#### Components of Aggregate Demand (AD)
$Y = C + I + G + NX$
- **C**: Private Final Consumption Expenditure (PFCE) - largest share of India's GDP.
- **I**: Gross Capital Formation (GCF) / Business Investment.
- **G**: Government Final Consumption Expenditure (GFCE).
- **NX**: Net Exports (Exports - Imports).

#### Keynesian Consumption Function
$C = \bar{C} + c(Y - tY)$
- Consumption depends on disposable income (National income minus tax).
- **c** (Marginal Propensity to Consume): The increase in consumer expenditure per unit increase in disposable income.

#### Tools of Fiscal Policy
1. **Government Spending**: Changes in revenue or capital expenditure that directly impact GDP. Acts as a counter-cyclical measure.
2. **Taxes**: Changes in direct and indirect taxes that influence economic agent behavior and thus GDP.

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Policy**: Government policy regarding taxation and public spending to influence aggregate demand and the economy. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)**: The increase in consumer expenditure resulting from a one-unit increase in disposable income.

## Section: 11.2.1 Implications of IS-LM Framework for Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
The IS-LM framework visually and mathematically demonstrates how changes in fiscal policy (government spending and taxation) affect the equilibrium interest rate and income. An increase in government spending acts as an expansionary policy shifting the IS curve right, raising both income and interest rates. Conversely, an increase in taxes shifts the IS curve left, lowering income and interest rates.

> **In Simple Terms:** If the government spends more money, it boosts the economy's total income, but also drives up interest rates because there's more demand for money. If the government raises taxes, people have less to spend, so total income drops and interest rates fall.

### Key Concepts

#### Increase in Government Spending
- **Mechanics**: Autonomous government spending increases Aggregate Demand (AD), shifting the IS curve to the right. 
- Output rises, which increases the demand for real money balances. Since money supply is fixed by the RBI, the interest rate must rise to reach equilibrium.
- **Crowding-Out Effect**: The higher interest rate decreases private investment. The government borrowing to finance its spending leaves less savings for the private sector and raises borrowing costs.
- **Indian Context**: Evidence from 1971-2003 suggests government spending often "crowds-in" private investment due to infrastructural bottlenecks, rather than crowding it out.

#### Increase in Taxes
- **Mechanics**: An increase in the proportional income tax rate (contractionary policy) reduces disposable income, lowering consumption and overall AD. 
- The IS curve shifts to the left and its slope increases (value of the multiplier decreases).
- The new equilibrium results in a lower level of income and a lower interest rate. 

### Definitions
- **Crowding-Out Effect**: The decline in private investment as a result of increasing government spending and borrowing. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Expansionary Fiscal Policy**: Policies like increasing government spending or cutting taxes to stimulate economic growth.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Gov Spending ↑ = IS shifts right = Income ↑, Interest Rate ↑ (Expansionary)
> - Taxes ↑ = IS shifts left/steepens = Income ↓, Interest Rate ↓ (Contractionary)

## Section: 11.3 & 11.4 Concepts and Evolution of Fiscal Policy in India 🔴

### Core Idea
Fiscal policy is the use of government revenue collection (taxation) and expenditure to influence a country's economy. In India, the policy architecture is defined by the Constitution (dividing powers between Union and States). Over decades, India's fiscal policy evolved from high-tax, public-sector-driven development (up to the 1980s) to a more liberalized, rationalized tax structure following the 1991 economic crisis.

> **In Simple Terms:** Fiscal policy is the government's budget strategy. Post-independence, India taxed heavily to fund public projects. After the 1991 crisis, the government realized high taxes were causing evasion and debt, leading to massive reforms that simplified and lowered taxes.

### Key Concepts

#### Objectives of Fiscal Policy
- Mobilize additional resources.
- Maintain economic stability (counter-cyclical measure).
- Allocate resources for social development.
- Reduce extreme inequality in income/wealth.

#### India's Fiscal Architecture
- **Federal Structure**: Powers divided via Union List, State List, and Concurrent List. Residuary powers vest with the Union.
- **Planning Commission** (1950): Formulated Five-Year Plans, categorizing expenditures into Plan (developmental projects) and Non-Plan (routine administration).

#### Fiscal Policy until 1990s
- **1950s-1970s**: Focused on self-sufficiency and equity. Extremely high marginal tax rates (up to 97.5% for personal income).
- **Kaldor's Report (1956)**: Highlighted that the tax system was inefficient with a narrow base; led to the introduction of wealth and gift taxes.
- **1980s Deterioration**: Rising developmental expenditure, subsidies, and interest payments led to severe fiscal indiscipline, expanding gross fiscal deficits, and ultimately the 1991 balance of payments crisis.

#### Fiscal Policy 1990s Onwards
- Post-1991 crisis, the Tax Reforms Committee (Chelliah Committee, 1992/1994) recommended simplification.
- Direct taxes were rationalized to three brackets (10%, 20%, 30%).
- Introduction of the service tax to widen the base.

### Definitions
- **Plan Expenditure**: Government spending related to long-term socio-economic goals determined by the planning process.
- **Non-Plan Expenditure**: Routine expenditures of the government for administration, salaries, etc.

## Section: 11.4.3 Fiscal Policy 1990s Onwards (Contd.) 🔴

### Core Idea
Post-1991, India aggressively rationalized its tax structure to correct the fiscal imbalances that led to the balance of payments crisis. Direct taxes (corporate and personal) were simplified and rates reduced. Minimum Alternative Tax (MAT) was introduced to catch "zero-tax companies," while indirect taxes saw the introduction of the service tax and the transition to CENVAT. Despite these revenue-side improvements, expenditure rigidities (high interest and subsidies) kept fiscal health fragile, culminating in a combined debt-to-GDP ratio crisis by the early 2000s.

> **In Simple Terms:** The government lowered tax rates to encourage people to actually pay them, and introduced new taxes (like the service tax) to cast a wider net. However, because they still had massive past debts and subsidies to pay off, their bank account was still bleeding money, requiring strict new rules to force them to balance the budget.

### Key Concepts
- **Direct Tax Reforms**: Corporate tax reduced gradually to 35% (plus dividend tax).
- **Minimum Alternative Tax (MAT)**: Introduced in 1996-97 to ensure companies taking advantage of multiple exemptions still paid a minimum of 30% of their book profits as tax.
- **Indirect Tax Reforms**: Transitioned from complex multi-rate excise to a single central VAT (CENVAT) in 2000-01. Peak customs duty dropped from 150%+ to 15% by 2005. Service tax was introduced in 1994-95.

### Definitions
- **Minimum Alternative Tax (MAT)**: A tax introduced to ensure that "zero-tax companies" using exemptions pay a minimum percentage of their book profits as tax. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **CENVAT**: Central Value Added Tax, which replaced the cascading indirect manufacturing taxes.

## Section: 11.5 The FRBM Act 🔴

### Core Idea
The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act of 2003 was enacted to enforce fiscal discipline, ensure equitable debt distribution over generations, and achieve long-term macroeconomic stability. It mandated progressive reductions in the Fiscal Deficit (to 3% of GDP) and the elimination of the Revenue Deficit.

> **In Simple Terms:** The FRBM Act is a law that forces the government to stick to a strict financial diet. It legally binds the government to stop spending more than it earns on everyday expenses (Revenue Deficit) and to limit its total borrowing (Fiscal Deficit).

### Key Concepts

#### Origins and Objectives
- Introduced following severe fiscal deterioration in the late 1990s and early 2000s (combined Centre-State liabilities hit 81% of GDP by 2003-04).
- Based on the E.A.S. Sarma Committee recommendations.
- **Key Targets**: Reduce Fiscal Deficit to 3% of GDP. Eliminate Revenue Deficit entirely.

#### Subsequent Committees and Implementation
- **Kelkar Task Force (2004)**: Recommended revenue-led fiscal consolidation and the introduction of a comprehensive GST.
- **12th Finance Commission**: Pushed States to enact their own FRBM laws by linking debt relief/incentives to compliance. (Karnataka was the first state to enact an FRBM Act in 2002).
- **Initial Success**: Target of 3% fiscal deficit was seemingly achieved in 2007-08, largely due to high GDP growth and tax buoyancy, not necessarily expenditure reduction.

### Definitions
- **FRBM Act (2003)**: Legislation mandating the central and state governments to institute transparent fiscal management systems and progressively reduce their fiscal and revenue deficits. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - FRBM Act Target: Fiscal Deficit to 3% of GDP. Revenue Deficit to 0%.
> - Enacted: 2003 (effective 2004).

## Section: 11.6 The Global Financial Crisis and Fiscal Policy 🔴

### Core Idea
The 2008 Global Financial Crisis severely tested India's fiscal policy. To combat the economic slowdown, the government implemented massive expansionary stimulus packages (tax cuts and spending hikes). This necessary intervention completely derailed the FRBM consolidation path, ballooning the fiscal deficit. Consequently, the FRBM Act had to be repeatedly amended, leading to the formation of the N.K. Singh Committee to design a new, realistic fiscal roadmap.

> **In Simple Terms:** When the 2008 global crash happened, the Indian government had to abandon its strict financial diet (FRBM Act) and spend heavily to save the economy. Afterwards, they had to rewrite the rules to get back on track.

### Key Concepts

#### Impact and Response to the 2008 Crisis
- Pre-crisis, India was already in an expansionary phase (farm loan waivers, NREGA expansion, 6th Pay Commission).
- Post-crisis, three stimulus packages were launched (excise duty cuts, plan expenditure boosts, interest subsidies).
- The Fiscal Deficit skyrocketed from a budgeted 2.5% to an actual 7.8% (including off-budget oil/fertilizer bonds) in 2008-09.

#### The N.K. Singh Committee (FRBM Review Committee - 2017)
- Formed to review and revise the FRBMA.
- **Key Recommendations**:
  1. Adopt a general government debt ceiling of 60% of GDP by 2022-23 (40% Centre, 20% States).
  2. Adopt fiscal deficit as the key operational target (path to 2.5% by 2022-23).
  3. Reduce Revenue Deficit steadily to 0.8% by 2022-23.

### Definitions
- **Stimulus Package**: A package of economic measures (tax cuts, spending increases) put together by a government to stimulate a floundering economy.

## Section: 11.7 Goods and Services Tax (GST) 🔴

### Core Idea
The Goods and Services Tax (GST) was introduced in 2017 to eliminate the fragmented, cascading tax structure (where taxes were paid on top of taxes) inherent in the previous central and state VAT systems. By unifying goods and services under a single, destination-based tax system, GST created a common national market. India adopted a "dual GST" model (CGST, SGST, IGST) to respect its federal structure.

> **In Simple Terms:** Before GST, goods were taxed multiple times as they moved across state borders or from factory to store, driving up prices. GST replaced this mess with one unified tax across the whole country, ensuring taxes are only paid on the "value added" at each step.

### Key Concepts

#### Rationale for GST
- **Previous System Flaws**: Central VAT (CENVAT) did not cover the distribution stage. State VAT didn't remove the CENVAT load (cascading effect). Service taxes weren't integrated with goods taxes. Central Sales Tax (CST) fragmented the national market.
- **Goal**: To tax the complete supply chain (from production to distribution) under a single, seamless tax.

#### The Dual GST Model
Because taxation powers are divided between the Union and States, both levy GST concurrently on a common base:
- **CGST**: Central Goods and Services Tax (levied by the Centre).
- **SGST**: State Goods and Services Tax (levied by the States).
- **IGST**: Integrated Goods and Services Tax (levied by the Centre on inter-state transactions and imports).
- *Note:* GST is a **destination-based tax**; the revenue accrues to the state where the good/service is consumed.

#### Tax Slabs and Exemptions
- **Main Slabs**: 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, 28%.
- **Exceptions**: Precious metals (3%), affordable housing (1%).
- **Exemptions**: Petroleum products, alcoholic drinks, and electricity remain outside GST (taxed separately by states).
- **Compensation Cess**: Levied on luxury/demerit goods (like tobacco and motor vehicles) on top of the 28% peak rate to compensate States for revenue loss.

#### Institutional Framework
- **GST Council**: Created under Article 279A. Chaired by the Union Finance Minister with State Finance Ministers as members.
  - **Voting Weight**: Centre = 1/3; States combined = 2/3.
  - Decisions require a 3/4ths majority of the weighted votes.
- **Compensation to States Act (2017)**: Guarantees states a 14% revenue growth (over the 2015-16 base year) for 5 years to compensate for any revenue loss due to GST transition.

### Definitions
- **Value Added Tax (VAT)**: A general tax applied at each point of exchange based on the value added to the product.
- **Goods and Services Tax (GST)**: A comprehensive, multi-stage, destination-based tax levied on every value addition. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cascading Effect**: When a tax is levied on a product at every step of the sale, including on the tax levied at the previous step (tax on tax). ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - GST Implemented: July 1, 2017.
> - Dual Model: CGST (Centre), SGST (State), IGST (Inter-state).
> - Not in GST: Alcohol, Petroleum, Electricity.
> - GST Council Voting: Centre has 1/3 weight, States have 2/3 weight. 75% required to pass.

## Section: 12.1 & 12.2 Principles and Pillars of Federal Finance 🔴

### Core Idea
Fiscal federalism deals with how economic responsibilities (revenue raising and spending) are divided between different levels of government (Union, State, Local). This division is guided by economic efficiency: nationwide public goods (defense) go to the Centre, while localized services (street lighting) go to local/state bodies. However, this division inherently creates imbalances—states have high expenditure needs but limited revenue powers (vertical imbalance), and poorer states struggle more than richer ones (horizontal imbalance). These imbalances are corrected via inter-governmental transfers.

> **In Simple Terms:** In a country as big as India, the central government can't manage everything locally, so it gives states and local bodies the power to spend money on their specific needs. But because the central government collects the biggest taxes (like Income Tax and corporate tax), it has to transfer a chunk of that money back to the states so they can actually afford to do their jobs.

### Key Concepts

#### Decentralisation Theorem
- A decentralized system of governance caters better to local tastes and preferences and is therefore more efficient than a centralized system for certain localized public goods.

#### Three Economic Responsibilities of Government (Musgrave)
1. **Resource Allocation**: Suitable for all levels depending on the service.
2. **Distribution of Wealth/Income**: Best suited for the national government (otherwise rich people would just migrate out of high-tax states).
3. **Stabilisation of the Economy**: Best suited for the national government.

#### The Assignments
- **Expenditure Assignment**: Governed by the benefit area (Subsidiarity Principle). Defense -> Union; Public Lighting -> Local.
- **Revenue Assignment**: Broad-based, mobile taxes (Personal Income Tax, Corporate Tax, Customs) are assigned to the Centre to prevent tax-induced market distortions. Less mobile taxes (Property tax, Stamp duty, Motor Vehicle tax) are assigned to the States/Local bodies.

#### Imbalances in Federal Finance
1. **Vertical Imbalance**: Expenditure responsibilities of sub-national governments are higher than their revenue-raising capacity.
2. **Horizontal Imbalance**: Disparity in the ability of constituent units at the same level (e.g., Bihar vs. Haryana) to meet their expenditure needs due to varying levels of economic development.

#### Correcting Imbalances: Inter-governmental Transfers
- **Revenue-sharing**: A specified percentage of central taxes is shared with the states.
- **Grants**: 
  - *Conditional (Specific Purpose)*: Tied to a specific use (e.g., matching grants for Centrally Sponsored Schemes to incentivize state behavior).
  - *Unconditional (General Purpose)*: Best for correcting baseline vertical/horizontal imbalances.

### Definitions
- **Fiscal Federalism**: The financial relations between units of governments in a federal government system. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Vertical Imbalance**: The mismatch between revenue-raising capacity and expenditure responsibilities at different levels of government. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Horizontal Imbalance**: The disparity in the fiscal capacity of government units at the same level. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Centre handles: Stabilization, Wealth Distribution, Broad-based Taxes (Income/Corporate).
> - States handle: Resource Allocation for regional goods, specific taxes (Stamp duty, Motor vehicle).
> - To fix the resulting gap, the Centre uses Revenue Sharing and Grants.

## Section: 12.3 Institutions of Federalism in India 🔴

### Core Idea
To manage the complex financial relationship between the Centre and the States, India relies on key institutions. The most important is the constitutionally mandated Finance Commission, which determines how central taxes are shared. Historically, the Planning Commission also played a massive role, and recently, the GST Council has emerged as a powerful joint decision-making body.

> **In Simple Terms:** Because the Centre and States constantly need to share money, there are specific referees (like the Finance Commission and the GST Council) who sit down, look at the rules, and decide exactly who gets what so it's fair for everyone.

### Key Concepts

#### 1. The Finance Commission (Art. 280)
- Appointed by the President every 5 years (or earlier).
- **Core Mandate**: Recommend tax devolution (sharing of central tax revenue) and grants-in-aid to cover state revenue deficits, fund local bodies, and disaster relief.
- **Process**: Estimates State revenue deficits, recommends a percentage share of the Union's distributable tax pool to the states, and provides revenue-deficit grants to states that still fall short after devolution.
- *Note*: State Finance Commissions (created via 73rd/74th amendments) do the same for State-to-Local body transfers.

#### 2. Planning Commission / NITI Aayog
- *Erstwhile Planning Commission*: Provided Plan grants/loans and a forum for states (National Development Council). It had massive allocative powers.
- *NITI Aayog (2015 onwards)*: Replaced the Planning Commission. Acts purely as an advisory "think tank" with no direct role in allocating fiscal transfers.

#### 3. GST Council (Art. 279A)
- A joint forum of the Centre and States. Takes decisions on GST rates, exemptions, and administrative procedures.
- Represents a pooling of sovereignty, moving towards cooperative federalism.

### Definitions
- **Tax Devolution**: The statutory sharing of central tax revenues with the states, as recommended by the Finance Commission. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: 12.4 The 14th and 15th Finance Commissions 🔴

### Core Idea
The 14th and 15th Finance Commissions marked significant shifts in how the Centre shares its tax revenues with the States. The 14th Finance Commission notably increased the States' share of the divisible pool from 32% to 42%, but simultaneously reduced specific-purpose grants, favoring unconditional transfers to enhance state autonomy. 

> **In Simple Terms:** The 14th Finance Commission decided to trust the states more. Instead of giving them money with strict conditions on how to spend it, they gave the states a much bigger, unconditional slice of the tax pie and told them to decide their own priorities.

### Key Concepts

#### The 14th Finance Commission (FC-14) Highlights
1. **Massive Jump in Devolution**: Increased States' share in net central taxes from 32% to 42%. (Largely to compensate for the discontinuation of Plan grants).
2. **Unconditional Transfers**: Discontinued several sector-specific/conditional grants, empowering states with greater autonomy over fund usage.
3. **Use of Demographic Data**: Shifted the demographic parameter. While keeping 1971 population data, it introduced a new "demographic change" variable (using 2011 data) to reflect current realities without penalizing states that controlled population growth.
4. **Forest Cover Weightage**: Introduced a 7.5% weight for forest cover in the devolution formula to balance ecology with economic development.
5. **Flexible FRBM Targets**: Allowed states a slight relaxation in their 3% Fiscal Deficit limit (up to 3.5%) if they met strict criteria (zero revenue deficit, low debt-to-GSDP, and low interest payments).

#### The 15th Finance Commission (FC-15) Note
- Due to the reorganization of Jammu & Kashmir into Union Territories, the 15th FC marginally reduced the states' share to 41%. 
- It fully shifted to using 2011 population data (causing controversy among southern states with lower population growth).

### Definitions
- **Tax Devolution Formula**: The mathematical formula used by the Finance Commission (incorporating factors like population, area, income distance, and forest cover) to determine each state's share of the central tax pool. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall:**
> - 13th FC Devolution: 32%.
> - 14th FC Devolution: 42%.
> - 15th FC Devolution: 41%.
> - FC-14 Innovation: Included Forest Cover in formula; allowed flexible fiscal deficit targets.

## Section: 12.5 Trends and Issues in Fiscal Federalism in India 🔴

### Core Idea
Despite the constitutional setup, India's fiscal federalism suffers from a strong centralizing bias ("quasi-federalism"). The Centre possesses more robust, elastic revenue sources (and deficit financing options) compared to the States, creating a persistent vertical imbalance. Furthermore, existing public policies have largely failed to break the "vicious cycle" of underdevelopment in poorer states, exacerbating horizontal imbalances (regional inequality).

> **In Simple Terms:** The system is rigged slightly in favor of the Centre. The Centre has more ways to raise money (including adding special 'cesses' that they don't have to share with states). Meanwhile, poorer states are caught in a trap: they are poor, so they collect less tax, which means they can't afford to build infrastructure, which keeps them poor. 

### Key Concepts

#### Centralisation and Vertical Imbalance
- **Quasi-Federalism**: The Indian system leans heavily towards the Centre. Residuary powers, the ability to redraw state boundaries, and dominance in the Concurrent List all empower the Union.
- **Tug-of-War for Resources**: 
  - *Cess and Surcharges*: The Centre increasingly levies cesses (now ~18.5% of gross tax revenue), which are explicitly excluded from the divisible pool shared with states.
  - *Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)*: The Centre initiates schemes on state subjects but requires states to provide matching funds. This distorts state priorities, forcing them to spend limited resources on central mandates rather than local needs.

#### Regional Equity and Horizontal Imbalance
- **The Vicious Cycle of Underdevelopment**: Poor Initial Conditions -> Low Public Resources -> Low Public Investment -> Low Levels of Development.
- **Failure of Equalization**: 
  - Except for Finance Commission transfers, other central funds (like former Plan assistance or CSS) have not been progressively targeted toward poorer states.
  - *FRBM Constraints*: Strict borrowing limits (FRBM Act) prevent poorer states from borrowing heavily to invest in infrastructure, locking them out of debt-financed growth that could break the cycle.

### Definitions
- **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)**: Schemes funded partially by the central government but implemented by states in areas that fall under the State List, usually requiring matching state funds. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Cess and Surcharge**: Additional taxes levied by the central government for specific purposes. Crucially, these are *not* shared with the states.
- **Quasi-Federalism**: A federal system with a strong unitary bias, where the central government holds significantly more power than the constituent units.

## Section: Appendix 12.1 & 12.2 Terms of Reference (ToR) 🟢

### Core Idea
This section provides the exact legal wording of the Terms of Reference (ToR) issued by the President to the 14th and 15th Finance Commissions. The ToR outlines the specific tasks the commissions must undertake, the constraints they must operate under, and the parameters they should consider when formulating their tax devolution and grant recommendations.

### Key Concepts

#### ToR of the 14th Finance Commission (Appendix 12.1)
- **Mandate**: Distribute net tax proceeds, govern grants-in-aid, and supplement resources for Panchayats and Municipalities.
- **Key Directives**:
  - Review the state of finances, deficits, and debt levels.
  - Suggest measures for maintaining a stable fiscal environment, including amendments to the FRBM Act.
  - Use 1971 population data, but permitted to account for post-1971 demographic changes.
  - Balance ecology/environment with development (leading to the forest cover weightage).
  - Assess the impact of the proposed Goods and Services Tax (GST).

#### ToR of the 15th Finance Commission (Appendix 12.2)
- **Mandate**: Standard devolution and grants tasks.
- **Key Directives**:
  - Propose a fiscal consolidation roadmap considering the responsibility of Centre and States to adhere to debt/deficit levels.
  - Examine if revenue deficit grants should be provided at all.
  - Consider the impact of the 14th FC's enhanced devolution on the Union's finances.
  - **Major Shift**: Mandated the use of **2011 population data** (a departure from the 1971 mandate used by previous commissions).
  - Propose measurable performance-based incentives for States (e.g., GST expansion, population control, ease of doing business, sanitation).
  - *Additional ToR*: Examine a separate funding mechanism for defense and internal security.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - The ToR is the specific "instruction manual" given to a Finance Commission by the President before it begins its work.
> - 14th FC ToR allowed demographic changes but anchored to 1971.
> - 15th FC ToR explicitly mandated the use of 2011 population data.
