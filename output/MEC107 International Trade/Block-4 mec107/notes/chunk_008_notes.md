## Section: 10.2.4 The Monetary Approach (continued) 🔴
<!-- See chunk 007 for start of this section -->

### Core Idea
The monetary approach handles fixed and flexible exchange rates differently but relies on the same core principle: the balance of payments deficit/surplus is a symptom of stock disequilibrium in the money market.

### Key Concepts

#### Monetary approach and flexible exchange rate
Under flexible exchange rates:
- **Currency Depreciation**: Results from an excess in the stock of **money supply**. (Higher money supply = higher inflation = currency loses value against others).
- **Currency Appreciation**: Results from an excess in the stock of **money demanded**. (Lower inflation = currency gains value).
- Unlike fixed exchange rates where equilibrium is painfully restored by international reserve flows, under flexible rates, equilibrium occurs automatically through domestic price changes that alter the valuation of the money stock. The central bank retains dominant control over its domestic money supply.
- Interestingly, inflationary pressures are shielded; a high-inflation nation transmits it mostly via rising imports rather than massive outflows of reserve money.

#### Criticisms of the Monetary Approach
1. **Demand for money is not constant**: It assumes stable money demand, but real-world money demand is highly unstable in the short run.
2. **Ignores "Sterilization"**: Assumes a direct unblocked link between money supply and BoP. In reality, central banks routinely use "sterilized interventions" to block this link and maintain both foreign reserves and desired domestic money levels.
3. **Only works in the long run**: The automatic self-adjustment mechanism completely disregards short-run BoP disequilibria because real-sector changes take a long time to materialize.
4. **Ignores real-sector shocks**: Disregards structural problems like external debt crises, acting as if purely altering the money supply easily fixes trade flaws.

## Section: 10.2.5 Synthesising all the approaches to BoP 🔴

### Core Idea
No single approach explains everything; they are complementary rather than strictly competitive. Policymakers must use all of them simultaneously to form a complete macroeconomic picture.

> **In Simple Terms:** 
> - Elasticity = Microeconomics (goods market relative prices).
> - Keynesian/Absorption = Macroeconomics (goods market income/expenditure).
> - Monetary = Disequilibrium theory (money market mismatches).

### Key Concepts

#### Differences and Complementarity
- The **Elasticities** approach looks at flow equilibrium and seeks to explain the *balance of trade* (just physical goods).
- The **Absorption/Keynesian** approaches look at flow equilibrium and seek to explain the *current account balance* (goods + services). The Absorption approach focuses on real output vs expenditure.
- The **Monetary** approach looks at stock-flow equilibrium and seeks to explain the *overall balance of payments* (current + capital). It concentrates on nominal demand for goods/securities and money accumulation.

## Section: 10.4 KEY WORDS 🟡

### Definitions
- **The Asset Approach to BoP**: Another name for the monetary approach under flexible rates, focusing exclusively on international investors trying to decide between securities denominated in two different currencies. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Domestic credit policy**: A way of measuring and controlling the growth in the total amount of money in a country's economy.
- **Sterilization policy**: Form of monetary action where a central bank seeks to limit the effect of inflows and outflows of capital on the domestic money supply, usually by buying or selling domestic financial assets to offset its foreign exchange interventions. ⭐ (exam-important)

> **Quick Recall Synthesis:**
> To fix a deficit: 
> - Elasticity says: **Devalue currency** (if elasticities > 1).
> - Absorption says: **Reduce spending** (Tight monetary/fiscal policy).
> - Keynesian says: **Output and employment will drop**.
> - Monetary says: **Just wait** (It fixes itself by draining excess money, provided you don't print more).
