## Section: 7.5 FORMS OF EXCHANGE RATE REGIME (Continued) 🔴
<!-- See chunk 001 for start of this section -->

### Core Idea
While floating exchange rates offer resource allocation benefits and monetary independence, market forces alone can cause severe volatility and misallocation of resources. To combine the benefits of both fixed and floating regimes, most countries today employ a "managed float," where market forces determine the rate but the central bank acts to smooth out extreme fluctuations.

> **In Simple Terms:** Instead of letting a boat drift entirely on its own (free float) or tying it firmly to the dock (fixed), a managed float means the captain steers the boat only when the water gets too rough.

### Key Concepts

#### Disadvantages of Floating Rates
Market forces may not establish the optimal rate, causing resource misallocation. Total lack of official intervention is impossible since domestic fiscal and monetary policies inevitably influence the rate. Wild fluctuations subject the economy to currency speculators, introducing severe uncertainty to import/export prices and risking capital flight.

#### Managed Floating (Intermediate Regime)
Also known as a "dirty float," this is an intermediate system where the central bank attempts to control fluctuations within a narrow band, but fundamental demand and supply still guide the trend. Authorities intervene using direct open market operations or interest rate manipulation. This represents a compromise between free-floating (zero intervention) and fixed (constant intervention) regimes.

#### Spectrum of Exchange Rate Regimes
Exchange rate arrangements are not binary but exist on a spectrum based on the frequency of intervention and the required volume of international reserves.
- **Fixed (Pegged)**: Highest intervention, largest reserve requirement.
- **Adjustable Peg / Crawling Peg**: Occasional, pre-arranged adjustments.
- **Managed Float / Wider Band**: Occasional intervention to curb volatility.
- **Freely Floating**: Zero direct intervention, minimal reserve requirement.

#### Changing Trends and The Need for Reform
Due to financial instability and abrupt capital flow reversals, there is growing demand for international monetary reform (sometimes called "Bretton Woods II"). Some propose transitioning away from reliance on a single dominant reserve currency (like the US dollar), which forces the issuer to run external deficits, toward a multi-currency system or using IMF Special Drawing Rights (SDRs).

### Definitions
- **Managed Floating Rates**: Rates under which authorities intervene to limit short-term currency fluctuations without the goal to keep the exchange rate at a certain predetermined level. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Dirty float**: A currency that floats in value in terms of other currencies but is not free of government intervention. Same as managed float.
- **Adjustable peg system**: A fixed rate system where rates can be pegged at new levels to correct fundamental disequilibria.
- **Crawling Pegs**: A regime where the exchange rate is regularly adjusted in the same direction by a pre-arranged amount.
- **Currency Boards**: A regime where the only role of monetary policy is to keep the domestic currency’s value constant relative to a particular foreign currency.
- **Dollarization**: When one country unilaterally adopts the currency of another.
- **Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)**: An international reserve asset created by the IMF in 1969 to support the Bretton Woods system. Defined as the weighted average of four major currencies. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: 7.6 INDIA’S EXCHANGE RATE REGIME 🟡

### Core Idea
India's exchange rate policy has evolved in response to macroeconomic reforms, transitioning from a par value system, mapping to a basket-peg, and eventually maturing into a managed float. The present system pairs market-determined rates with central bank interventions designed solely to curb excess volatility and speculative attacks.

> **In Simple Terms:** India lets the market decide the value of the Rupee, but the Reserve Bank of India acts like a referee, stepping in only if players start getting too wild and the Rupee jumps or falls too sharply.

### Key Concepts

#### Evolution of India's Exchange Rate
Post-independence, India used the par value system, migrating to a basket-peg, and finally shifting to a managed float in line with early 1990s liberalization. India holds full convertibility on the current account (achieved August 20, 1994) but manages capital account convertibility carefully due to the challenges of the "impossible trinity."

#### Principal Features of Current Regime
1. **Market Determined**: Exchange rates are set by the market.
2. **Control Framework**: The regime operates within an exchange control framework.
3. **RBI Intervention**: The RBI actively intervenes to modulate volatility and sharp depreciations.
4. **Primary Currency**: The US dollar is the principal currency for RBI transactions.
5. **Reference Rate**: The RBI announces a daily Reference Rate (noon in Bombay) for SDR transactions and the Asia Clearing Union.

### Definitions
- **Convertibility of rupee on current account**: Implies the Rupee can be converted to any foreign currency at existing market rates freely for the trade of goods and services. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Convertibility of rupee on capital account**: Freedom to convert rupees into foreign currency and back for capital transactions. India currently does not have full capital account convertibility.
- **Full Convertibility**: When there are no current or capital account restrictions for the free movement of funds.
- **Exchange control**: A cap imposed by a monetary authority on foreign currency buying by domestic buyers.
- **Clean float**: A regime where the monetary authority does not intervene in the foreign exchange market in any way whatsoever.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - India’s regime is a **Managed Float**.
> - Rupee is fully convertible on the **Current Account**, but NOT on the **Capital Account**.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing the Indian Rupee is fully convertible globally. → ✅ Correct: The Rupee is only fully convertible on the current account (trade), while capital account conversions remain restricted.
