## Section: Foreign Trade Policy of 2015-20 🔴

### Core Idea
The FTP 2015-20 aimed to integrate trade policy with campaigns like "Make in India." Its primary target was to increase India's share of global trade from 2.1% to 3.5% and double exports to $900 billion by 2020 through drastic simplifications, notably consolidating multiple incentive schemes into MEIS and SEIS.

> **In Simple Terms:** The 2015-20 policy was all about cutting red tape. It merged a confusing mix of export bonuses into two simple schemes—one for goods, one for services—and allowed businesses to do their paperwork completely online.

### Key Concepts

#### MEIS and SEIS Integration
The policy dramatically streamlined export incentives. For goods, it launched the Merchandise Exports from India Scheme (MEIS), and for services, the Services Exports from India Scheme (SEIS). Exporters received "duty credit scrips" (which act like a currency to pay import duties) ranging from 2% to 5%, with zero conditionality. These scrips were fully transferable.

#### Ease of Doing Business
A myriad of structural simplifications characterized the policy:
- **EPCG Export Obligation**: Reduced from 90% to 75% for capital goods sourced locally.
- **Single Window Interface**: Allowed traders to electronically lodge documents with customs at a single point, bypassing multiple physical agencies.
- **24*7 Customs Clearance**: Implemented at 19 seaports and 17 air cargo complexes.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Policy Criticisms and Roadblocks:**
1. **WTO Dispute**: In 2019, a WTO panel ruled that India's export subsidies (under MEIS and SEIS) violate WTO rules and must be withdrawn, forcing a rethink of subsidy-based promotion.
2. **FTA Skepticism**: There's a prevailing belief that Free Trade Agreements (FTAs) have disadvantaged India, highlighted by India's November 2020 exit from the RCEP.
3. **GST Bottlenecks**: Implementational delays regarding input duty credit and refunds harmed exporter liquidity.

### Definitions
- **Status Holders**: Entrepreneurs certified by the DGFT as major export players who are allowed to self-certify their manufactured goods as originating from India. ⭐ (exam-important)

## Section: Services Trade Policy 🟡

### Core Idea
Due to rapid technological shifts, the services sector required its own distinct trade policy. Supported vigorously under SEIS, setting corresponding regulatory regimes for services is crucial given their multiplier impact on overall economic activity.

## Section: Recalibrating India's Foreign Trade Policy 🟡

### Core Idea
Given the global economic slowdown and rising protectionism, India needs a specialized body to perform deep, research-based trade policy analysis. Empowering institutions like India's Tariff Commission (TC) is essential to navigate complex negotiations and the impact of non-tariff barriers.

> **In Simple Terms:** Since global trade is getting more complicated and protective, India needs a dedicated group of experts (like a revived Tariff Commission) to constantly track changing rules, tariffs, and tech trends to keep Indian exporters safe.

### Key Concepts

#### Reimagining the Tariff Commission (TC)
A key proposal is reviving the TC to mirror bodies like the USITC. Its responsibilities would map implications of tariff offers by trade partners, explore AI tools to track export markets, and provide cohesive policy advice linking exchange rates to domestic prices.

## Section: Impact of Trade Policy Reforms 🟡

### Core Idea
Trade liberalization fundamentally accelerated India's economic growth. While capital and intermediate goods saw rapid tariff reductions that significantly boosted manufacturing productivity and access to quality inputs, consumer goods remained protected much longer.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing reforms instantly boosted all sectors equally. → ✅ Correct: The pace varied widely. Protection for capital/intermediate goods was slashed rapidly, whilst consumer goods stayed protected for nearly a decade post-1991.

## Section: Foreign Trade Policy 2023 🔴

### Core Idea
The FTP 2023 pivots from pure subsidies towards "remission of duties" and collaborative export promotion. It introduces new pathways for e-commerce hubs and high-end technological items, and notably offers an Amnesty Scheme for old, stalled authorizations.

> **In Simple Terms:** Since the WTO banned the old subsidies, the 2023 policy focuses instead on making the process cheaper and faster using tech (like e-commerce hubs) and helping exporters clear off their old, stuck paperwork without heavy penalties.

### Key Concepts

#### Four Pillars of FTP 2023
1. **Incentive to Remission**: Moving away from flat subsidies to remitting duties.
2. **Export Promotion via Collaboration**: Strengthening synergy between Exporters, States, Districts, and Indian Missions.
3. **Ease of Doing Business**: Process re-engineering, IT-based schemes, and transaction cost reduction.
4. **Emerging Areas**: Facilitating export of dual-use technology items (SCOMET) and developing e-commerce export hubs.

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Schemes under FTP 2023:**
- **One-time Amnesty Scheme**: To close old pending authorizations and allow exporters to start afresh.
- **Towns of Export Excellence**: Recognizing new towns for localized export promotion.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - FTP 2015-20 brought MEIS, SEIS, and a 2.1% to 3.5% trade share target. 2019 WTO ruling forced their withdrawal.
> - FTP 2023 focuses on the "Incentive to Remission" shift, e-commerce, SCOMET items, and the Amnesty Scheme.
> - RCEP: India withdrew in Nov 2020 over FTA concerns.
> - Prior to 1991, India experienced the "Hindu rate of growth" of just 3 to 4 percent.
