## Section: Theory of Intra-Industry Trade (Continued) 🔴

### Mechanisms / Processes
**Effects of Free Trade in Krugman's Model**
When two identical countries ('A' and 'B') open up free trade, they merge into an "integrated economy" twice the size of either individually. This leads to:
1. **Consumption Fall**: Consumption of each specific variety falls.
2. **Real Wage Rise**: Real wage (w/p) rises due to increased efficiency.
3. **Variety Increase**: Total number of varieties available to consumers increases because the global market offers more varied options.
4. **Scale Effect**: Output produced by each surviving firm increases to exploit economies of scale.
5. **Selection Effect**: The total number of varieties produced within each individual country falls as some firms are forced to exit due to competition.

**Ideal Variety Approach (Lancaster & Helpman)**
1. Every consumer has a preference for a specific "ideal variety" rather than all varieties symmetrically.
2. The supply side features free entry and exit, identical cost functions, and scale economies.
3. Due to scale economies, an infinite number of varieties cannot be produced, so some consumers settle for less-than-ideal varieties.
4. Profit-maximizing behavior ensures no two firms produce the exact same variety, leading to "perfect monopolistic competition."

**IIT in Identical Products (Brander & Krugman)**
- Trade in completely identical products can happen due to "reciprocal dumping" or "cross-hauling."
- Oligopolistic firms maximize profits by bearing transport costs to dump their identical products into foreign markets, because entering a foreign market where their share is smaller can yield higher marginal revenue than selling more domestically.

**IIT in Intermediate Products (Ethier)**
- Returns to scale depend on the size of the world market, leading to global dispersion of production stages and promoting trade in differentiated intermediate goods. 

### Key Concepts

#### Vertical IIT Models
Unlike horizontal models, vertical IIT models focus on differences in factor endowments and income distribution:
- **Falvey (1981)**: Higher quality products need a higher capital-labor ratio. Capital-abundant (higher per-capita income) countries export high quality; labor-abundant countries export low quality. IIT intensity correlates with per-capita income differences.
- **Falvey and Kierzkowski (1987)**: Demand for a product's quality depends uniquely on an individual's income. Unequal income distribution drives the aggregation of demand for various qualities.
- **Flam & Helpman (1987)**: Quality differentiation is driven by un-equal endowments of effective labor/skills rather than capital. 

| Attribute | Horizontal IIT | Vertical IIT |
|-----------|----------------|--------------|
| Primary Driver | Economies of scale & Imperfect competition | Factor endowments & Income distribution |
| Expected Partner Similarity | Similar countries (similar endowments) | Dissimilar countries |

### Definitions
- **Reciprocal Dumping (Cross-hauling)**: A situation in oligopoly models where firms dump identical products into each other's domestic markets because marginal revenue abroad exceeds domestic marginal revenue. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Scale Effect**: The expansion of output by surviving firms following trade liberalization.
- **Selection Effect**: The forced exit of some firms following trade liberalization, reducing the number of varieties produced in each individual country.

## Section: Measurement of IIT 🟡

### Core Idea
Analyzing intra-industry trade empirically presents two major challenges: defining what actually constitutes an "industry" due to overlapping trade categories, and finding an accurate mathematical index to express the intensity of IIT relative to total trade.

### Key Concepts

#### The Problem of Categorical Aggregation
To measure IIT, we must define an industry by how well products substitute in production and consumption. However, official trade classifications often group products with different production functions or end uses into a single category. Higher levels of aggregation artificially inflate the recorded extent of IIT. Economists generally agree that products under 4- or 6-digit Harmonized System (HS) codes roughly correspond to an industry.

#### Grubel Lloyd (GL) Index
The most common measure of IIT intensity within an industry. 
Formula: `GLi = ( (Xi + Mi) - |Xi - Mi| ) / (Xi + Mi) * 100`
Where Xi = Exports, Mi = Imports. 
- If GLi = 0, there is no IIT (either Xi or Mi is zero).
- If GLi = 100, there is perfect IIT (Xi = Mi).

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Assuming the GL index measures the *absolute value* or volume of IIT. → ✅ Correct: The GL index is independent of absolute volume and only measures intensity/proportion.
- ❌ Mistake: Assuming finer trade classifications increase the GL index. → ✅ Correct: Higher statistical aggregation (broad generic groups) generally inflates the GL index due to categorical aggregation issues.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Defining an industry is tricky; aggregation causes measurement problems.
> - GL index measures INTENSITY of IIT from 0-100.
> - GL formula uses absolute difference of exports and imports divided by total trade.
> - IIT can occur in identical goods (reciprocal dumping) and intermediate goods.
