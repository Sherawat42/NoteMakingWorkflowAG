## Section: The 2007-2008 Global Financial Crisis (Continued) 🔴

### Core Idea
The 2008 Global Financial Crisis officially surfaced in late 2007 when institutional investors realized the true risk of subprime mortgages. A domino effect caused the real estate bubble to burst, leading to panic selling, a severe liquidity crunch in the banking system, and massive macroeconomic fallout including surging unemployment and stock market crashes. The crisis was combatted using an unprecedented mix of monetary liquidity injections and aggressive fiscal stimulus.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine everyone realizing at the exact same moment that the "safe" investments they bought are actually worthless. Panic ensues, banks stop lending to anyone, businesses can't get cash to operate, and workers get laid off in droves. 

### Key Concepts

#### Root Causes of the 2008 Crisis
Several compounding factors triggered the meltdown:
1. **US Banking System Malpractice**: Banks ignored minimum capital ratios to maximize returns, leading to aggressively high leverage ratios. They stopped verifying borrower creditworthiness.
2. **Global Imbalances**: Export-driven Asian economies (like China) ran massive trade surpluses, pegging their currencies and pouring trillions of dollars into US Treasury bonds. This flood of foreign capital artificially suppressed US interest rates, fueling cheap mortgages.
3. **Originate-to-Distribute (OTD) Model**: Securitisation allowed banks to originate risky loans and immediately sell them off, removing the incentive to care if the borrower would default.
4. **Flawed US Monetary Policy**: The Federal Reserve kept interest rates abnormally low for an extended period, which incentivized banks to take on riskier borrowers to maintain profit margins.
5. **Political Mandates**: Government initiatives aimed at tackling inequality aggressively pushed banks to expand homeownership by relaxing underwriting standards.

#### Sequence of the Collapse
1. Early 2007: Major mortgage backers refuse to acquire more high-risk loans; specialized lenders declare bankruptcy.
2. Mid 2007: Credit Rating Agencies (CRAs) downgrade asset-backed instruments in massive numbers.
3. August 2007: Shadow banking system faces a severe liquidity freeze; short-term funding is withdrawn. 
4. September 2008: The panic culminates. Lehman Brothers declares bankruptcy, sparking a full-blown global panic.

#### Macroeconomic Effects
The crisis shocked both credit supply and demand:
- **Interest Rates Skyrocketed**: While government bond rates stayed low, corporate borrowing rates spiked, freezing corporate investment.
- **Credit Supply Froze**: Syndicated loans plummeted. Banks hoarded cash.
- **Real Economy Crash**: Consumer wealth eroded as housing prices fell 30% and stocks dropped ~50% by mid-2009. Nine million US jobs were lost by 2009.

#### Initial Policy Responses
- **Monetary Policy**: Central banks injected massive liquidity, guaranteed deposits, and slashed interest rates until they hit a "liquidity trap" (near 0% where monetary policy loses further effectiveness).
- **Fiscal Policy**: The government rolled out the **Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)** to acquire bank shares and recapitalize banks. They also passed the **American Recovery and Reinvestment Act** ($780 billion in tax cuts and spending) which ballooned the US deficit to 9% by 2010.

### Definitions
- **Syndicated Loans**: Large loans provided by a group of lenders (syndicate) to a single corporate borrower. ⭐
- **Liquidity Trap**: An economic situation where interest rates are brought down to near zero, rendering further conventional monetary policy ineffective at stimulating the economy. ⭐
- **Originate-to-Distribute (OTD) Model**: A banking model where loans are originated not to be held until maturity, but to be immediately securitised and distributed to investors.
- **TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program)**: A US government initiative to purchase toxic assets and equity from financial institutions to strengthen the financial sector.

### Mechanisms / Processes
> **The Domino Effect of 2008:**
> Easy monetary policy & Political pressure → Subprime lending boom (OTD Model) → Interest rates rise → Widespread borrower defaults → Asset-Backed Securities downgraded → Banks hoarding cash (Liquidity Crunch) → Corporate borrowing costs spike → Investment and Consumption collapse → Recession & High Unemployment.

## Section: Currency Crises 🟡

### Core Idea
A currency crisis happens when investors lose faith in a nation's economy and rapidly move their money out, causing the country's currency value to plummet. It often forces central banks to exhaust their foreign reserves trying to defend the currency's value. 

> **In Simple Terms:** If investors suddenly believe a country is in financial trouble, they rush to sell their investments and convert their local money into a safer foreign currency, causing the local currency's value to crash.

### Key Concepts

#### Mechanics of a Currency Crisis
- **Capital Flight**: A rapid withdrawal of money from a country by investors. As they sell the domestic currency to buy foreign currency, the exchange rate worsens.
- **Central Bank Intervention**: Central banks attempt to stabilize the currency by selling off foreign reserves (like USD or Gold) to buy back their own currency to maintain a fixed peg or manage a float.
- **Indicators**: Current account deficits, rapid inflation, or investor uneasiness over government policies precede these crises. 
- *Historical Example*: The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis began as a currency crisis when Thailand delinked the Baht from the US Dollar due to depleted foreign reserves, sparking regional panic.

### Definitions
- **Currency Crisis**: Occurs when the value of a country's currency sharply declines due to volatile markets and lack of faith in the economy. ⭐
- **Capital Flight**: The rapid withdrawal of investments and conversion of domestic currency into foreign currency by investors fearing instability.

## Section: Historical Financial Crises 🟢

### Core Idea
Financial crises have recurred throughout history, often triggered by overconfidence, abrupt policy shifts, or speculative bubbles. 

### Key Concepts
- **Credit Crisis of 1772 (London)**: Rapid loan expansion fueled by colonial wealth resulting in overconfidence.
- **Great Depression 1929-39 (US)**: Wall Street crash exacerbated by poor policy decisions.
- **OPEC Oil Price Shock 1973**: Retaliation halting oil exports to the US/allies causing severe inflation and shortages.
- **Asian Crisis 1997**: Speculative capital flows and excessive debt, sparked by Thailand abandoning its USD peg.
- **Global Financial Crisis 2007-08**: Bursting of the US housing bubble dragging down key financial institutions.
