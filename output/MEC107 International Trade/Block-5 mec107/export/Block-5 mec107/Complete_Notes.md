## Section: Globalisation of Financial Markets 🟡

### Core Idea
The globalisation of financial markets is an ongoing process of increasing interdependence, where borrowers, lenders, and investors have worldwide access to capital. This transformation is driven by technological advances, financial innovation, and liberalization policies that have dismantled cross-border controls.

> **In Simple Terms:** Financial markets are no longer restricted by national borders. Like the internet connecting people globally, modern financial systems connect money globally, allowing funds to seamlessly flow to wherever they are needed.

### Key Concepts

#### Drivers of Globalisation
The growth in cross-border financial flows is driven by institutions expanding geographically, the deregulation of capital controls since the 1980s, and technological advances that allow rapid data computation and secure information exchange. The invention of instruments like derivatives also allows participants to customize their risk exposures.

#### Risks in Global Markets
While globalisation increases efficiency, it also spreads vulnerabilities. The interconnectedness means a crisis in one region (e.g., the 1997 East Asian crisis or 2008 US crisis) can have disruptive and protracted effects across the global economy. 

### Definitions
- **Derivative**: A financial instrument whose value is derived from the performance of an underlying asset, making it possible for borrowers and lenders to customize and adjust their risk exposures over time.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Liberalization & Technological Advances → 
2. Removal of distance/geography barriers → 
3. Increased cross-border capital flows → 
4. Deepened global financial integration & interdependence.

> **Quick Recall:**
> - Globalisation is a process, not a fixed state.
> - Key drivers: technology, deregulation, innovation (derivatives).
> - Financial markets remain prone to contagious crises.

## Section: International Financial Markets & Instruments 🔴

### Core Idea
International financial markets facilitate the buying and selling of foreign assets across borders by individuals, institutions, and governments. They include markets for stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities, and derivatives, relying on a diverse set of financial instruments to function as stores of value, means of payment, and tools for risk transfer.

> **In Simple Terms:** If you want to buy goods from another country, you need their currency. The foreign exchange market is the giant global marketplace where different countries' currencies and financial assets are traded 24 hours a day.

### Key Concepts

#### The Foreign Exchange Market
A fundamental part of international finance, the forex market allows conversion of currencies and provides insurance against foreign exchange risk. It is the largest asset class in the world, marked by high liquidity and continuous trading (24 hours a day, Monday to Friday). Most transactions don't involve physical currency but rather the transfer of electronic balances.

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Liquidity | Huge volume of trading, making it the largest asset class in the world. |
| Operation Time | Continuous operation 24/5 (from Sunday in Sydney to Friday in NY). |
| Major Centers | London (largest), New York, Tokyo. |
| Profit Margins | Low relative profit margins compared to fixed income markets. |

#### Major Players
- **Borrowers/Issuers**: Corporates, banks, governments needing forex funds.
- **Lenders/Investors**: Agents in surplus looking to invest.
- **Intermediaries**: Central banks, commercial banks, brokers, and financial institutions handling the transactions.

#### Purposes of Foreign Exchange Markets
Businesses use these markets for four main reasons:
1. Converting export or foreign investment income into domestic currency.
2. Paying foreign entities for products/services in their local currency.
3. Investing spare cash short-term in foreign money markets.
4. **Currency Speculation**: Moving funds short-term to profit from changing exchange rates.

#### Types of Financial Instruments
Financial instruments represent a legal agreement of monetary value. They fall into three main categories:
1. **Equity-based**: Represents ownership of the asset.
2. **Debt-based**: Represents a loan made by an investor to the owner.
3. **Foreign exchange**: Primarily currency agreements and derivatives.

### Definitions
- **Financial Markets**: Places where buyers and sellers participate in the trade of assets such as equities, bonds, currencies, and derivatives.
- **Foreign Exchange Market**: The organizational setting where businesses, governments, and banks buy and sell foreign currencies and other debt instruments. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Foreign Exchange Risk**: The adverse consequences of unpredictable changes in exchange rates. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Financial Instruments**: Real or virtual documents representing a legal agreement involving monetary value, acting as a means of payment, store of value, and mechanism to transfer risk. ⭐ (exam-important)
- **Currency Speculation**: The short-term movement of funds from one currency to another in the hopes of profiting from shifts in exchange rates.

## Section: Types of International Financial Markets 🟡

### Core Idea
International financial markets are broadly classified into primary (new issues) and secondary (trading existing instruments) markets. Depending on the operations, they further split into money, credit, capital, and equity markets. The Eurocurrency market plays a major wholesale role in this ecosystem, providing highly liquid capital without the interference of a specific country's central bank.

> **In Simple Terms:** Financial markets are like different departments in a global supermarket. The money market handles short-term cash, the capital market handles long-term investments, and the Eurocurrency market is a special "offshore" zone where you can borrow a country's currency outside of that country.

### Key Concepts

#### Classification of Markets
Markets are primarily categorized as:
- **Money markets**: Short-term exchange transactions.
- **Credit markets**: Deposit taking and lending.
- **Capital markets**: Long-term issuance of securities.
- **Equity markets**: Issuance of ownership shares (equities).

#### Eurocurrency Market
This wholesale market deals with currencies used outside their country of origin (e.g., US dollars traded in Germany are "Eurodollars"). It is not subject to the domestic central bank's reserve requirements, allowing banks (Euro banks) to offer higher yields to depositors and fixed/competitive rates to borrowers. It provides medium to long-term lending on floating interest rates, which are typically rolled over every 3 or 6 months.

### Definitions
- **Eurocurrency Market**: A wholesale market where transactions are denominated in a currency other than the domestic currency of the country where the transaction takes place. ⭐
- **LIBOR (London Inter-bank Offer Rate)**: The interest rate at which banks borrow funds from other banks in the London inter-bank market. It serves as a benchmark for short-term and floating-rate loans worldwide. ⭐
- **LIBID (London Inter-bank Bid Rate)**: The rate bit by banks on Eurocurrency deposits.
- **LIMEAN**: The average of LIBOR and LIBID, often used for interbank transactions.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. **Interest Rates in the Forward Market**: Spot rate + Premium (if foreign interest rate is lower) OR Spot rate - Discount (if foreign interest rate is higher).

## Section: Instruments of International Financial Markets 🔴

### Core Idea
Global markets offer a large variety of instruments to raise funds and manage risk, divided into short-term money market tools and long-term capital market tools. Furthermore, debt instruments allow borrowing without yielding ownership, while equity instruments sell ownership rights to overseas investors across multiple markets.

> **In Simple Terms:** Companies and governments raise money globally by either borrowing it (bonds/commercial paper) or selling a piece of themselves (equities). They also use special contracts (derivatives) to protect against sudden currency fluctuations.

### Key Concepts

#### Derivative Instruments
These tools derive their value from an underlying asset and are heavily used for hedging risks. Examples include:
- **Forward Contracts**: Agreements to lock in an exchange rate for a future transaction.
- **Options**: The right (but not the obligation) to buy (Call) or sell (Put) an asset at a predetermined price.
- **Futures**: Exchange-traded agreements to buy/sell at a set price on a future date.
- **Interest Rate Swaps**: Agreements between two parties to swap interest rate obligations, often exchanging fixed rates for floating rates in different currencies.

#### Debt and Equity Instruments
| Category | Instrument | Typical Features |
|----------|------------|------------------|
| **Debt (Short-term)** | Euro commercial Paper | Unsecured, < 270 days, issued at a discount. |
| **Debt (Long-term)** | International / Global Bonds | High ratings, large size, > 1 year maturity, simultaneous placement in multiple countries. |
| **Equity** | Depository Receipts (ADRs, GDRs) | Mechanism to trade foreign company shares on domestic stock exchanges (e.g., a foreign firm listed in New York uses ADRs). |

### Definitions
- **American Depository Receipts (ADRs)**: Instruments that allow US investors to trade shares of non-US companies in US markets.
- **Global Depository Receipts (GDRs)**: Instruments allowing equity capital to be raised across multiple international markets through one security.
- **Certificate of Deposit (CD)**: A savings certificate issued by commercial banks indicating a maturity date, interest rate, and amount.

## Section: Foreign Exchange Exposure & Risk 🔴

### Core Idea
Multinational companies face significant risks when dealing with multiple currencies because exchange rates fluctuate constantly. This exposure manifests in three main forms: transaction risk (short-term cash flow), translation risk (accounting valuation), and economic risk (long-term strategic environment).

> **In Simple Terms:** Foreign exchange risk is the danger that your money's value changes before you receive or pay it. If an Indian firm agrees to pay $1 million to a US supplier next year, a sudden surge in the dollar's value makes that payment much more expensive in Rupees. 

### Key Concepts

#### The Three Types of Risk
1. **Transaction Risk**: Arises from contractual foreign currency cash flows (e.g., committed imports/exports). The risk lies in the exchange rate fluctuating between the commitment date and the payment date.
2. **Translation Risk**: (Accounting exposure). Arises when a company must convert foreign-denominated assets and liabilities into the domestic currency for financial reporting.
3. **Economic Risk**: The long-run, strategic impact of macroeconomic factors (inflation, sovereign default, government policy) on future sales and costs. It is the most intricate risk to mitigate.

#### Hedging Strategies: Leading and Lagging
Firms can protect against transaction and translation risks by adjusting when they receive and pay cash:
- **Lead Strategy**: Collect receivables early if the foreign currency is expected to depreciate. Pay payables early if the currency is expected to appreciate.
- **Lag Strategy**: Delay collecting receivables if the currency is expected to appreciate. Delay payables if the currency is expected to depreciate.

### Definitions
- **Transaction Risk**: Exposure due to currency exchange rate fluctuations between the time a financial commitment is made and the time it is payable. ⭐ 
- **Translation Risk**: The accounting exposure from converting values of foreign assets and liabilities into the domestic currency. ⭐
- **Economic Risk**: The risk exposure of foreign investments due to long-run changes in macroeconomic conditions and their effect on future prices, sales, and costs. ⭐

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing that economic and transaction risk are the same. → ✅ Correct: Transaction risk only applies to specific, existing short-term contracts. Economic risk applies to the broad, long-term operational health of the company against global shifts.

## Section: Explaining Financial Crises 🔴

### Core Idea
A financial crisis occurs when the value of financial assets plunges, liquidity dries up, and entities default on their debts. These crises can be broadly divided into systemic crises, which threaten the entire financial ecosystem, and non-systemic crises, which are isolated to a specific sector. They are often rooted in macroeconomic imbalances, irrational market behavior, and regulatory deficiencies.

> **In Simple Terms:** A financial crisis is like a massive heart attack for the economy. Suddenly, banks don't have enough money, people and companies can't pay their debts, prices for things like houses or stocks crash, and panic spreads across the global system.

### Key Concepts

#### Systemic vs. Non-Systemic Crises
- **Systemic Crisis**: The risk of cascading failure that breaks down the entire financial system due to deep vertical and horizontal integration. Examples include the Great Depression (1930s) and the 2008 Global Financial Crisis.
- **Non-Systemic Crisis**: Isolated failures in one or a few markets or sectors, such as the Savings and Loan Crisis.

#### Causes of Crises
- **Fundamental Macroeconomic Imbalances**: Shocks in money supply, inflation, large fiscal/current account deficits.
- **Irrational Factors**: Bank runs (panic withdrawals), financial contagion/spillovers, limit to arbitrage, and the bursting of asset bubbles.
- **Structural/Regulatory Mismatches**: As noted by Schumpeter, bank credit drives innovation and growth, but a lack of regulation creates a dangerous mismatch between banks' short-term liabilities (deposits) and long-term investments (loans).

### Definitions
- **Financial Crisis**: A severe situation where financial asset prices plummet, widespread defaults occur, and liquidity vanishes. ⭐
- **Systemic Crisis**: The risk of a breakdown of an entire financial system caused by cascading failures. ⭐
- **Non-Systemic Crisis**: A crisis limited to one or a few isolated markets or sectors.

## Section: The 2007-2008 Global Financial Crisis 🔴

### Core Idea
The 2008 global financial crisis originated in the US housing market due to heavily encouraged subprime lending. These high-risk mortgages were repackaged into complex credit derivatives (like RMBS and CDOs) that hid the true risk. When housing prices fell and interest rates rose, borrowers defaulted en masse, causing these complex financial products to fail and sparking a global liquidity crisis through financial contagion.

> **In Simple Terms:** The US government pushed banks to give home loans to people with bad credit. Banks then bundled these risky loans together, disguised them as safe investments, and sold them globally. When people stopped paying their loans, the whole house of cards collapsed worldwide.

### Key Concepts

#### Origins: Subprime Lending & Deregulation
A political mandate to increase US homeownership forced lenders—encouraged by the Federal Reserve—to relax strict lending criteria. Commercial banks began aggressively issuing "subprime loans" to borrowers with poor credit histories.

#### The Role of Credit Derivatives
To get these risky loans off their balance sheets, commercial banks sold their mortgage portfolios to investment banks. Investment banks used **Credit Derivatives** to transfer this risk to third parties. 
- **Unfunded Credit Derivatives**: Contingent instruments (like Credit Default Swaps) where the protection seller only pays out if a default occurs, with no initial payment from the protection seller.
- **Funded Credit Derivatives**: Instruments (like CDOs or RMBS) where the counterparty pays upfront to cover potential default.

#### Residential Mortgage-Backed Securities (RMBS) Structure
1. **Origination**: Commercial bank approves risky home loans.
2. **Transfer**: Bank sells the portfolio to an Investment Bank to free up capital.
3. **Special Purpose Entity (Trustee)**: The investment bank creates a trustee to manage the portfolio.
4. **Tranching**: The trustee pools the loans and issues new securities backed by them (RMBS). These are split into tranches based on risk:
   - **Senior notes**: Lowest risk, lowest return. (Given high investment grades by rating agencies).
   - **Mezzanine / Junior notes**: Medium risk/return.
   - **Equity tranches**: Highest risk, highest return (often bought by the investment bank itself because no one else would).
5. **Insurance**: The trustee buys credit protection from a seller (often the same investment bank) to cover potential defaults.

#### The Bubble Burst & Contagion
When US interest rates rose between 2004-2006, borrowers could no longer afford their subprime mortgage payments. Housing prices then plummeted in 2007. The RMBS and CDO securities lost their value overnight, becoming highly illiquid. Banks backing them faced bankruptcy. Due to deep global financial integration, this US-centric crisis quickly infected Europe and the broader real economy worldwide.

### Definitions
- **Subprime Lending**: Making loans to people who may have difficulty maintaining the repayment schedule, reflecting lower creditworthiness. ⭐
- **Credit Derivatives (CDs)**: Off-balance-sheet financial contracts that allow a lender to transfer the credit risk of a financial instrument to a third party for a fee. ⭐
- **Residential Mortgage-Backed Securities (RMBS)**: A type of funded credit derivative formed by pooling various residential mortgages. ⭐
- **Special Purpose Entity (Trustee)**: An entity created to isolate financial risk, used in securitisation to manage pooled assets and issue securities.

### Mechanisms / Processes
1. Subprime loans issued → 2. Loans pooled and sold to Investment Banks → 3. Repackaged into structured tranches (RMBS/CDOs) → 4. Sold to global investors → 5. Interest rates rise / Housing prices fall → 6. Widespread mortgage defaults → 7. RMBS lose value → 8. Global banking liquidity crisis.

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes
- ❌ Mistake: Believing the 2008 crisis was solely caused by greedy homeowners. → ✅ Correct: It was a systemic failure caused by political mandates, relaxed lending standards, misleading credit ratings, and opaque financial reengineering by investment banks.

## Section: The 2007-2008 Global Financial Crisis (Continued) 🔴

### Core Idea
The 2008 Global Financial Crisis officially surfaced in late 2007 when institutional investors realized the true risk of subprime mortgages. A domino effect caused the real estate bubble to burst, leading to panic selling, a severe liquidity crunch in the banking system, and massive macroeconomic fallout including surging unemployment and stock market crashes. The crisis was combatted using an unprecedented mix of monetary liquidity injections and aggressive fiscal stimulus.

> **In Simple Terms:** Imagine everyone realizing at the exact same moment that the "safe" investments they bought are actually worthless. Panic ensues, banks stop lending to anyone, businesses can't get cash to operate, and workers get laid off in droves. 

### Key Concepts

#### Root Causes of the 2008 Crisis
Several compounding factors triggered the meltdown:
1. **US Banking System Malpractice**: Banks ignored minimum capital ratios to maximize returns, leading to aggressively high leverage ratios. They stopped verifying borrower creditworthiness.
2. **Global Imbalances**: Export-driven Asian economies (like China) ran massive trade surpluses, pegging their currencies and pouring trillions of dollars into US Treasury bonds. This flood of foreign capital artificially suppressed US interest rates, fueling cheap mortgages.
3. **Originate-to-Distribute (OTD) Model**: Securitisation allowed banks to originate risky loans and immediately sell them off, removing the incentive to care if the borrower would default.
4. **Flawed US Monetary Policy**: The Federal Reserve kept interest rates abnormally low for an extended period, which incentivized banks to take on riskier borrowers to maintain profit margins.
5. **Political Mandates**: Government initiatives aimed at tackling inequality aggressively pushed banks to expand homeownership by relaxing underwriting standards.

#### Sequence of the Collapse
1. Early 2007: Major mortgage backers refuse to acquire more high-risk loans; specialized lenders declare bankruptcy.
2. Mid 2007: Credit Rating Agencies (CRAs) downgrade asset-backed instruments in massive numbers.
3. August 2007: Shadow banking system faces a severe liquidity freeze; short-term funding is withdrawn. 
4. September 2008: The panic culminates. Lehman Brothers declares bankruptcy, sparking a full-blown global panic.

#### Macroeconomic Effects
The crisis shocked both credit supply and demand:
- **Interest Rates Skyrocketed**: While government bond rates stayed low, corporate borrowing rates spiked, freezing corporate investment.
- **Credit Supply Froze**: Syndicated loans plummeted. Banks hoarded cash.
- **Real Economy Crash**: Consumer wealth eroded as housing prices fell 30% and stocks dropped ~50% by mid-2009. Nine million US jobs were lost by 2009.

#### Initial Policy Responses
- **Monetary Policy**: Central banks injected massive liquidity, guaranteed deposits, and slashed interest rates until they hit a "liquidity trap" (near 0% where monetary policy loses further effectiveness).
- **Fiscal Policy**: The government rolled out the **Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)** to acquire bank shares and recapitalize banks. They also passed the **American Recovery and Reinvestment Act** ($780 billion in tax cuts and spending) which ballooned the US deficit to 9% by 2010.

### Definitions
- **Syndicated Loans**: Large loans provided by a group of lenders (syndicate) to a single corporate borrower. ⭐
- **Liquidity Trap**: An economic situation where interest rates are brought down to near zero, rendering further conventional monetary policy ineffective at stimulating the economy. ⭐
- **Originate-to-Distribute (OTD) Model**: A banking model where loans are originated not to be held until maturity, but to be immediately securitised and distributed to investors.
- **TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program)**: A US government initiative to purchase toxic assets and equity from financial institutions to strengthen the financial sector.

### Mechanisms / Processes
> **The Domino Effect of 2008:**
> Easy monetary policy & Political pressure → Subprime lending boom (OTD Model) → Interest rates rise → Widespread borrower defaults → Asset-Backed Securities downgraded → Banks hoarding cash (Liquidity Crunch) → Corporate borrowing costs spike → Investment and Consumption collapse → Recession & High Unemployment.

## Section: Currency Crises 🟡

### Core Idea
A currency crisis happens when investors lose faith in a nation's economy and rapidly move their money out, causing the country's currency value to plummet. It often forces central banks to exhaust their foreign reserves trying to defend the currency's value. 

> **In Simple Terms:** If investors suddenly believe a country is in financial trouble, they rush to sell their investments and convert their local money into a safer foreign currency, causing the local currency's value to crash.

### Key Concepts

#### Mechanics of a Currency Crisis
- **Capital Flight**: A rapid withdrawal of money from a country by investors. As they sell the domestic currency to buy foreign currency, the exchange rate worsens.
- **Central Bank Intervention**: Central banks attempt to stabilize the currency by selling off foreign reserves (like USD or Gold) to buy back their own currency to maintain a fixed peg or manage a float.
- **Indicators**: Current account deficits, rapid inflation, or investor uneasiness over government policies precede these crises. 
- *Historical Example*: The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis began as a currency crisis when Thailand delinked the Baht from the US Dollar due to depleted foreign reserves, sparking regional panic.

### Definitions
- **Currency Crisis**: Occurs when the value of a country's currency sharply declines due to volatile markets and lack of faith in the economy. ⭐
- **Capital Flight**: The rapid withdrawal of investments and conversion of domestic currency into foreign currency by investors fearing instability.

## Section: Historical Financial Crises 🟢

### Core Idea
Financial crises have recurred throughout history, often triggered by overconfidence, abrupt policy shifts, or speculative bubbles. 

### Key Concepts
- **Credit Crisis of 1772 (London)**: Rapid loan expansion fueled by colonial wealth resulting in overconfidence.
- **Great Depression 1929-39 (US)**: Wall Street crash exacerbated by poor policy decisions.
- **OPEC Oil Price Shock 1973**: Retaliation halting oil exports to the US/allies causing severe inflation and shortages.
- **Asian Crisis 1997**: Speculative capital flows and excessive debt, sparked by Thailand abandoning its USD peg.
- **Global Financial Crisis 2007-08**: Bursting of the US housing bubble dragging down key financial institutions.

## Section: Lessons Learned & Regulatory Needs 🟢

### Core Idea
The 2008 Global Financial Crisis was a multidimensional failure of politics, monetary policy, and fragmented regulation. To prevent future crises, central banks must transparently and actively regulate commercial banks—enforcing minimum capital ratios and strict leverage limits.

> **In Simple Terms:** Preventative medicine is better than a cure. If governments properly monitor banks and stop them from gambling with borrowed money, severe economic meltdowns can be avoided. 

### Key Concepts

#### Timely Policy Action
While monetary and fiscal policies were deployed during the 2008 crisis to pull the economy out of recession, policymakers acted too late. Had they intervened earlier, the depth and severity of the recession could have been minimized.

#### The 2022 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences
The crisis underscored the critical role of banks in the macroeconomy. The 2022 Nobel Prize recognized the foundational research on banks and financial crises by three economists:
1. **Douglas W. Diamond & Philip H. Dybvig (1983)**: Developed theoretical models explaining that banks perform a crucial role via "maturity transformation" (using short-term demand deposits to fund long-term projects). This exact mechanism makes them inherently vulnerable to bank runs without deposit insurance.
2. **Ben S. Bernanke (1983)**: Demonstrated that when banks fail, valuable banking relationships are destroyed. The resulting contraction in credit supply leaves deep, long-lasting scars on the real economy.

### Definitions
- **Maturity Transformation**: The process whereby banks take short-term sources of finance, such as deposits, and turn them into long-term borrowings, such as mortgages. ⭐
- **Collateralised Debt Obligation (CDO)**: A synthetic derivative product that groups different types of loans to offer a single investment product to institutional investors. ⭐
- **Securitisation**: The process in which certain types of assets are pooled together and repackaged into interest-bearing securities. ⭐
